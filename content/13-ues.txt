title: Upper East Side & Museum Mile
area: Manhattan
blurb: Gilded Age mansions turned museums, the Met and the Guggenheim, Carnegie's house, German Yorkville, and Gracie Mansion.
=== OVERVIEW
The Upper East Side has been, for more than a century, the most famous wealthy neighborhood in America. Its name is shorthand for old money, private schools, charity galas, and Park Avenue co-ops with famously selective boards.

It wasn't always that way. In the mid-nineteenth century, much of the area was rocky, rural land, dotted with farms, country estates along the East River, and shantytowns. The New York and Harlem Railroad ran up Fourth Avenue, now Park Avenue, in an open cut, belching smoke and noise. Elevated trains rumbled along Second and Third Avenues starting in the late 1870s, and the blocks near them filled with tenements for working-class immigrants: Germans, Irish, Hungarians, Czechs, and others.

The transformation came in two stages. First, after Central Park opened, the very rich began building mansions along Fifth Avenue facing the park, moving north from the Gilded Age mansions in the Fifties. By the early 1900s, Fifth Avenue from 59th Street to 96th Street was lined with palaces built by the families of the industrial age: steel, oil, railroads, banking, and tobacco. Second, after Grand Central's tracks were covered in the early twentieth century, Park Avenue became a grand boulevard, and luxury apartment buildings rose along it. The rich discovered that a large apartment with a doorman was much easier to run than a mansion with dozens of servants.

As the mansions became impractical, many of them were demolished, but many were converted into museums, consulates, schools, and cultural institutions. That's why Fifth Avenue along the park, from the Met at 82nd Street north to about 105th Street, has so many museums that it's called Museum Mile. Many of these museums are the former homes and private collections of the people who made the great American fortunes.

East of Lexington Avenue, the story is different. The area known as Yorkville was a working-class neighborhood of German, Hungarian, and Czech immigrants, with breweries, beer halls, and churches. Elevated trains kept it modest for decades. After the elevated trains were torn down in the 1940s and 50s, high-rise apartments replaced the tenements, and the neighborhood became more uniformly affluent. And at the far east, along the river, is Gracie Mansion, the official home of New York's mayor.

On this walk, you'll see how private wealth became public culture, and how a working-class immigrant neighborhood became part of the city's most famous zip codes.
=== STOP The Park Avenue Armory @ 40.7677,-73.9659
You're at Park Avenue and 66th Street, in front of a massive brick building that looks like a medieval fortress, with towers and crenellations. This is the Park Avenue Armory, completed in 1880 as the headquarters of the Seventh Regiment of the New York State Militia.

The Seventh Regiment was nicknamed the Silk Stocking Regiment, because so many of its members came from New York's wealthiest families. It was a social club as well as a military unit. It also had a practical role: in the nineteenth century, the city's militia was used to put down riots and labor disturbances, including the deadly Draft Riots of 1863. Armories built in this era often had fortress-like designs, reflecting the fears of the wealthy that the city's working classes might rise up.

The building was paid for not by the government but by the members themselves, and they spared no expense. The interior rooms were designed by some of the most celebrated designers of the era, including Louis Comfort Tiffany, the son of the jeweler, and Stanford White. The Veterans Room, designed by Tiffany's firm, is a masterpiece of the Aesthetic Movement, with dark wood, metalwork, and stained glass.

Behind the front building is the drill hall, a vast iron-trussed space of about fifty-five thousand square feet, one of the largest unobstructed interior spaces in New York. It was modeled on the great train sheds of the era. The regiment drilled here, and it was also used for sporting events and exhibitions.

In the 2000s, a nonprofit took over the building and turned it into a cultural center. The drill hall now hosts large-scale art installations, theater, music, and performances that couldn't fit anywhere else, along with the famous Winter Show, an antiques fair.

Look around the neighborhood, too. Just north is Hunter College, a public college founded in 1870 as a teacher-training school for women. Along Park Avenue, look at the elegant apartment buildings, mostly built in the 1910s and 20s, many designed by the architect Rosario Candela, whose buildings are considered among the most desirable in the city.
=== STOP The Frick Collection @ 40.7712,-73.9673
At Fifth Avenue and 70th Street, facing Central Park, stands a low, elegant mansion of limestone set back behind a garden. This is the Frick Collection.

It was built between 1912 and 1914 as the home of Henry Clay Frick, designed by Thomas Hastings of Carrère and Hastings, the architects of the New York Public Library. Frick was one of the most ruthless industrialists of the Gilded Age. He made his fortune producing coke, a fuel used in steelmaking, in western Pennsylvania, and became the chairman of Carnegie Steel, Andrew Carnegie's company. In 1892, during a strike at the Homestead steel works near Pittsburgh, Frick brought in hundreds of armed Pinkerton agents. A battle broke out between the strikers and the Pinkertons, and people were killed on both sides. The state militia then occupied the town, and the strike was broken. Weeks later, an anarchist named Alexander Berkman burst into Frick's office, shot him twice and stabbed him. Frick survived, and reportedly returned to work within about a week.

Frick and Carnegie eventually had a bitter falling out. Frick moved to New York, and built his mansion on Fifth Avenue, about twenty blocks south of Carnegie's. Some say he wanted to make Carnegie's house look like a miner's shack.

Frick spent his later years collecting European art, and he intended from the beginning that his house and collection would become a museum after his death. He died in 1919, and the museum opened in 1935. The Frick has an extraordinary collection, including works by Vermeer, Rembrandt, Holbein, Goya, Titian, and Turner, displayed as they would have been in a private home, among furniture and decorative objects. Many visitors find it the most intimate and beautiful museum in the city.

The Frick underwent a major renovation and expansion in the early 2020s, opening up the second floor of the mansion, where the family's private rooms were, for the first time. During the renovation, the collection was displayed temporarily in the Breuer building on Madison Avenue, the former home of the Whitney Museum.
=== STOP The Metropolitan Museum of Art @ 40.7794,-73.9632
You're on Fifth Avenue at 82nd Street, at the foot of the famous steps of the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

The Met was founded in 1870 by a group of businessmen, financiers, artists, and civic leaders, including Theodore Roosevelt Senior, who wanted New York to have an art museum to rival the great museums of Europe. Its first home was in a building on Fifth Avenue downtown. In 1880, it moved into a red brick Gothic building inside Central Park, designed by Calvert Vaux and Jacob Wrey Mould. That original building is now buried within the museum; part of its brick facade is visible inside, in the Lehman Wing.

The grand Beaux-Arts facade you see on Fifth Avenue was designed by Richard Morris Hunt and completed in 1902, after his death, by his son. Look up at the tops of the paired columns. Above them, there are large rough blocks of stone. They were intended to be carved into sculptures representing great periods of art, but the money ran out, and they've been left uncarved for more than a century.

Today, the Met is the largest art museum in the Americas, with a collection of around one and a half million objects spanning five thousand years. Its galleries include Egyptian art, including the Temple of Dendur; European paintings, including works by Rembrandt, Vermeer, and Van Gogh; an American Wing, with entire period rooms; arms and armor; and much more. The museum's annual fundraising gala, the Met Gala, held each May for the Costume Institute, has become one of the biggest celebrity events in the world.

The steps themselves are one of the great public spaces in the city. People sit here to eat lunch, meet friends, watch street performers, and people-watch. In the beloved 1967 children's novel From the Mixed-Up Files of Mrs. Basil E. Frankweiler, two children run away from home and secretly live inside the Met, sleeping in an antique bed and bathing in a fountain.

If you go to the roof garden in the warmer months, you'll get a spectacular view over Central Park and the skyline.
=== STOP The Guggenheim & the Neue Galerie @ 40.7830,-73.9590
Walk north along Fifth Avenue. At 86th Street, look for an elegant Beaux-Arts mansion on the corner, built in 1914 for the industrialist William Starr Miller and later home to Grace Vanderbilt. Today it's the Neue Galerie, a museum of early twentieth-century German and Austrian art and design, founded by the cosmetics heir Ronald Lauder and the art dealer Serge Sabarsky.

Its star is Gustav Klimt's 1907 portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer, the dazzling gold painting often called The Woman in Gold. The painting was seized by the Nazis from the Bloch-Bauer family in Vienna, and after the war it hung in an Austrian state museum for decades. Adele's niece, Maria Altmann, who had fled to the United States, fought a long legal battle and won its return in 2006. Lauder then bought it for a reported one hundred thirty-five million dollars, at the time a record price for a painting. Downstairs, Café Sabarsky serves Viennese coffee and pastries in an elegant, wood-paneled room.

Continue north to 89th Street, where you'll see one of the most famous buildings of the twentieth century: the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, designed by Frank Lloyd Wright.

Solomon Guggenheim came from a family that made an enormous fortune in mining and smelting, especially copper and silver. Advised by the artist Baroness Hilla von Rebay, he collected what they called non-objective art, abstract painting by artists like Wassily Kandinsky. In 1943, they commissioned Wright to design a museum for the collection. The project took sixteen years, through endless disputes with city officials, the museum's board, and Wright's own stubbornness. Wright died in April 1959, about six months before the museum opened that October.

The design is a radical departure from traditional museums. Instead of a series of rooms, it's a single continuous spiral ramp, coiling up around a central atrium, under a glass skylight. Visitors typically take an elevator to the top and walk down the ramp. Wright, who mostly despised cities, designed the building as a kind of organic shell, set apart from the grid of Manhattan. Some artists objected that the curved, sloping walls were terrible for displaying paintings. The building's admirers say the building is itself the greatest work of art in the collection.

Look at the building from across the street. Its white, inverted, stacked spirals widen as they rise. When it opened, critics compared it to a washing machine, an inverted cupcake, and a snail.
=== STOP Carnegie Hill: Andrew Carnegie's Mansion @ 40.7843,-73.9578
At Fifth Avenue and 91st Street, look for a large red brick and limestone mansion set within a garden. This was the home of Andrew Carnegie, built between 1899 and 1902.

When Carnegie built here, this area was considered remote, and the land around his mansion was still partly empty and rocky, with shanties nearby. He chose it for the space and sunlight. He wanted a big garden, and he deliberately bought up surrounding lots to protect his light and air. The neighborhood around it came to be known as Carnegie Hill.

Carnegie, as you may have heard at Carnegie Hall, was born in Scotland in 1835, emigrated to Pittsburgh as a poor boy, and worked as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill and a telegraph messenger before rising in the railroad industry and then founding the steel company that made him one of the richest people in history. In 1901, he sold Carnegie Steel to J. P. Morgan, who combined it with other companies to form U.S. Steel, the first company in the world valued at over one billion dollars. Carnegie then devoted himself to giving his fortune away. In an essay called The Gospel of Wealth, he argued that the rich had a moral obligation to use their wealth for the public good, and that the man who dies rich dies disgraced.

Carnegie described the house as the most modest, plainest, and most roomy house in New York, though it had more than sixty rooms. It was one of the first private homes in the United States to have a structural steel frame, appropriately enough for a steel magnate, and it included an Otis passenger elevator and an early form of air conditioning and central heating.

Today, the mansion is home to the Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum, the only museum in the United States devoted entirely to historic and contemporary design. The museum was founded in 1897 by Sarah, Eleanor, and Amy Hewitt, the granddaughters of Peter Cooper, the founder of Cooper Union. Its collection includes everything from wallpaper and textiles to furniture, product design, and graphic design.

A block north, at 92nd Street, is the Jewish Museum, housed in the French Gothic mansion of the banker Felix Warburg, completed in 1908. The Warburgs were among the leading families of the German Jewish financial elite in New York, along with the Schiffs, the Loebs, and the Lehmans, a group sometimes called Our Crowd.
=== STOP Yorkville @ 40.7775,-73.9510
Walk east to East 86th Street and Second Avenue. You're in Yorkville, the eastern part of the Upper East Side.

Starting in the late nineteenth century, Yorkville became a working-class immigrant neighborhood. After the General Slocum disaster in 1904, which you may have heard about on the East Village tour, many German families left the Lower East Side and resettled here. 86th Street became known as the German Broadway, lined with beer halls, cafés, bakeries, butchers, dance halls, and German-language theaters and movie houses. Hungarians, Czechs, Slovaks, and Irish also lived in the neighborhood, with their own churches and social clubs.

Yorkville also had a dark chapter. In the 1930s, the German American Bund, a pro-Nazi organization, was active here, holding rallies and parades along 86th Street. Its activities provoked counter-protests and violent confrontations. Its leader, Fritz Kuhn, was convicted of embezzling the group's funds in 1939, and the Bund collapsed after the United States entered World War Two.

A few traces of German Yorkville survive. Look for Schaller and Weber, a German butcher and grocer on Second Avenue, open since 1937, and its sausage shop next door. Glaser's Bake Shop, on First Avenue, operated from 1902 until 2018. There are still a few German restaurants nearby.

A few blocks north, between Second and Third Avenues and 90th to 93rd Streets, stood the Jacob Ruppert Brewery, one of the largest breweries in the country, makers of Knickerbocker beer. Jacob Ruppert Junior, the brewery's heir, used his fortune to buy the New York Yankees in 1915. He bought Babe Ruth from the Boston Red Sox in 1919 and built the original Yankee Stadium in 1923, turning the Yankees into the most successful franchise in American sports. The brewery closed in 1965 and was demolished. Apartment towers, named Ruppert Towers, now stand on the site.

Underneath Second Avenue runs the Second Avenue Subway. It was first proposed in the 1920s, and construction started and stopped several times over the decades. The first phase, from 63rd Street to 96th Street, finally opened on January first, 2017. The station at 86th Street has large, bright mosaic artworks by the artist Chuck Close. It's worth a peek.
=== STOP Gracie Mansion & Carl Schurz Park @ 40.7760,-73.9431
End your walk at Carl Schurz Park, along the East River at the end of East 86th Street, and at Gracie Mansion, the official residence of the mayor of New York City.

Gracie Mansion was built in 1799 as a country house for Archibald Gracie, a Scottish-born merchant and shipping magnate. In his time, this was the countryside, far north of the city, and wealthy merchants built summer estates along the East River. Gracie entertained prominent guests here, including Alexander Hamilton, John Quincy Adams, and the future King Louis Philippe of France. Gracie lost his fortune in the economic turmoil around the War of 1812 and sold the house in 1823.

After passing through several owners, the city took over the property in 1896 and made it part of the park. For decades, it housed the Museum of the City of New York, and a concession stand. In 1942, at the urging of Robert Moses, it became the official residence of the mayor. Fiorello La Guardia was the first mayor to live here. Most mayors since have lived in the house, though Michael Bloomberg, who was a billionaire with his own townhouse, chose not to.

The park is named after Carl Schurz, a German revolutionary who fled to America after the failed revolutions of 1848 in Germany. He became a general in the Civil War, a United States senator from Missouri, Secretary of the Interior, and a journalist and reformer. He's credited with a famous version of the phrase My country, right or wrong: if right, to be kept right; and if wrong, to be set right. The park honored him because of the large German population in Yorkville. He lived nearby, at the end of his life.

Walk to the promenade along the river. Look out at the water. This stretch of the East River, where the river meets the Harlem River and Long Island Sound, is called Hell Gate, from the Dutch Hellegat. Its swirling currents and submerged rocks wrecked hundreds of ships. In the 1870s and 1880s, the Army Corps of Engineers undertook a huge project to clear the rocks. In 1885, they detonated about three hundred thousand pounds of explosives to blow up Flood Rock. It was one of the largest planned explosions in history before the atomic bomb, and it reportedly was felt as far away as Princeton, New Jersey.

Across the water, you'll see Roosevelt Island, Randall's Island, and Queens. From the promenade, you can watch boats and barges pass by, and the lights of the Triborough Bridge, now the Robert F. Kennedy Bridge, in the distance.
