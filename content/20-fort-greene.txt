=== TOUR
id: fort-greene
title: Navy Yard, Fort Greene & Downtown Brooklyn
area: Brooklyn
blurb: Where the Arizona and Missouri were built, the Prison Ship Martyrs, brownstone Fort Greene, BAM, the old bank tower, and why the Dodgers left.
=== OVERVIEW
This walk covers the neighborhoods just east of Downtown Brooklyn: the Brooklyn Navy Yard on the waterfront, the brownstone neighborhood of Fort Greene, and the commercial heart of the borough around Fulton Street and Atlantic Avenue.

For more than a century and a half, the Brooklyn Navy Yard was one of the most important shipyards in the United States. Ships built here fought in every American war from the War of 1812 to Vietnam. At its peak in World War Two, the yard employed around seventy thousand people working around the clock, making it one of the largest industrial employers in the country. The yard's workers, and the businesses that served them, filled the surrounding neighborhoods.

Fort Greene takes its name from a Revolutionary War fort, built in 1776 under the direction of General Nathanael Greene, as part of the defenses of Brooklyn. In the nineteenth century, the area developed into a neighborhood of handsome brownstones and townhouses for the merchants and professionals of the independent City of Brooklyn. In the twentieth century, it became one of the centers of Black middle-class life in Brooklyn, and in the 1980s and 90s, it was home to a creative renaissance of Black artists, filmmakers, musicians, and writers, including Spike Lee, whose film company is based here.

Downtown Brooklyn, around Fulton Street, Flatbush Avenue, and Atlantic Avenue, was the shopping and business center of Brooklyn, with department stores, banks, and theaters. Like downtowns across America, it declined in the mid-twentieth century. In recent decades, it has been transformed by a rezoning, new towers, and the Barclays Center arena.

There's also a story here about the most painful loss in Brooklyn's history: not a battle or a fire, but a baseball team. As you walk, you'll find out why the Brooklyn Dodgers left for Los Angeles, and what that has to do with the arena at the corner of Atlantic and Flatbush.
=== STOP The Brooklyn Navy Yard @ 40.6996,-73.9742
You're at the Brooklyn Navy Yard, at Flushing Avenue and Carlton Avenue, outside Building 92, a brick building that serves as the yard's visitor center and museum.

The site sits on Wallabout Bay, a curve of the East River. During the Revolutionary War, the British anchored prison ships here, decaying old warships where they held American prisoners of war. Conditions were horrific: prisoners were crammed below decks, starved, and ravaged by disease. More than eleven thousand prisoners are believed to have died on these ships, more than died in all the battles of the Revolution combined. Their bodies were often buried in shallow graves along the shore, and for years afterward, bones washed up on the beach. You'll see their memorial in Fort Greene Park.

The federal government bought the site in 1801 and established the New York Navy Yard, commonly known as the Brooklyn Navy Yard. Over the following century and a half, it built and repaired many of the Navy's most important ships. The USS Maine, whose explosion in Havana harbor in 1898 helped spark the Spanish-American War, was built here. The battleship USS Arizona, sunk at Pearl Harbor on December seventh, 1941, was built here and launched in 1915. And the battleship USS Missouri, on whose deck Japan signed its surrender ending World War Two, in September 1945, was built here and launched in 1944.

During World War Two, the yard was a city of its own, with dry docks, foundries, machine shops, and cranes, and around seventy thousand workers, including thousands of women and African Americans who found industrial jobs here for the first time.

The Navy closed the yard in 1966, a devastating blow to Brooklyn. The city bought it and turned it into an industrial park. Today, it's home to hundreds of businesses employing thousands of people: manufacturers, artists, designers, and film studios, including Steiner Studios, one of the largest film and television production facilities on the East Coast. There's a rooftop farm, a distillery, and companies making everything from furniture to food. If you have time, Building 92's exhibits tell the yard's story, and tours of the yard are offered.
=== STOP Fort Greene Park & the Prison Ship Martyrs Monument @ 40.6913,-73.9757
Walk south to Fort Greene Park, and climb to the top of the hill, where a tall Doric column rises from a broad granite stairway.

This hill was the site of Fort Putnam, built in 1776 under General Nathanael Greene, to defend Brooklyn against the British. It was rebuilt during the War of 1812 and renamed Fort Greene in his honor.

The park was among the first public parks in Brooklyn. The poet Walt Whitman, as editor of the Brooklyn Daily Eagle in the 1840s, campaigned for its creation, arguing that the working people of the crowded city needed open space. It was laid out in 1847, and then redesigned in 1867 by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, the designers of Central Park and Prospect Park.

The monument at the top is the Prison Ship Martyrs Monument, dedicated in 1908 to the more than eleven thousand American prisoners who died on British prison ships in Wallabout Bay during the Revolution, which you heard about at the Navy Yard. For decades, their remains, collected from the shore, were kept in various vaults. In 1873, they were placed in a crypt here, beneath the stairway. Then, the architects McKim, Mead and White designed this monument: a column about one hundred forty-nine feet tall, at the time among the tallest Doric columns in the world, topped by a bronze urn. President-elect William Howard Taft dedicated it in November 1908. The crypt is beneath the stairway, though it's not open to the public.

The prisoners on the ships came from all thirteen colonies and from many countries, including sailors captured on American privateers, and some were of African descent. Each year, a ceremony is held here to remember them.

From the top of the hill, look around at the view, and at the neighborhood of brownstones surrounding the park. On a clear day you can see the Manhattan skyline to the northwest.
=== STOP Fort Greene Brownstones @ 40.6880,-73.9740
Walk to South Portland Avenue and South Oxford Street, between DeKalb and Lafayette Avenues, two of the most beautiful residential blocks in Brooklyn.

These rows of brownstones and Italianate townhouses were built mostly in the 1850s to 1870s, for prosperous merchants and professionals. Brownstone is a type of sandstone, quarried mostly in Connecticut and New Jersey, that was hugely popular in nineteenth-century New York. It was relatively cheap, easy to carve, and gave a sense of solid respectability. Most brownstone houses are actually brick buildings with a brownstone veneer on the front.

Fort Greene went through many changes. In the early twentieth century, as wealthier residents moved to newer neighborhoods, many houses were divided into rooming houses. After World War Two, Black families, many working at the Navy Yard, moved into the neighborhood. Fort Greene developed a strong Black middle-class community. The novelist Richard Wright wrote much of his landmark 1940 novel, Native Son, while living on Carlton Avenue, nearby.

In the 1980s and 90s, Fort Greene became the center of what was sometimes called a Black Bohemia or a Black Renaissance in Brooklyn. The filmmaker Spike Lee, who grew up in Fort Greene, set up his production company, 40 Acres and a Mule Filmworks, in a converted firehouse on DeKalb Avenue. His films, like She's Gotta Have It and Do the Right Thing, were rooted in Brooklyn. Writers, jazz musicians like Branford Marsalis, artists, and actors lived in the neighborhood.

Since the 2000s, Fort Greene has gentrified, and brownstones now sell for millions of dollars. The Black population has declined significantly, a change that's been the subject of much discussion, including Spike Lee's own outspoken criticism.

Look for the Lafayette Avenue Presbyterian Church, at Lafayette Avenue and South Oxford Street. It was founded in 1857 by abolitionists and was reportedly a stop on the Underground Railroad. Inside, there's a collection of Tiffany stained glass windows.
=== STOP BAM: The Brooklyn Academy of Music @ 40.6863,-73.9776
Walk west on Lafayette Avenue to the Brooklyn Academy of Music, known as BAM, in the large building with a terracotta and brick facade on the corner of Ashland Place.

BAM was founded in 1861, making it one of the oldest performing arts centers in the United States. Its first building was in Brooklyn Heights, on Montague Street, but it burned down in 1903. The current building, the Peter Jay Sharp Building, opened in 1908. In its early years, BAM hosted the biggest names in the performing arts: the opera singer Enrico Caruso, the actress Sarah Bernhardt, the dancer Isadora Duncan, and the Metropolitan Opera, which performed here regularly.

Like much of Brooklyn, BAM declined in the mid-twentieth century. Its turnaround came in 1967, when Harvey Lichtenstein became its director. Lichtenstein transformed BAM into one of the world's leading centers for avant-garde and experimental performance. He brought companies and artists that were too daring for Manhattan's establishment venues. In 1983, he launched the Next Wave Festival, which showcased artists like the composer Philip Glass, the director Robert Wilson, the choreographers Merce Cunningham and Pina Bausch, and the performance artist Laurie Anderson. BAM became a destination, drawing audiences from Manhattan to Brooklyn at a time when few people crossed the river for culture.

BAM's growth has been an anchor for the Brooklyn Cultural District, which now includes theaters, galleries, and other institutions, and BAM itself has expanded with additional theaters and cinemas in the neighborhood.

Look around at the area. Mark Morris, the choreographer, built his dance center across the street. And the large modern buildings nearby are part of the district's growth, many with arts spaces on their lower floors.
=== STOP The Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower @ 40.6855,-73.9766
Look up at the tall tower with a gold-domed top and four large clock faces, at One Hanson Place, at the corner of Ashland Place and Flatbush Avenue. This is the Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower, completed in 1929.

For eighty years, it was the tallest building in Brooklyn, at about five hundred twelve feet. It was built for the Williamsburgh Savings Bank, one of the largest savings banks in Brooklyn, a type of bank that held the deposits of working people and made mortgage loans. The tower was designed to be visible from all over Brooklyn, a symbol of the bank's strength and stability, completed just before the stock market crash.

Its four clock faces, each about twenty-seven feet across, were among the largest in the world when it was built. They became a landmark for generations of Brooklynites.

Step inside, if possible, to see the former banking hall, now an event space. It's one of the most spectacular interiors in Brooklyn, with soaring arches, mosaics, and marble. Its decoration is full of symbols of thrift, industry, and security, the virtues a savings bank wanted its depositors to associate with it.

For many decades, the upper floors of the tower were filled with dentists' offices. It's a piece of Brooklyn trivia: if you went to the dentist in Brooklyn in the mid-twentieth century, there's a good chance it was here. In the 2000s, the tower was converted to luxury condominiums. The banking hall became an event venue.

Next to the tower is the Atlantic Terminal, a hub for the Long Island Rail Road and nine subway lines. It's one of the busiest transit hubs in New York.
=== STOP Barclays Center & the Dodgers' Lost Stadium @ 40.6826,-73.9754
Walk south across Flatbush Avenue to the corner of Atlantic and Flatbush Avenues, to the Barclays Center, the arena with the rusted steel skin and a giant oculus over its entrance plaza.

This intersection holds one of the great what-ifs of New York history. In the 1950s, the Brooklyn Dodgers were one of the most beloved teams in baseball. They played at Ebbets Field, in the Flatbush neighborhood, but it was small, aging, and lacked parking. The team's owner, Walter O'Malley, wanted to build a new, modern stadium, possibly a domed one, right here, at Atlantic and Flatbush, above the Long Island Rail Road terminal, where subway lines and commuter trains converged.

O'Malley needed the city's help to acquire the land. But Robert Moses, who controlled much of the city's development, refused to support a stadium here. He wanted the team to move to a site in Flushing Meadows, in Queens. O'Malley refused to move the team to Queens. Meanwhile, Los Angeles offered O'Malley a large tract of land for a new stadium. After the 1957 season, the Dodgers left Brooklyn for Los Angeles. Brooklynites felt betrayed. For many, O'Malley was the villain, though historians have argued that Moses deserves a large share of the blame. Ebbets Field was demolished in 1960 and replaced with apartment buildings. A few years later, the Mets began playing in Queens, in Shea Stadium, on the very site Moses had offered the Dodgers.

More than half a century later, major league sports finally came back to this corner. In 2012, the Barclays Center opened here, as part of a large and controversial development called Atlantic Yards, now called Pacific Park. It is home to the Brooklyn Nets basketball team, who moved from New Jersey, the first major professional sports team in Brooklyn since the Dodgers. The development involved the use of eminent domain to acquire property, and it led to years of protests and legal battles by neighborhood residents.

The arena's design, with its weathered steel exterior, was meant to evoke the borough's industrial heritage.
=== STOP Fulton Mall & Junior's @ 40.6900,-73.9822
End your walk on Fulton Street, between Flatbush Avenue and Adams Street, in the pedestrian-friendly shopping area known as the Fulton Mall.

For most of the twentieth century, Fulton Street was Brooklyn's downtown, its equivalent of Herald Square, lined with department stores, movie palaces, and banks. The biggest department store was Abraham and Straus, known as A and S. It was founded in 1865 by Abraham Abraham, a Jewish merchant. In the 1890s, he brought in partners from the Straus family, the same family that ran Macy's. A and S became one of the largest department stores in the country, and its building at Fulton and Hoyt Streets became a landmark. In 1995, A and S was merged into Macy's, and the building became a Macy's store.

Fulton Street declined in the 1970s and 80s, as shoppers moved to the suburbs. In 1977, it was converted into a pedestrian and bus mall, and it became a busy shopping district for Black and Caribbean Brooklynites, known for its sneaker stores, hip-hop fashion, and music. It was celebrated in early hip-hop culture. Since the 2000s, it has gentrified, with national chains, new office buildings, and high-rise apartments.

Look for the Dime Savings Bank of Brooklyn building, on DeKalb Avenue, near Fulton Street, with its massive dome and columns, modeled on the Pantheon in Rome, completed in 1908. Look for the dimes incorporated into its decoration. Its banking hall has been preserved as part of a new residential tower, which became one of the tallest buildings in Brooklyn.

Finally, at the corner of Flatbush and DeKalb Avenues, look for Junior's, a restaurant that has been serving its famous cheesecake since 1950. It was founded by Harry Rosen, who named it after his two sons. Its cheesecake, rich and creamy, made with cream cheese, became famous across the country. Junior's survived a fire in 1981, rebuilt, and remains a Brooklyn icon.

A slice of cheesecake at Junior's is a fitting way to end a walk through Brooklyn's downtown.
