area: Manhattan
blurb: Tenements, pushcarts, Yiddish newspapers, delis, and the Italian streets of Mulberry and the Bowery.
=== OVERVIEW
If you want to understand how America absorbed the great waves of immigration of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, there is no better place to walk than the Lower East Side.

For about a century, from the 1840s to the 1920s, these streets were among the most densely populated places on Earth. At its peak, around 1900, parts of the Lower East Side packed in several hundred thousand people per square mile, a density that rivaled or exceeded the most crowded parts of Bombay or London at the time. They lived in tenements, five and six story walk-up buildings built to squeeze as many families as possible onto a standard twenty five by one hundred foot city lot.

The first great wave was German. By the 1850s, the area east of the Bowery and north of Division Street was known as Kleindeutschland, Little Germany. It was one of the largest German speaking cities in the world, with its own beer halls, newspapers, theaters, and gymnastic clubs. Over time, prosperous Germans moved uptown, to Yorkville and elsewhere, a migration accelerated by a terrible tragedy in 1904 that we'll talk about on the East Village tour.

Then came the Eastern European Jews. Between about 1880 and 1924, around two and a half million Jews came to America, fleeing poverty and pogroms in the Russian Empire, Austria-Hungary, and Romania. A large share of them came first to these streets. They brought Yiddish, a garment industry built on small workshops, a passionate radical politics, and a culture of newspapers, theater, and debate. At the same time, Italians, mostly from southern Italy and Sicily, settled just to the west, around Mulberry Street, in what became Little Italy.

This was where the garment industry was born, in crowded sweatshops in tenement apartments. It was where the labor movement found some of its fiercest organizers. It was also where urban reformers, horrified by conditions, pushed the laws that shaped how cities everywhere build housing, requiring windows, air shafts, toilets, and fire escapes.

After immigration restrictions in 1924, the neighborhood slowly emptied. Puerto Rican families arrived after World War Two, giving the area east of Avenue A the name Loisaida, a Spanglish rendering of Lower East Side. Chinatown expanded from the south. And in the last few decades, the neighborhood has become a destination for bars, galleries, and restaurants, with new towers rising alongside the old tenements.

As you walk, look for the fire escapes zigzagging down the fronts of buildings, the decorative cornices at the tops of tenements, which landlords used to make cheap buildings look respectable, and the old Jewish delis and appetizing shops that have survived a century of change.
=== STOP Mulberry Street & Little Italy @ 40.7193,-73.9973
You're on Mulberry Street, the main artery of what remains of Little Italy.

In the late nineteenth century, Italian immigrants, overwhelmingly from the poor rural south, from Campania, Calabria, Basilicata, and Sicily, settled in the blocks around here. Many came as young men intending to work for a few years and return home, and many did. Others brought their families and stayed. Streets in Little Italy were often organized by region and even by village. Neapolitans clustered on one block, Sicilians on another, Genoese on a third, each with its own dialect, saints, and festivals.

The most famous of those festivals still happens right here. The Feast of San Gennaro, honoring the patron saint of Naples, has been held every September since 1926. For eleven days, Mulberry Street fills with sausage and pepper stands, zeppole, cannoli, games, and a procession carrying the saint's statue through the streets.

At its height in the early twentieth century, Little Italy stretched over dozens of blocks. Today it's a few blocks of restaurants, mostly along Mulberry between Broome and Canal. After World War Two, the children and grandchildren of the original immigrants moved to Brooklyn, Staten Island, Queens, and New Jersey. Chinatown expanded north into the space. Today, very few Italian Americans actually live here.

A few institutions remain. On Grand Street, a short walk east, Ferrara Bakery has been selling Italian pastries since 1892, and claims to be among the first espresso bars in the country. The Italian American Museum, at the corner of Mulberry and Grand, occupies the former Banca Stabile, a bank founded in the 1880s that served as a lifeline for immigrants: it held their savings, wrote letters home for those who couldn't write, sold steamship tickets, and wired money back to Italy. Banks like this, informal and family run, were the backbone of immigrant financial life.

That history connects to a much bigger story. In San Francisco, an Italian immigrant named Amadeo Giannini founded the Bank of Italy in 1904 to serve immigrants who were ignored by established banks. It grew into the Bank of America. The idea that ordinary working people deserved bank accounts and small loans was, in part, an immigrant innovation.

Little Italy is also tied to the history of organized crime, and the neighborhood is not shy about that legend. The restaurant Umberto's Clam House, for example, became infamous in 1972 when the gangster Joey Gallo was shot dead while celebrating his birthday there. It has since moved to a new location on Mulberry Street.
=== STOP Old St. Patrick's Cathedral @ 40.7237,-73.9953
At the corner of Mott and Prince Streets, behind a high brick wall, is the Basilica of St. Patrick's Old Cathedral. Before the famous St. Patrick's Cathedral on Fifth Avenue was built, this was the cathedral of the Catholic Archdiocese of New York.

It was completed in 1815, designed by Joseph François Mangin, one of the two architects of City Hall. It was among the earliest Gothic Revival buildings in the country. It was built at a time when Catholics were a small and distrusted minority in Protestant New York. As Irish immigration surged in the 1830s and 40s, anti-Catholic feeling grew violent, and the nativist movement held marches and riots against immigrants. According to tradition, the high brick wall around the churchyard, which you can still see, served as a fortification, and parishioners at times stood guard to protect the church from nativist mobs.

The church later became the spiritual home of the Italians of Little Italy as the neighborhood changed around it. In 1866, it was badly damaged by fire and rebuilt. When the new cathedral on Fifth Avenue opened in 1879, this one was demoted to a parish church. In 2010, Pope Benedict the Sixteenth elevated it to the rank of a minor basilica.

Beneath the church are catacombs, rare in New York, where prominent early Catholic families are buried. One notable figure who was once buried in the cemetery here is Pierre Toussaint, a formerly enslaved Haitian who became a wealthy hairdresser to New York's elite in the early nineteenth century, and used his fortune to support orphans, the poor, and the building of churches, including this one. He is now a candidate for sainthood, and his remains were moved to the crypt of the Fifth Avenue cathedral, a remarkable honor.

The cathedral and its grounds have appeared in many films. The baptism scene in The Godfather, in which Michael Corleone renounces Satan while his men carry out killings across the city, was filmed inside.

Walk around the outside of the wall along Mulberry and Mott Streets. This little patch of graves and old trees is one of the most peaceful and atmospheric corners in downtown Manhattan.
=== STOP The Bowery @ 40.7224,-73.9930
You're on the Bowery, one of the oldest roads in New York. Its path follows a Lenape trail, and the Dutch named it after the word bouwerij, meaning farm, because it led to the farms north of town, including Governor Peter Stuyvesant's.

In the early nineteenth century, the Bowery was a lively, working class entertainment strip, a rival to the more genteel Broadway. It had theaters, beer gardens, oyster bars, and dime museums. The Bowery Theatre, which opened in 1826, was famous for raucous crowds and melodramas. The Bowery Boys, a working class gang and youth subculture, swaggered up and down the street in high hats and slicked hair, and became stock characters in plays and later movies.

In 1878, the Third Avenue elevated railway was built above the Bowery. The El cast the street into permanent shadow and noise, and the neighborhood declined. By the early twentieth century, the Bowery had become the most famous skid row in America, lined with flophouses, cheap lodging houses where men rented cages or cots by the night, along with missions and saloons. The Bowery Mission, which opened in 1879 and still operates at 227 Bowery, is one of the oldest rescue missions in the country. For much of the twentieth century, saying someone ended up on the Bowery meant they had hit bottom.

Look at the grand building with the classical columns and arched entrance at 130 Bowery, near Grand Street. This was the Bowery Savings Bank, designed by McKim, Mead and White and completed in 1894. It was a savings bank for working people, built in a monumental style meant to reassure depositors that their small savings were safe. Today it's an event space.

Also notable is the building that looks like a stack of offset white boxes, at 235 Bowery. That's the New Museum, a contemporary art museum designed by the Japanese firm SANAA and completed in 2007. Its arrival marked the transformation of the Bowery from skid row to an art and luxury corridor.

The Bowery is also famous today for its restaurant supply district, a stretch of shops selling commercial kitchen equipment, from industrial mixers to stacks of plates. If you're a serious home cook, it's worth a browse. And farther north is where the punk club CBGB once stood, which you can visit on the East Village tour.
=== STOP The Tenement Museum @ 40.7188,-73.9900
You're now on Orchard Street, standing near 97 Orchard Street, the building that houses the Lower East Side Tenement Museum.

The building was constructed in 1863 by a German immigrant named Lucas Glockner. It's a typical tenement: five stories plus a basement, with four apartments per floor, each about three hundred twenty five square feet, divided into three small rooms. Only one of those rooms had windows to the outside. At first, there was no indoor plumbing at all: residents used outhouses in the backyard, and water came from a pump. Over about seventy years, from 1863 to 1935, an estimated seven thousand people from more than twenty countries lived in this one building.

In 1935, rather than comply with new housing laws, the landlord evicted the residents and sealed the upper floors. The apartments stayed closed for over fifty years. In 1988, the historian Ruth Abram and her colleague Anita Jacobson found the building and discovered the sealed floors almost like a time capsule, with layers of wallpaper, linoleum, and objects left behind. They founded the museum, which has restored individual apartments to tell the stories of specific families who actually lived there: a German Jewish dressmaker abandoned by her husband during the Panic of 1873, Italian Catholic immigrants, Sicilians, Eastern European Jews, Irish, and later families.

The building's history is also the history of housing law. Reformers horrified by tenement conditions pushed a series of laws. The Tenement House Act of 1879 required that every bedroom have a window, which led landlords to build the so-called dumbbell tenement, with narrow air shafts pinched into the middle of the building. The shafts were dark, foul, and often filled with garbage. Then the Tenement House Act of 1901 required much larger courtyards, indoor toilets for each apartment, and better fire safety. That 1901 law is considered one of the founding pieces of American housing regulation.

Orchard Street itself was the center of a famous pushcart market. By the early 1900s, hundreds of peddlers lined the streets selling everything from pickles and herring to hats, fabric, and pots. In the mid-twentieth century, Orchard Street became known as a bargain shopping district, especially on Sundays, since many Jewish merchants closed on Saturdays for the Sabbath.

The museum offers guided tours of the apartments, and if you have the chance, they're one of the most moving and intimate history experiences in the city.
=== STOP Eldridge Street Synagogue @ 40.7147,-73.9937
In front of you, on Eldridge Street, is a building that seems almost out of place among the tenements around it: a tall facade with a great rose window, horseshoe arches, and Moorish and Gothic details. This is the Eldridge Street Synagogue, completed in 1887.

It was the first great synagogue built in America by Eastern European Jews. Before this, most synagogues for the new immigrants were tiny congregations meeting in rented rooms or converted churches. This building was a statement. Its builders, mostly working class immigrants who had prospered a little, wanted to show that they had arrived and that Judaism had a permanent place in America. On the High Holidays at the turn of the century, the sanctuary was reportedly packed with worshippers, with crowds spilling into the street.

The Moorish Revival style, with its horseshoe arches and geometric patterns, was popular for synagogues across Europe and America in the nineteenth century. It evoked the era of Muslim Spain, sometimes remembered as a golden age for Jewish culture, and set synagogues apart from the Gothic style of Christian churches.

As the Jewish population moved away to Brooklyn, the Bronx, and the suburbs, the congregation shrank dramatically. By the 1950s, the main sanctuary was closed off, and the remaining worshippers met in the smaller basement chapel. Pigeons lived in the sanctuary, and the building deteriorated badly. In the 1980s, preservationists launched a long effort to save it, and after a restoration of about twenty years, it reopened in 2007 as the Museum at Eldridge Street.

Look at the great round window on the facade. The original window had been lost, and in 2010 it was replaced with a new stained glass window designed by the artist Kiki Smith with the architect Deborah Gans, featuring a field of stars on a blue background.

The synagogue now sits at the edge of Chinatown. On the surrounding blocks you'll see Chinese signs, groceries, and businesses, mixed with traces of the old Jewish neighborhood: an old building cornerstone with Hebrew lettering, a former matzo bakery, a sign over a doorway. It's a compact lesson in how New York neighborhoods layer one community atop another.
=== STOP Seward Park & the Forward Building @ 40.7140,-73.9902
You're at Seward Park, at the corner of Essex Street and East Broadway. Opened in 1903, it's often cited as the first permanent, municipally built public playground in the United States. Reformers argued that children of the tenements had nowhere to play but the street, and that parks with organized play would make them healthier and keep them out of trouble. On opening day, crowds of children reportedly swarmed the playground.

Now look at the tall building across the street at 175 East Broadway, with ornate carvings and the name FORWARD across the top. This was the headquarters of the Jewish Daily Forward, known in Yiddish as the Forverts. It was founded in 1897, and under its legendary editor, Abraham Cahan, it became the most widely read Yiddish newspaper in the world. At its peak in the 1920s, it had a circulation of around a quarter million copies.

The Forward was socialist, pro-union, and passionately engaged in teaching immigrants how to become Americans. It ran English lessons, advice on etiquette and hygiene, and serialized fiction by writers including Isaac Bashevis Singer, who later won the Nobel Prize. Its most famous feature was the Bintel Brief, meaning a bundle of letters, an advice column where readers wrote in with their problems: husbands who had abandoned them, children who refused to keep kosher, dilemmas of love, work, and faith in a new country. It's one of the great primary sources of immigrant life.

Look closely at the facade. Above the entrance, there are relief portraits of socialist figures, including Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels. Today the building has been converted into luxury condominiums, which says something about how far the neighborhood has traveled.

Nearby on East Broadway is the Educational Alliance, a settlement house founded by established German Jews to help the newer Eastern European arrivals assimilate. It offered classes, libraries, art lessons, and a gym. Among the young people who passed through it were artists and performers who went on to shape American culture.

The garment unions also had deep roots here. The International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union, founded in 1900, drew much of its strength from Jewish and Italian women working in shops on these streets. In 1909, tens of thousands of shirtwaist makers went on strike in what became known as the Uprising of the Twenty Thousand. Two years later, the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire, which you'll hear about on the Greenwich Village tour, killed one hundred forty six workers, many of them young women from this neighborhood.
=== STOP Katz's, Russ & Daughters & Houston Street @ 40.7223,-73.9874
You're now on East Houston Street, which, a New Yorker will tell you, is pronounced HOW-ston, not like the city in Texas. It is named after William Houstoun, a Georgia delegate to the Continental Congress.

On the corner of Houston and Ludlow is Katz's Delicatessen, founded in 1888. It's one of the last of the great Jewish delis that once filled these streets. You take a ticket at the door, order from a counterman who slices your pastrami by hand, and the counterman will usually give you a sample while he works. A tip, traditionally, earns you a thicker sandwich. During World War Two, when the owners' sons were serving overseas, Katz's coined the slogan Send a salami to your boy in the army, and shipped salamis to soldiers. And yes, the famous scene in the 1989 movie When Harry Met Sally was filmed here. A sign hangs over the table where it happened.

A few doors west is Russ and Daughters, an appetizing shop, opened in 1914 by Joel Russ, a Polish immigrant who began by selling herring from a pushcart. Note the distinction: in New York Jewish food culture, a delicatessen sells meat, and an appetizing store sells fish and dairy, things you eat with bagels, like smoked salmon, herring, whitefish, and cream cheese. The separation comes from kosher dietary law, which prohibits mixing meat and dairy. Russ and Daughters was also notable as one of the first businesses in the country to have Daughters in its name. Joel Russ had no sons, and he made his daughters partners in the 1930s.

Also on Houston Street is Yonah Schimmel Knish Bakery, which has been baking knishes, dough pockets stuffed with potato, kasha, or other fillings, since 1910. It looks like it hasn't changed much since then.

These three businesses are survivors. In 1920, the Lower East Side had dozens of delis, appetizing stores, bakeries, and dairy restaurants. Most closed as the Jewish population left. The ones that remain survived by becoming destinations, drawing visitors from all over the world.
=== STOP Essex Market & the Williamsburg Bridge @ 40.7182,-73.9877
End your walk at Delancey and Essex Streets, near the Manhattan entrance of the Williamsburg Bridge.

Essex Market, now in a modern building at the Essex Crossing development, has its roots in a reform by Mayor Fiorello La Guardia. In the 1930s, the streets of the Lower East Side were still clogged with thousands of pushcart vendors. La Guardia, the son of an Italian father and a Jewish mother from Trieste, wanted to modernize the city and clear the streets. He created a system of enclosed public markets, where vendors could rent stalls with refrigeration and running water. The original Essex Market opened in 1940, across the street. In 2019, it moved into the new building here.

Essex Crossing itself has an unusual history. In 1967, the city demolished tenements on several blocks here for an urban renewal project, displacing hundreds of mostly Puerto Rican and Jewish families, with a promise that they could return to new housing. But politics stalled the project, and for nearly fifty years the land sat empty, mostly as parking lots. It was one of the largest undeveloped city-owned sites in Manhattan. Construction finally began in the 2010s, with a mix of market rate and affordable housing, a park, offices, and the market.

Now look up at the Williamsburg Bridge, rising above Delancey Street. When it opened in 1903, it was the longest suspension bridge in the world, surpassing the Brooklyn Bridge. It was also the first major suspension bridge with towers made entirely of steel rather than stone. Its builders went for strength over beauty, and critics at the time considered it ugly.

But the bridge transformed both sides of the river. It gave the crowded Lower East Side an escape route. Tens of thousands of Jewish families moved across it to the Williamsburg section of Brooklyn, so many that the bridge was sometimes nicknamed the Jews' highway. Today, Williamsburg is home to a large Hasidic community, as well as the young, artsy population that made it famous in the 2000s. You can walk across the bridge on its pedestrian path, and pick up the Williamsburg and Greenpoint tour on the other side.

Look back at Delancey Street as you go. It was widened for the bridge approach, and it became a busy commercial strip of bargain stores. The 1988 movie Crossing Delancey took its title from the idea that crossing this street, from the old neighborhood to the uptown world, was the defining journey for the children of immigrants. It still is, in a way. The story of the Lower East Side is the story of people arriving, working, saving, and eventually crossing over to somewhere else.
