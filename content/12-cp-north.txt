=== TOUR
id: cp-north
title: Central Park: The Ramble to Harlem Meer
area: Manhattan
blurb: The Ramble's woods, Belvedere Castle, the Great Lawn, a 3,500-year-old obelisk, Seneca Village, the Reservoir, the North Woods, and the Conservatory Garden.
=== OVERVIEW
The northern two-thirds of Central Park are quieter, wilder, and less visited than the southern end, and many New Yorkers consider them the best part of the park.

Olmsted and Vaux designed the park as a sequence of moods. The south end, near the city's busiest streets, has the more formal and social features: the Mall, the Dairy, the Carousel, the zoo. As you move north, the landscape becomes more rugged and more pastoral. The Ramble, north of the Lake, is a dense, winding woodland. Above it, at Vista Rock, Belvedere Castle overlooks the park. Beyond that are the great open lawns and the Reservoir. And in the far north, above 100th Street, the North Woods were designed to evoke the Adirondack Mountains, with a ravine, a stream, and waterfalls.

The northern section also holds the park's deepest history. During the Revolutionary War, British and Hessian troops built fortifications on the high ground near the northern end, and during the War of 1812, Americans built a blockhouse there that still stands, the oldest structure in the park. And on the west side, between about 82nd and 89th Streets, there once stood Seneca Village, a community of African American property owners, along with Irish and German immigrants, that was destroyed to build the park.

The north end of the park was also slower to be completed, and for much of the twentieth century it was less maintained and less safe than the south end. In the 1970s and 80s, much of it was overgrown and neglected. The restoration of the north end by the Central Park Conservancy, beginning in the 1980s and continuing to this day, has been one of its biggest achievements. Projects completed in recent years include a rebuilt Lasker Rink and pool area near the Harlem Meer, reconnecting the park's landscape to the water.

This tour starts in the Ramble, just north of the Lake, and ends at the Conservatory Garden on Fifth Avenue at 105th Street. It's a long walk, a little over two miles if you take a direct path, so feel free to take it in pieces.
=== STOP The Ramble @ 40.7783,-73.9700
You're in the Ramble, about thirty-six acres of woodland just north of the Lake, roughly between 73rd and 79th Streets.

Olmsted called it a wild garden. It was designed to feel like a natural forest, with winding paths, rocky outcrops, a small stream called the Gill, and dense trees and shrubs. But it's as carefully designed as any part of the park. The stream is artificial and fed by city water. The rocks were placed. The paths were laid out to disorient you, so that you lose your sense of the city around you. It's very easy to get lost in the Ramble, and that's exactly the point.

The Ramble is one of the best birding spots in North America. Central Park sits on the Atlantic Flyway, a major migration route for birds traveling between South and Central America and Canada. For migrating birds flying over a vast expanse of concrete, Central Park looks like an oasis, and they drop in to rest and feed. More than two hundred species of birds have been recorded in the park. In the spring and fall migration seasons, especially May, the Ramble fills with warblers, thrushes, tanagers, and orioles, and with birders carrying binoculars. Look for small feeders near the Azalea Pond in winter.

The park's most famous birds, though, have lived in the surrounding city. In the 1990s, a red-tailed hawk nicknamed Pale Male built a nest on the facade of a luxury apartment building on Fifth Avenue, overlooking the park, and he and his mates raised chicks there for years. When the building's co-op board removed the nest in 2004, birders protested, and the nest was eventually allowed to be rebuilt. More recently, a Eurasian eagle-owl named Flaco, who escaped from the Central Park Zoo in 2023, became a city celebrity during the year he lived free in the park and around Manhattan.

In the twentieth century, the Ramble also became known as a gathering place for gay men, who found privacy in its winding paths during an era when gay life was largely hidden. It's part of the park's history that is now openly acknowledged.
=== STOP Belvedere Castle & Turtle Pond @ 40.7794,-73.9690
Climb up to Belvedere Castle, the small stone castle perched on Vista Rock, the second highest natural point in the park.

Calvert Vaux and Jacob Wrey Mould designed it, and it was completed in 1869. It's a folly, an ornamental building with no real defensive purpose, a fantasy castle designed to be seen from a distance as a romantic focal point. The name Belvedere means beautiful view in Italian, and from the castle's terraces, you get one of the best views in the park, over Turtle Pond, the Great Lawn, and the surrounding skyline.

Originally, the castle had open windows and no doors. It was meant as a lookout, not a building. In 1919, the United States Weather Bureau set up a station here, and the castle was enclosed. Today, automated instruments here and nearby measure the official weather for Central Park, which is often cited in weather reports as the weather for New York City. When you hear on the news that Central Park got six inches of snow, those measurements come from instruments here and nearby.

By the 1970s, the castle had been vandalized and was in ruins. It was restored in the 1980s, and again in 2019. It now houses a visitor center with nature exhibits for children.

Below the castle is Turtle Pond, which, as its name suggests, is home to many turtles, mostly red-eared sliders, many of them abandoned pets. Look for them basking on rocks and logs on sunny days. The pond is what's left of the old Lower Reservoir, which was drained in the 1930s.

West of the pond is the Delacorte Theater, the open-air amphitheater that has been home to Shakespeare in the Park since 1962. The producer Joseph Papp began staging free Shakespeare in the 1950s, and fought Robert Moses, who wanted to charge admission. Papp won, and the theater was built with funding from George Delacorte. Performances are still free, and people line up for hours, sometimes overnight, for tickets. Stars like Meryl Streep, Al Pacino, and Denzel Washington have performed here.

Next to the theater is the Shakespeare Garden, planted with flowers and plants mentioned in Shakespeare's plays and poems.
=== STOP The Great Lawn @ 40.7812,-73.9665
North of the castle and Turtle Pond is the Great Lawn, a vast oval field of about fifty-five acres, with baseball diamonds and broad lawns.

It wasn't originally a lawn at all. In Olmsted and Vaux's plan, this space was occupied by the Lower Reservoir, a rectangular reservoir built in the 1840s as part of the Croton water system, which predated the park and was incorporated into it. By the 1930s, the reservoir was no longer needed and was drained.

While it sat empty during the Great Depression, homeless New Yorkers built a shantytown in the empty reservoir bed. They called it Hooverville, or Hoover Valley, after President Herbert Hoover, whom they blamed for the Depression. Shacks were made of scrap wood, cardboard, and tin, and some residents, unemployed bricklayers and carpenters, built surprisingly substantial structures. Hoovervilles sprang up all over the country, but this one, in the middle of the richest city in America, was a powerful symbol. The camp was cleared in 1933, and the reservoir bed was filled in to create the Great Lawn.

Since then, the Great Lawn has been the site of some of the largest gatherings in American history. In September 1981, Simon and Garfunkel reunited for a free concert here, drawing about half a million people. In June 1982, about a million people gathered here for a rally against nuclear weapons, one of the largest political demonstrations in American history. Pope John Paul the Second celebrated mass here in 1995, and in 1997, Garth Brooks drew an enormous crowd for a free concert.

All those crowds took a toll. By the 1990s, the Great Lawn was a dust bowl. The Conservancy rebuilt it in the late 1990s with new drainage, irrigation, and soil, and now closes it during wet weather and in winter to protect the grass. Big concerts are mostly gone, but the New York Philharmonic and the Metropolitan Opera still hold free summer performances here.

Look around at the skyline framing the lawn: the towers of Fifth Avenue to the east, and Central Park West to the west, rise just above the trees, like walls around a giant room.
=== STOP Cleopatra's Needle @ 40.7797,-73.9654
On a small hill just east of the Great Lawn, behind the Metropolitan Museum of Art, stands the oldest man-made object in Central Park, and one of the oldest outdoor monuments anywhere in the Americas: a granite obelisk nicknamed Cleopatra's Needle.

It has nothing to do with Cleopatra. It was carved around 1450 BC, more than a thousand years before Cleopatra's time, for the Egyptian pharaoh Thutmose the Third, and erected at the temple city of Heliopolis. Look for the hieroglyphics carved on its sides, though they're badly weathered on some faces. Around 12 BC, the Romans moved it to Alexandria, where it stood outside a temple built by Cleopatra in honor of Julius Caesar, or Mark Antony, depending on the source. That's how it got its nickname.

In the 1870s, the ruler of Egypt, Khedive Ismail Pasha, offered the obelisk to the United States as a gift, hoping to win friendship and support. A similar obelisk had gone to London. The cost of moving it, more than one hundred thousand dollars, was paid by William Henry Vanderbilt, the son of the Commodore. A Navy officer named Henry Gorringe engineered the move. The obelisk, about seventy feet tall and weighing over two hundred tons, was lowered, loaded into the hold of a steamship through a hole cut in its hull, shipped across the Atlantic, floated to the Hudson River, and hauled across Manhattan on a special railway over several months. Its cornerstone was laid in October 1880, in a Masonic ceremony attended by thousands, and the obelisk itself was raised here in January 1881.

New York's climate, with its freeze-thaw cycles and air pollution, has been much harder on the stone than three thousand years in Egypt. Some of the hieroglyphics have eroded significantly since it arrived.

Look at the base, at the corners. You'll see bronze crabs. They're replicas of crabs the Romans placed under the obelisk to support it when they moved it to Alexandria. The originals are in the Met.

Speaking of which, the massive building next to you is the back of the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Look for the glass wall of the Sackler Wing. Inside, visible through the glass, is the Temple of Dendur, an Egyptian temple built around 15 BC, given to the United States by Egypt in the 1960s, in gratitude for American help in saving monuments threatened by the Aswan High Dam. So here, two ancient Egyptian monuments face each other across a park in Manhattan.
=== STOP Seneca Village @ 40.7852,-73.9693
Walk west across the park to the area near West 85th Street, near Central Park West. Here, between roughly 82nd and 89th Streets, stood Seneca Village.

In 1825, a young African American shoeshiner named Andrew Williams bought three lots of land here from a white landowner named John Whitehead. Other Black New Yorkers followed, buying lots of their own. At that time, slavery had not yet been fully abolished in New York State; final emancipation came in 1827. Seneca Village was one of the first communities of African American property owners in New York.

Owning property mattered for another reason. In 1821, New York State had changed its voting laws, eliminating property requirements for white men, but requiring Black men to own at least two hundred and fifty dollars in property to vote. In Seneca Village, many Black residents met that threshold. Property ownership was a path to political rights.

By the 1850s, Seneca Village had about two hundred twenty-five residents, mostly African American, along with Irish and German immigrants. It had three churches, including an African Methodist Episcopal Zion church, a school, and cemeteries. Residents worked as laborers, servants, cooks, and in various trades. It was a stable, settled community, far from the crowded neighborhoods downtown.

In 1857, the city took the land by eminent domain to build Central Park. Residents were compensated, though many contested the amounts as inadequate. The village was destroyed. Newspapers of the time sometimes described the area's residents dismissively, calling their homes shanties, which helped justify the removal. Where the residents went is largely unknown.

For more than a century, Seneca Village was mostly forgotten. In the 1990s, historians began to recover its story, and in 2011, archaeologists excavated the site, finding foundations of houses and thousands of artifacts, including household items like ceramics, a toothbrush, and a child's shoe. In recent years, the Conservancy has installed signs and a display here explaining the history.

As you stand here, look at the landscape around you: the gentle slope, the rocks, the playground nearby. It's a reminder that the park's pastoral beauty was built on top of a real community, with real homes and families.
=== STOP The Reservoir @ 40.7855,-73.9624
Climb to the Reservoir, the vast body of water covering much of the park between 86th and 96th Streets. Officially, it's the Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis Reservoir.

It was built between 1858 and 1862, as part of the Croton water system, to hold water from the Croton Aqueduct. It covers about one hundred six acres, and holds about a billion gallons of water. Olmsted and Vaux had to design the park around it, and its huge size is one reason the park is so narrow in places.

For more than a century, it was part of the city's water supply. In 1993, it was decommissioned, after a newer system of underground water tunnels made it obsolete. The water you see today is no longer for drinking.

Running around it is a track of about one point six miles, one of the most famous running paths in the world. Joggers have used it since the jogging craze of the 1970s, and on a nice morning, it's packed with runners. Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis, who lived on Fifth Avenue facing the park, was a regular jogger here, and after her death in 1994, the reservoir was renamed in her honor. She was also a passionate advocate for the city's landmarks and was instrumental in saving Grand Central Terminal.

Look around: the view across the water is one of the most spectacular in the city, especially at sunrise or sunset, with the skylines of the Upper East and Upper West Sides on either side, and Midtown's towers to the south. The open water also attracts birds, especially in winter, when ducks, geese, gulls, and grebes gather here.

Look for the old gatehouses on the north and south ends of the Reservoir. These stone buildings housed the machinery that controlled the flow of water. Along the reservoir's edges, notice the beautiful iron fences. They were replaced in the 2000s with replicas of the original 1920s designs.

North of the Reservoir lies the North Meadow, a large area with baseball and soccer fields, and the Central Park Tennis Center, where New Yorkers have played tennis for decades.
=== STOP The North Woods & the Blockhouse @ 40.7962,-73.9568
Walk to the northern end of the park, into the North Woods, around 102nd to 106th Streets on the west side.

Olmsted and Vaux designed this area to evoke the Adirondack Mountains of upstate New York. It's the most rugged part of the park, with steep slopes, rocky outcrops, and dense woods. Through it runs the Loch, Scottish for lake, a stream that flows through a ravine, over small waterfalls, into the Harlem Meer. The stream is artificial; its water comes from the city supply. But the effect is strikingly real. Standing in the ravine, it's easy to forget you're in Manhattan.

Look for the stone arches that carry paths and roads over the ravine. The Glen Span Arch, at the western entrance to the ravine, is a rustic stone arch. And the Huddlestone Arch, near the east end, is made of huge, rough boulders, some weighing many tons, held together only by gravity, without any mortar.

Now climb to the top of the rocky hill at the northwest corner of the North Woods, near 109th Street. Here you'll find the Blockhouse, a small stone fort and the oldest structure in Central Park.

It was built in 1814, during the War of 1812. The British had burned Washington, D.C., that August, and New Yorkers feared a British invasion from the north, by way of the Harlem River. Volunteers built a line of fortifications across upper Manhattan, including this blockhouse, on high ground overlooking the approaches to the city. The British never came. The blockhouse never saw combat. Olmsted and Vaux kept it as a picturesque ruin. Today it's an empty stone shell, but its walls and gun ports are still visible.

This area also saw action in the Revolutionary War. In 1776, after the Battle of Brooklyn, George Washington's army retreated north through Manhattan. The British later built fortifications in this area, near a tavern called McGown's Pass Tavern, which stood on a hill near the east side of the park, overlooking the old Post Road. The steep ridge here made it a strategic chokepoint.
=== STOP Harlem Meer & the Conservatory Garden @ 40.7937,-73.9524
End your walk on the east side of the park, at the Harlem Meer and the Conservatory Garden.

The Harlem Meer is an eleven-acre lake in the park's northeast corner. Meer is the Dutch word for lake, a nod to the Dutch village of Haarlem that stood nearby in the colonial era. The Meer was dredged and restored in the 1990s and is now stocked with fish for catch-and-release fishing. On its north shore is the Dana Discovery Center, a visitor center. On the south side, look for the newly rebuilt pool and rink complex, which has reopened the shoreline to the public.

Now walk to Fifth Avenue at about 105th Street, and look for an ornate wrought iron gate. These are the Vanderbilt Gates, which once stood at the entrance to the Cornelius Vanderbilt the Second mansion on Fifth Avenue at 58th Street, which you may have heard about on the Fifth Avenue tour. They were made in Paris in 1894. When the mansion was demolished, the gates were donated to the city, and they were installed here in 1939.

Walk through the gates into the Conservatory Garden, the only formal garden in Central Park. It's six acres, divided into three sections, each in a different style. The central garden, in the Italian style, has a large lawn surrounded by hedges and an iron pergola covered with wisteria. The north garden, in the French style, has concentric beds of flowers around a fountain called the Untermyer Fountain, with three dancing maidens. The south garden, in the English style, has lush perennial borders and a small pool with a statue of two children, inspired by the characters in Frances Hodgson Burnett's novel The Secret Garden.

The garden's name comes from a large glass conservatory that stood here from 1899 to 1934, when it was demolished. Robert Moses then created the formal garden, which opened in 1937. By the 1970s, it had fallen into disrepair. It was restored in the 1980s and is now one of the most beautiful and peaceful places in the city. It's a popular spot for wedding photos.

It's a fitting end for a walk through the park. Olmsted and Vaux designed Central Park to give people of all classes access to beauty, peace, and nature. Here, in the northern end, near Harlem and East Harlem, that vision is as alive as ever.
