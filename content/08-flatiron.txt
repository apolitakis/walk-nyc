title: Union Square, Flatiron & Gramercy
area: Manhattan
blurb: Protests and Tammany Hall, Ladies' Mile department stores, Teddy Roosevelt's birthplace, the Flatiron, and Tin Pan Alley.
=== OVERVIEW
In the second half of the nineteenth century, the center of New York was not Midtown and not Wall Street. It was the stretch of Broadway and Fifth Avenue between 14th and 34th Streets. This was where fashionable New Yorkers shopped, dined, went to the theater, and lived.

The story of this area is the story of the city's relentless northward march. As commerce pushed up from lower Manhattan, wealthy families kept moving north to stay ahead of it. In the 1830s and 40s, they built mansions around Union Square and Gramercy Park. By the 1850s and 60s, Madison Square, at 23rd Street, was the most fashionable address in the city, surrounded by luxury hotels and mansions. Then the shops followed. In the 1870s and 80s, a stretch of Broadway and Sixth Avenue became known as Ladies' Mile, lined with enormous department stores that catered to middle- and upper-class women. Theaters clustered around Union Square and Madison Square. Restaurants like Delmonico's moved uptown to be near their clientele.

By the early 1900s, the fashionable crowd had moved on again, to upper Fifth Avenue and Midtown, and the department stores followed them to Herald Square and Fifth Avenue. The neighborhood shifted to light manufacturing, toy companies, publishing houses, and photography studios. The area around the Flatiron Building became the center of the toy industry, with an annual toy fair. Later, in the 1990s, the neighborhood got a new identity as Silicon Alley, the hub of New York's first internet startup boom, with companies like DoubleClick, which Google later acquired.

This walk takes you through some of the most important buildings in the history of American architecture and commerce: the Flatiron, which helped define the skyscraper; the Metropolitan Life Tower; the grand cast iron and stone palaces of Ladies' Mile; and the private, gated Gramercy Park, which still requires a key to enter. Along the way, you'll hear about Tammany Hall, Theodore Roosevelt, Andy Warhol, and the birth of the American popular song.
=== STOP Union Square @ 40.7358,-73.9906
You're in Union Square. The name doesn't refer to labor unions, though it would be fitting. It comes from the union, or junction, of two major roads: Broadway and the old Bowery Road, which met here.

In the nineteenth century, this was a fashionable residential square, and then, as commerce moved in, a center of theaters, hotels, and luxury retail. Tiffany and Company, for example, had its flagship store on the west side of the square from 1870 to 1906, in a cast iron building that still stands. Tiffany was founded downtown in 1837 by Charles Lewis Tiffany as a stationery and fancy goods store, and became the most prestigious jeweler in America. The company followed its customers north, from lower Broadway to Union Square, and later to Fifth Avenue.

Union Square's real identity, though, is as New York's great stage for protest. On September fifth, 1882, about ten thousand workers marched from City Hall to Union Square in what is considered the first Labor Day parade in the United States. In the following decades, socialists, anarchists, suffragists, and labor unions rallied here. In 1927, crowds gathered here to protest the execution of Sacco and Vanzetti, two Italian anarchists convicted of murder in a widely criticized trial. After September eleventh, 2001, the square became a spontaneous memorial, filled with candles and handwritten messages. It remains the go-to place for protests in the city.

Look at the equestrian statue of George Washington at the south end. Sculpted by Henry Kirke Brown, it was dedicated in 1856, and it's one of the oldest public sculptures in New York.

On the east side of the square, at the corner of 17th Street, look for the neo-Georgian building with a peaked roof. This was the last home of Tammany Hall, the Democratic political machine that dominated New York politics for more than a century. Tammany provided jobs, favors, and help to immigrants in exchange for votes, and its bosses, including William Tweed, became synonymous with corruption. Tammany moved into this building in 1929. Its power faded in the following decades, and today the building houses offices and shops.

At the south side of the square, above the stores, you might see a giant digital display. Originally an art installation called Metronome, it was reprogrammed in 2020 as the Climate Clock, counting down the time left to limit global warming.

On the west side, at 33 Union Square West, the Decker Building, with its Moorish-style details, was where Andy Warhol moved his studio, The Factory, in 1968. In June of that year, the radical feminist writer Valerie Solanas shot Warhol there. He survived, but barely.

On Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays, and Saturdays, the square hosts the Union Square Greenmarket, founded in 1976, which helped start the farmers' market movement in New York. Some of the city's top chefs shop here.
=== STOP Ladies' Mile @ 40.7389,-73.9902
You're on Broadway, around 18th to 20th Streets. Look around at the grand buildings on both sides, many with cast iron facades, mansard roofs, and huge plate-glass windows at street level. These were department stores, and this was Ladies' Mile.

From about the 1860s to the early 1900s, the area along Broadway, Fifth Avenue, and especially Sixth Avenue between roughly 14th and 23rd Streets was the premier shopping district in America. The department store was essentially invented in this period, and some of the most important American retailers had their flagships here: Lord and Taylor, Arnold Constable, B. Altman, Siegel-Cooper, and R. H. Macy and Company, which was on Sixth Avenue at 14th Street before moving to Herald Square in 1902.

Department stores changed American life. They offered fixed prices instead of haggling. They displayed huge varieties of goods under one roof. They offered amenities like restaurants, restrooms, reading rooms, and delivery. And critically, they created one of the first public spaces where middle-class women could go alone, without male chaperones, and spend the day. That's why the area was called Ladies' Mile.

Look for the Lord and Taylor building at Broadway and 20th Street, completed in 1870, with its cast iron facade and corner tower. Lord and Taylor was founded in 1826 and became one of the oldest department stores in the country, before closing its last stores in 2021. Over on Sixth Avenue, look for the massive Siegel-Cooper building, between 18th and 19th Streets, completed in 1896. It was one of the largest stores in the world. It featured a fountain under its central atrium with a statue of the Republic, and the phrase Meet me at the fountain became a common way for New Yorkers to arrange meetings.

The El, the elevated railway along Sixth Avenue, brought shoppers right to the store doors. But by the early 1900s, the stores began following their wealthy customers north, to Herald Square and Fifth Avenue in Midtown. The buildings were taken over by manufacturers and wholesalers. In 1989, the area was designated the Ladies' Mile Historic District, and in recent decades, retail has returned, with big-box stores and chains filling the old palaces.

The ABC Carpet and Home store on Broadway at 19th Street occupies a building originally built for W. and J. Sloane, a high-end furniture and carpet firm. The interior, crammed with antiques and rugs, still gives a hint of the old department store atmosphere.
=== STOP Theodore Roosevelt's Birthplace @ 40.7386,-73.9893
At 28 East 20th Street, between Broadway and Park Avenue South, look for a brownstone with a flag. This is the Theodore Roosevelt Birthplace National Historic Site.

Theodore Roosevelt was born here on October twenty-seventh, 1858, into a wealthy old New York family of Dutch descent. His father, Theodore Senior, was a glass importer, banker, and philanthropist who helped found the American Museum of Natural History and the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Young Theodore was a sickly, asthmatic child, and he famously built up his strength through intense exercise, including in a home gymnasium his father had built for him on the porch.

Roosevelt lived here until he was fourteen. He went on to become a state legislator, New York City police commissioner, assistant secretary of the Navy, leader of the Rough Riders in the Spanish-American War, governor of New York, vice president, and, after the assassination of William McKinley in 1901, president of the United States. At forty-two, he was the youngest person ever to become president. As president, he was a trustbuster, breaking up corporate monopolies, including the Northern Securities railroad trust organized by J. P. Morgan. He was a conservationist who protected huge areas of public land, and he won the Nobel Peace Prize for mediating an end to the Russo-Japanese War.

Here's a twist: this is not the original house. The original was demolished in 1916 to make way for a commercial building. After Roosevelt died in 1919, his family and supporters bought the site and rebuilt the house as a replica, with Roosevelt's sister and widow helping to recreate the interiors from memory and photographs. Many of the furnishings inside are original to the family. The reconstruction was designed by Theodate Pope Riddle, one of the first women licensed as an architect in the United States. It opened in 1923.

The museum is run by the National Park Service, and tours of the rooms are free. It's a strange and interesting artifact: a historically accurate reconstruction of a house from the 1860s, built in the 1920s, and now itself almost a century old.
=== STOP Gramercy Park @ 40.7378,-73.9862
You're at Gramercy Park, between 20th and 21st Streets and Park Avenue South and Third Avenue. Notice something unusual: the park is surrounded by a tall iron fence, and the gates are locked.

Gramercy Park is one of the very few private parks left in New York. It was created in 1831 by a lawyer and developer named Samuel Ruggles, who bought a marshy farm here, drained it, and laid out sixty-six lots around a private park. Owners of the lots surrounding the park were given keys, and the right to use the park was written into the property deeds. To this day, only residents of the buildings facing the park have access, and keys are tightly controlled. The locks are changed each year. Hotel guests at the Gramercy Park Hotel have sometimes been given access, and once a year, on Christmas Eve, the park is traditionally opened to the public for a few hours.

The name Gramercy is thought to come from a Dutch name for the marshy area, possibly Krom Moerasje, meaning little crooked swamp, and a creek that ran through it.

Look around at the historic buildings surrounding the park. On the south side, at 15 Gramercy Park South, is the National Arts Club, housed in a Victorian Gothic mansion that once belonged to Samuel J. Tilden. Tilden was the governor of New York who helped bring down Boss Tweed, and he ran for president in 1876, winning the popular vote but losing in the Electoral College after a disputed deal. According to a long-told story, Tilden, fearful of his political enemies, had a secret tunnel built from his house to escape to 19th Street. The mansion's facade, renovated by Calvert Vaux, features carved portraits of writers like Shakespeare, Goethe, and Dante.

Next door, at number 16, is The Players, a private club founded in 1888 by the actor Edwin Booth, one of the greatest Shakespearean actors of the nineteenth century. Booth was also the brother of John Wilkes Booth, the man who assassinated Abraham Lincoln. Edwin was devastated by his brother's crime and withdrew from the stage for months. Stanford White redesigned the building for the club. Booth lived in an apartment on the top floor, and his room has been preserved as it was when he died in 1893. Look for the statue in the center of the park: it's Edwin Booth, as Hamlet.

A short walk east, at Irving Place and 18th Street, is Pete's Tavern, which claims to be the oldest continuously operating bar in New York, open since 1864, and to have stayed open during Prohibition disguised as a flower shop. The writer O. Henry lived nearby and, according to the bar, wrote his famous Christmas story The Gift of the Magi in one of its booths.
=== STOP The Flatiron Building @ 40.7411,-73.9897
You're looking at one of the most famous buildings in the world, the Flatiron Building, at the intersection of Broadway, Fifth Avenue, and 23rd Street.

When it was completed in 1902, it was officially named the Fuller Building, after the George A. Fuller Company, a pioneering Chicago construction firm that built it as its New York headquarters. But New Yorkers immediately nicknamed it the Flatiron, because its triangular shape resembled a clothes iron. The building fills a narrow triangular lot created where Broadway crosses Fifth Avenue on a diagonal. At its narrowest point, the tip, it's only about six and a half feet wide.

The architect was Daniel Burnham of Chicago, one of the great figures of American architecture. He had led the design of the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. He's famous for the phrase, Make no little plans; they have no magic to stir men's blood. The Flatiron is a steel-frame skyscraper, but Burnham clad it in limestone and terracotta, with elaborate decoration inspired by the Italian Renaissance. The facade is divided like a classical column, with a base, a shaft, and a capital.

When it opened, many people thought it would blow over. The intersection does create strong wind gusts, and there's a well-known story that in the early 1900s, men would gather on 23rd Street to watch the wind lift women's skirts. When police shooed them away, it was called giving them the twenty-three skidoo. Etymologists aren't sure this story is the true origin of the phrase, but it's a good one.

The building became a favorite subject for photographers and painters. Alfred Stieglitz and Edward Steichen both made famous photographs of it, in fog and twilight, which helped establish photography as a fine art. Stieglitz said it looked like a ship moving forward.

In the 1990s, the area around the building was nicknamed Silicon Alley, as internet startups moved into the neighborhood's old loft buildings. The Flatiron itself was home to the publishing company Macmillan for decades. More recently, the building was emptied for a long renovation, and plans have been announced to convert much of it into residences.

The best view is from the small plaza at the north tip, where you can see its prow cutting through the city like the bow of a ship.
=== STOP Madison Square Park & the Met Life Tower @ 40.7420,-73.9877
You're in Madison Square Park, across from the Flatiron Building. In the second half of the nineteenth century, Madison Square was the most fashionable place in New York.

The park was surrounded by grand hotels, like the Fifth Avenue Hotel, where presidents stayed and political deals were made, and by mansions. Delmonico's opened a restaurant on the square. In the 1870s and 80s, the raised right arm and torch of the Statue of Liberty was displayed in this park for several years, part of a fundraising campaign to pay for the statue's pedestal. Visitors paid to climb up inside the torch.

Look across Madison Avenue, at the northeast corner of the park. On that site stood the original Madison Square Garden. The second Garden, designed by Stanford White and opened in 1890, was one of the grandest entertainment buildings in the country, with a tall tower modeled on the Giralda in Seville, topped by a gilded statue of Diana by Augustus Saint-Gaudens. On June twenty-fifth, 1906, Stanford White was shot dead in the rooftop theater of the Garden by Harry Kendall Thaw, a deranged millionaire who was obsessed with his wife, Evelyn Nesbit, a famous model and showgirl with whom White had had a relationship. The trial was called the trial of the century. The Garden was demolished in 1925, and the name moved uptown. On the site now stands the New York Life Insurance Building, by Cass Gilbert, completed in 1928, with its golden pyramid roof.

Now look at the tall white tower on the east side of the park, at Madison Avenue and 24th Street, with a giant clock and a gold cupola. This is the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company Tower, completed in 1909. Modeled on the Campanile of St. Mark's in Venice, it was the tallest building in the world until the Woolworth Building surpassed it in 1913. Each of its four clock faces is about twenty-six feet across. Met Life was one of the giants of the insurance industry, selling small policies to working-class families, and its headquarters here were a symbol of its dominance.

The park has a few more stories. Baseball's early history is linked to this area: the Knickerbocker Base Ball Club was organized near here in 1845 by players who had been playing on open lots around Madison Square, and its rules became the basis of modern baseball. At the northwest corner of the park, a granite obelisk marks the grave of General William Worth, a hero of the Mexican-American War, who is actually buried underneath it.

And finally, look for the Shake Shack in the park. It started in 2001 as a hot dog cart run by the restaurateur Danny Meyer, as part of an art project in the park. The cart was so popular that he opened a permanent kiosk in 2004. Shake Shack is now a publicly traded company with hundreds of locations around the world.
=== STOP Tin Pan Alley @ 40.7458,-73.9893
End your walk on West 28th Street, between Broadway and Sixth Avenue. On the north side of the street, look for a row of modest nineteenth-century brick buildings. This block is Tin Pan Alley, the birthplace of the American popular music industry.

From around the 1890s to the early 1900s, dozens of sheet music publishers had offices on this block. Before recorded music became widespread, the music business ran on sheet music. Families bought printed music to play on their parlor pianos, and a hit song could sell millions of copies. Publishers employed songwriters to write hits and song pluggers, piano players who demonstrated songs for customers, vaudeville performers, and bandleaders.

According to legend, the name Tin Pan Alley came from a journalist who wrote that the sound of dozens of pianos playing different songs at once, through open windows, sounded like people banging on tin pans.

The songs that came out of Tin Pan Alley shaped American culture. Composers and lyricists associated with this world include Irving Berlin, who wrote Alexander's Ragtime Band, God Bless America, and White Christmas; George Gershwin, who worked as a song plugger as a teenager; Scott Joplin, who lived in New York for a time; and many others. Songs like Take Me Out to the Ball Game and Give My Regards to Broadway came from this era and this industry.

Tin Pan Alley was also a place where Black composers and performers, Jewish immigrants, and others found a way into American popular culture, often against the odds. African American composers like James Weldon Johnson, his brother J. Rosamond Johnson, and Bob Cole wrote hit songs for the Tin Pan Alley publishers.

The music business followed the theaters uptown, toward Times Square, and later to the Brill Building at Broadway and 49th Street, where songwriters wrote many of the pop hits of the 1950s and 60s. In 2019, several buildings on this block were designated as New York City landmarks, after preservationists fought to save them.

Look around, too, at the flower shops nearby. Sixth Avenue and 28th Street is the heart of the city's Flower District, where wholesalers sell flowers and plants to florists. Early in the morning, the sidewalks are filled with greenery.

As you leave, imagine the sound of dozens of pianos drifting out of these windows, each one playing a different song, all hoping to be the next big hit.
