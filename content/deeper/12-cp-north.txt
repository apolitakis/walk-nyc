=== STOP The Ramble
=== DEEPER The Gardener from Vienna | people
You heard that Olmsted called the Ramble a wild garden, and that its rocks and paths were all placed. Olmsted set the idea. The man who chose and planted much of what grew here was the park's chief gardener, Ignaz Pilat.

Pilat was born in Austria in 1820 and trained as a gardener in Vienna's botanical gardens. The upheavals of 1848, when revolutions swept across Europe, sent him abroad, and he ended up in America. By the late 1850s he was working on the new park under Olmsted, and he rose to be its chief gardener, in charge of the groves, the shrubs, the vines, and the flower beds. The planting design of the Ramble is credited to him.

In 1863, Olmsted crossed the Isthmus of Panama by train, on his way to a job in California. He was dazzled by the jungle passing his window, by forests draped in vines, and he sent Pilat a long letter about it, full of ideas for the park. He wanted something of that mystery and abundance here, made with plants tough enough for a New York winter. You can feel the idea around you: shrubs layered under trees, vines over rocks, and paths that vanish around a bend.

Pilat didn't live to see the park grow up. He died of tuberculosis, then called consumption, at his home in New York in 1870, at about fifty. Woods take decades to fill in, and what you're walking through is the result he and Olmsted were after: a garden designed so carefully that it seems no one designed it at all.
=== DEEPER Flaco's Year | nature
You heard that a Eurasian eagle-owl named Flaco lived free in the park for a year. Here's how that year began, and what his death revealed.

Eagle-owls are among the largest owls in the world, with orange eyes, feathered ear tufts, and wings that can stretch close to six feet. Flaco had lived for more than a decade in an enclosure at the Central Park Zoo. On February 2, 2023, someone cut the steel mesh of his exhibit, and he flew out.

Zoo staff worried that he would starve, since he had never hunted for himself, and they tried to lure him back. But people soon watched him catch and eat the park's rats, and the zoo eventually stopped trying to recapture him. He became a celebrity. Birders and photographers followed him through the park, and later through the streets, where he perched on water towers and fire escapes and hooted into the night.

On February 23, 2024, Flaco flew into a building on West 89th Street and died. The final report on his death found more than a crash. He had a severe infection of pigeon herpesvirus, picked up from eating the city's feral pigeons. And his body held four different rat poisons, the kinds commonly used across New York. The zoo said the illness and the poisons would likely have killed him anyway, and may be why he hit the building.

Those poisons work by stopping blood from clotting, and they can build up in any hawk or owl that eats a poisoned rat. Flaco escaped his cage, but not the city's food chain.
=== DEEPER The Birder in the Ramble | culture
You heard that birders crowd the Ramble every spring. On the morning of May 25, 2020, one of them, Christian Cooper, was out here when he saw a dog running loose. In the Ramble, dogs must always be on a leash, to protect the birds and the plants.

Cooper, a birder since childhood and a former editor at Marvel Comics, asked the dog's owner, a white woman, to leash it. She refused. He took out some dog treats, a tactic he used to get owners to leash their dogs, and started recording on his phone. She told him she was going to call the police and say that an African American man was threatening her life. Then she did.

His video, shared online that day, was watched by millions. It was the same day George Floyd was killed by a police officer in Minneapolis, and the two videos became part of the same national reckoning. Within days, a group of young Black scientists organized the first Black Birders Week, to celebrate Black people who study and love the outdoors, and to talk honestly about the dangers they can face there.

Cooper became the most famous birder in America almost overnight. In 2023 he published a memoir, Better Living Through Birding, and began hosting a National Geographic television series, Extraordinary Birder.

So when you see someone here with binoculars, waiting for a warbler, remember that the Ramble's quiet belongs to everyone.
=== STOP Belvedere Castle & Turtle Pond
=== DEEPER The Weather on the Castle | history
You heard that the official weather for Central Park is measured here. The park's weather record is one of the longest in the country, and it didn't begin at the castle.

In 1869, New York State authorized a weather observatory in Central Park. For its first half century it was based at the Arsenal, near Fifth Avenue and 64th Street, which you may have passed on the southern park tour, with its instruments on the roof. In 1919, as you heard, the Weather Bureau moved into the castle, and from the start of 1920 this was the official site.

The instruments didn't stay put. As the trees grew, they were shifted around the grounds to keep them in the open, and today they sit in a fenced enclosure nearby, screened by trees. Some meteorologists grumble that the leaves shade the sensors and block the breeze, so the park can read differently from the airports. But the park's record is prized for its length: it lets you compare a hot afternoon this summer with one in the 1870s.

Nearly everything is automated now, with one exception. When it snows, a person still goes out to a flat white board set on the ground, measures the snow with a ruler, and wipes the board clean to start again. That job has passed through several hands over the years. Since 2015, it has belonged to the staff of the Central Park Conservancy. So when the news says Central Park got six inches, someone in boots probably measured it by hand.
=== DEEPER The Rock Beneath the Castle | underfoot
You heard that the castle sits on Vista Rock. Look down at the rock itself, wherever it breaks through the ground around you.

This is Manhattan schist, the bedrock of much of the island. It began as mud and sand on the floor of an ancient sea. Roughly four hundred fifty million years ago, as continents collided and pushed up a range of mountains, those layers were buried, squeezed, and heated until their minerals grew into new crystals. Geologists call rock like this metamorphic, which means changed in form.

Look closely and you can see the change. The rock glitters, because it's full of mica, a mineral that forms thin, flat flakes, like tiny shards of a broken mirror. In places you may spot small dark red specks. Those are garnets. And notice that the rock has a grain, a layering, often tilted at a steep angle. That's the squeezing, frozen in place.

Now look at the tops of the outcrops. In spots they're smooth, almost polished, and scored with long, straight scratches. Those were made by ice. In the last ice age, tens of thousands of years ago, a great ice sheet covered this whole region. As it ground slowly south, stones frozen into its base worked like sandpaper and chisels on the bedrock.

The ice left things behind, too. Scattered around the park are boulders that don't match the ground they sit on, carried here by the ice and dropped when it melted. Geologists call them erratics.

Olmsted and Vaux didn't blast this away. They made it part of the scenery, and set the castle on top of it, so it seems to grow from the rock.
=== DEEPER The Cottage from Sweden | culture
A short walk west of the castle, near 79th Street, stands a small wooden building with a steep roof that looks as if it wandered in from a Scandinavian village. In a way, it did.

The Swedish Cottage was built in Sweden in 1875 as a model schoolhouse, shipped across the Atlantic, and put up at the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia in 1876, the world's fair for the hundredth birthday of American independence. When the fair closed, the city of New York bought it, and in 1877 it was taken apart again and rebuilt here. Accounts differ on how much Olmsted himself had to do with that.

For decades, the park used it for whatever was needed, including as a tool shed. Then, in 1947, it found its calling. The Parks Department had a traveling marionette troupe that put on puppet shows around the city, and that year the troupe moved into the cottage. In the 1970s, it was remodeled with a permanent stage.

Generations of New York children have seen their first play here, watching marionettes act out fairy tales on strings. It's said to be one of the last public marionette theaters in the country.

It makes a nice pair with the Delacorte, a short walk away. Two theaters share this corner of the park: one for Shakespeare under the stars, and one for the park's smallest actors and its youngest audiences.
=== STOP The Great Lawn
=== DEEPER The Concert in Central Park | culture
You heard that Simon and Garfunkel reunited here in September 1981. It's worth knowing how unlikely that was.

Paul Simon and Art Garfunkel had split up more than a decade earlier, after Bridge Over Troubled Water, and had rarely performed together since. The show on September 19, 1981, was a free benefit to help pay for the restoration of the park, which was badly run down. The two men expected a modest crowd. About half a million people came, and the concert was filmed and recorded for a live album.

The most charged moment didn't make the album. Nine months earlier, John Lennon had been shot outside the Dakota, on the edge of the park, as you may have heard at Strawberry Fields. Simon had written a new song, The Late Great Johnny Ace, about the death of a 1950s rhythm and blues singer and about the night he heard that Lennon had died. He sang it here. In the middle of the song, a man climbed onto the stage and rushed toward him. Security pulled the man away before he reached Simon, and Simon kept singing. The song was left off the record, though you can see the moment in the concert film, and a studio version came out two years later.

The reunion didn't last. The two toured together and began a new album, but it was finished as a Paul Simon solo record. Still, for one night, half a million people sat on this lawn and sang along, and the night became part of the story of the park's comeback.
=== DEEPER A Million for Disarmament | politics
You heard that about a million people gathered here in June 1982 to protest nuclear weapons. It's widely called the largest antinuclear demonstration in American history.

The date, June 12, 1982, was chosen to coincide with a special session of the United Nations General Assembly on disarmament, meeting a few miles away on the East River. The early 1980s were a frightening time. The Cold War had turned icy again, both superpowers were building new weapons, and a grassroots campaign was calling for a freeze: a halt by both sides to testing, building, and deploying nuclear weapons.

Marchers filled the streets of Midtown on their way to the park. By mid-afternoon, police estimated the crowd at more than seven hundred fifty thousand, and by the end of the day, about a million. They came from across the country and from overseas.

On the stage, Bruce Springsteen sang with Jackson Browne, and James Taylor, Joan Baez, Linda Ronstadt, and Peter, Paul and Mary performed.

Did it work? The freeze itself never became policy. But within a few years, Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev were negotiating the first treaty to actually eliminate a whole class of nuclear weapons. Historians still argue over how much credit the marchers deserve. But for one day, this lawn, packed from edge to edge, showed how many people were afraid, and how many wanted something done.
=== DEEPER Diana Ross in the Rain | people
You heard that the Great Lawn drew enormous crowds for free concerts. One of the most remembered took place in a downpour.

On July 21, 1983, Diana Ross, by then more than a decade into her solo career and one of the biggest stars in the world, gave a free concert here, which was being filmed for television. Less than half an hour in, a violent storm swept over the park. Ross stayed on stage, soaked through, and kept singing, then urged the vast crowd to leave slowly and calmly so that no one would be hurt.

She came back the very next evening, at six o'clock, for an unplanned second show.

The concerts were supposed to raise money for a playground in the park. Instead, the costs of putting on a second show, and the lawsuits that followed, swallowed the profits. In the end, Ross handed Mayor Ed Koch a check for two hundred fifty thousand dollars.

The playground got built. Ground was broken in 1986, at the west edge of the park near 81st Street, not far from here, and it's called the Diana Ross Playground. If you pass it, and hear children shrieking on the slides, that's partly thanks to a singer who refused to leave the stage in a thunderstorm.
=== STOP Cleopatra's Needle
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Two Pharaohs on One Stone | look
You heard that the obelisk was carved for Thutmose the Third. Look at the face where the hieroglyphics are clearest, and you'll find that he wasn't the only pharaoh to sign it.

The obelisk is a single piece of red granite, quarried far up the Nile at Aswan. Down the center of each face runs one column of hieroglyphics for Thutmose. About two centuries later, Ramesses the Second, the most self-promoting of all the pharaohs, had two more columns carved on each face, one on either side of the original, celebrating his own military victories. So the stone carries two kings' names, cut by different hands, generations apart.

Look for the ovals called cartouches, which frame a royal name. Look for birds, including the falcon, the symbol of the god Horus and of kingship.

On some faces, the carving is almost gone. In January 2011, Zahi Hawass, then Egypt's chief of antiquities, wrote to the mayor and the Conservancy that the obelisk had weathered badly. If New York could not care for it properly, he wrote, he would take the necessary steps to bring it home. City officials disputed that it was being neglected, and experts who studied it found no sign of significant ongoing erosion. Hawass had sent a similar complaint a few years before.

Still, in May 2014, conservators began a careful cleaning. Before they started, photographers from the Met documented the stone in detail from a lift. As one official put it at the time, the obelisk was clearly not falling apart, but it could definitely use a good cleaning.
=== DEEPER The Box Beneath the Obelisk | legends
You heard that the obelisk's cornerstone was laid in a Masonic ceremony. The Masons had a particular reason to care.

When Henry Gorringe lifted the obelisk from its base in Alexandria, he reported finding objects in the foundation that seemed to him clearly Masonic: a rough altar of red granite, a polished cube, a square, and an iron trowel. A committee of Masons in Egypt, including Egyptologists, reportedly agreed that the emblems were Masonic. Others were doubtful. Albert Pike, a leading American Freemason, reportedly said they bore no resemblance to Freemasonry at all. Whether the obelisk's builders had anything to do with the Masons is a legend, not history. But when the obelisk was raised here, the emblems were set back exactly as they had been found in Alexandria.

The cornerstone ritual was led by the Grand Master of New York's Masons, Jesse B. Anthony. Inside the stone, they sealed a time capsule: a copy of the 1870 census, a Bible, Webster's Dictionary, the complete works of Shakespeare, a guide to Egypt, and a copy of the Declaration of Independence. And one more thing, a small box placed by one of the men who arranged for the obelisk to come to New York, whose contents were never made public.

No one has opened it since, and the Conservancy has said it has no plans to. So somewhere beneath your feet is a box that, as far as anyone knows, no one living has seen inside. What's in it is anyone's guess.
=== DEEPER The Twin in London | history
You heard that a similar obelisk went to London. It's this one's twin. The two stood as a pair, first at Heliopolis, then in Alexandria. Paris has an obelisk too, but it came from a different temple, at Luxor.

The London obelisk had a far harder journey. It had toppled long ago and lay half buried in the sand. Egypt's ruler gave it to Britain in 1819, but for nearly sixty years no one wanted to pay to move it. When the British finally did, they sealed it inside an iron cylinder, just big enough to hold it, with a small deck, a mast, and a cabin on top. The strange vessel was named Cleopatra, and a steamship called the Olga was to tow it to England.

They left Alexandria in September 1877. On October 14, in a storm in the Bay of Biscay, the cylinder began to roll out of control. The Olga sent six volunteers in a small boat to rescue the Cleopatra's crew. The boat was swamped, and all six drowned. The Olga's captain finally brought his ship alongside and took off the Cleopatra's captain and five crewmen, and the cylinder was left to the sea.

It didn't sink. It was found drifting, towed into a Spanish port, and eventually brought to London, where the obelisk was raised beside the Thames, on the Victoria Embankment, in 1878. The names of the six sailors who died are remembered on a plaque at its base, and its bronze sphinxes still carry shrapnel scars from a German air raid in the First World War.

This obelisk, as you heard, made its own crossing three years later, tucked in the hold of a steamship.
=== STOP Seneca Village
=== DEEPER The Wilsons of All Angels' | people
You heard that Seneca Village had three churches. One of them was All Angels' Church, an Episcopal congregation where Black and white New Yorkers worshipped together.

Its porter and sexton, the man who looked after the building and grounds, was William Godfrey Wilson. When a state census taker came through in 1855, Wilson was forty-one. He lived next to the church with his wife, Charlotte, and their eight children, in a three-story wood-frame house with a stone foundation and a brick chimney. That isn't a shanty. It's a substantial family home.

When archaeologists dug here in 2011, they went looking for two houses they had found in the archives, and the Wilson house was among the most important. They found pieces of its metal roofing, kitchen utensils, scraps of clothing, and a stoneware beer bottle.

Why did so much survive? Because when the city cleared the village, its crews took away what stood above the ground, often salvaging the wood, and left what lay below. Foundations, cellars, and whatever had fallen into them stayed where they were, under the new park's soil, for a century and a half. The findings were published in 2018, in a report called Seneca Village, A Forgotten Community.

Remember the Wilsons the next time you hear the village described as a cluster of shacks. The record says something else: a father with a steady job at his church, a big family, and a three-story house with a brick chimney, on land the city decided it needed more.
=== DEEPER Four Thousand Dollars | politics
You heard that residents were compensated for their land, though many said the amounts were too low. One document shows what that looked like.

Andrew Williams, the young shoeshiner who bought the village's first lots in 1825, had paid one hundred twenty-five dollars for his land. Three decades later, he had a house on it. In 1856, as the city's commissioners set the prices for the land they were taking, Williams swore out an affidavit. He valued his house and lots at four thousand dollars. He had been offered thirty-five hundred, he said, and he had refused. The document survives in the city's Municipal Archives.

It's a small piece of paper, but it says a lot. Williams wasn't a squatter pleading to stay. He was a property owner, arguing over a price, using the legal tools available to him.

And remember the two hundred fifty dollar rule that applied only to Black voters. A man with four thousand dollars of property had cleared it many times over. When the city took the land, it took the property that qualified him. Unless he bought property somewhere else, he could lose his vote along with his home.
=== DEEPER The Village That Survived | history
You heard that Seneca Village was one of the first communities of Black property owners in New York. Across the East River, a similar community did better.

In the 1830s, on farmland at the eastern edge of Brooklyn, a free Black man named James Weeks bought land, and the settlement that grew around it took his name: Weeksville. Like Seneca Village, it was a place where Black families could own property, and with it, for Black men, the right to vote. It grew much larger than Seneca Village, with its own churches and schools. During the Draft Riots of 1863, when mobs attacked Black New Yorkers in Manhattan and burned the Colored Orphan Asylum you may have heard about on the Midtown tour, some families fled to Weeksville for safety.

But Weeksville, too, was nearly forgotten, as Brooklyn's streets grew over it. Then, in 1968, a researcher named James Hurley and a pilot named Joseph Haynes flew over the neighborhood, looking for traces of the old settlement. From the air, they spotted a few old wooden houses that stood at an odd angle to the modern streets, because they had been built along an old country lane called Hunterfly Road.

Neighbors, led by a woman named Joan Maynard, organized to save them. The houses became city landmarks, and in 1973, the community's society bought them. Today they're the heart of the Weeksville Heritage Center in Crown Heights. If Seneca Village makes you wonder what was lost here, Weeksville shows you what might have been.
=== STOP The Reservoir
=== DEEPER The Marathon Began Here | culture
You heard that the track around the Reservoir is one of the most famous running paths in the world. The world's biggest marathon began a short distance from here.

On September 13, 1970, the first New York City Marathon was run entirely inside Central Park, around and around the park's loop drive. The organizers, Fred Lebow and Vince Chiappetta, had a budget of about a thousand dollars. The entry fee was one dollar. A hundred twenty-seven runners started, and fifty-five finished. One woman entered, and she had to drop out. The winner, a city firefighter named Gary Muhrcke, crossed the line in about two hours and thirty-one minutes.

Lebow was a Romanian-born immigrant who worked in the garment industry, and he had a showman's instincts. In 1976, for the nation's bicentennial, the race left the park and ran through all five boroughs. It became a sensation. Today it has more than fifty thousand finishers, and it still ends here in Central Park.

In 1992, ill with brain cancer, Lebow ran the course himself, alongside Grete Waitz of Norway, who won the women's race here nine times. He finished in about five and a half hours. He died two years later.

Look for his statue at Engineers' Gate, the park entrance at Fifth Avenue and 90th Street, just east of the Reservoir. He's in running clothes, checking his watch, as if timing every runner who passes.
=== DEEPER The Tunnels Below | underfoot
You heard that the Reservoir was retired in 1993, after a newer system of water tunnels made it obsolete. One of those tunnels passes deep beneath this part of the park.

New York's drinking water comes mostly from reservoirs upstate, and it reaches the city through enormous tunnels bored through solid rock, hundreds of feet down. City Tunnel Number One opened in 1917, and Number Two in 1936. For decades, neither could be shut down for inspection, because there was no backup.

So in 1970, the city began City Water Tunnel Number Three. Its first stage runs about thirteen miles, from Hillview Reservoir in Yonkers, under the Bronx, under Manhattan and beneath Central Park, then under the East River and Roosevelt Island into Queens. It was put into service in 1998, at a cost of about a billion dollars. The new tunnel was built partly so that the two old ones could, at last, be shut down and repaired. Later stages have followed, and the tunnel is still not finished.

The digging was done by sandhogs, the same trade you may have heard about at the Holland Tunnel, with drills and dynamite and, later, giant boring machines. Twenty-four people have died on the project since 1970, most of them sandhogs.

So as you walk around the Reservoir, the water in front of you is just for looking at. The water the city drinks is moving far below your feet.
=== DEEPER The Riders' Path | history
You may have heard on the southern park tour that Olmsted and Vaux gave carriages, riders, and walkers separate paths. The Reservoir is circled by one of those riding paths, a soft dirt bridle path just outside the runners' track.

Before the automobile, horses were everywhere in New York, and stables were as common as garages are today. For more than a century, a stable a couple of blocks west of the park, at 175 West 89th Street, sent horses out onto these paths. The building, designed by Frank A. Rooke, went up in 1892 as a stable, and in time became the Claremont Riding Academy, where generations of New Yorkers learned to ride. Riders led their horses out of the building, down the street and across the avenues, into the park.

Claremont was the oldest continuously operated stable in New York City and the last public stable in Manhattan. Rising costs and fewer riders finally closed it, at five in the afternoon on April 29, 2007. The building, on the National Register of Historic Places, now belongs to a school.

The bridle path is still here, and it belongs mostly to runners now, who like its soft surface. But it was built for hooves, and for a hundred years, the clatter of horses on 89th Street was as much a part of this neighborhood as the joggers are today.
=== STOP The North Woods & the Blockhouse
=== DEEPER The Gate of the Exonerated | politics
As you may have heard at the Pond, the park's entrances were named in 1862 for the people of the city: artisans, scholars, mariners, and more. In 2022, for the first time since then, a new name was added, on 110th Street between Malcolm X Boulevard and Fifth Avenue: the Gate of the Exonerated.

On April 19, 1989, a young woman jogging in the park was attacked and raped near the 102nd Street Cross Drive. Five teenagers from Harlem, Black and Latino, aged fourteen to sixteen, were arrested: Antron McCray, Kevin Richardson, Yusef Salaam, Raymond Santana, and Korey Wise. After hours of police questioning, they gave confessions that they soon took back, confessions that didn't match one another or the evidence. Much of the press treated them as guilty from the start, and Donald Trump, then a real estate developer, bought full-page newspaper ads calling for the death penalty. All five were convicted in 1990 and spent between about six and thirteen years in prison.

In 2002, Matias Reyes, a man already imprisoned for other crimes, confessed that he alone had committed the attack, and his DNA matched the evidence. That December, the five men's convictions were thrown out. In 2014, the city settled their lawsuit for forty-one million dollars, and in 2023, Yusef Salaam was elected to the City Council, representing Harlem.

The gate was dedicated on December 19, 2022, twenty years to the day after the convictions were vacated. It honors the five, and everyone who has been wrongly convicted.
=== DEEPER The Nuns at McGown's Pass | history
You heard about McGown's Pass Tavern, on the hill near the park's eastern edge. Before the park, that hill had another life, as a convent and a school.

In the 1840s, the Sisters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul acquired the property and founded a school for girls there, the Academy of Mount Saint Vincent. When the park took the land, the Sisters moved north to an estate in the Bronx overlooking the Hudson, called Font Hill, where their college stands to this day.

Their old buildings stayed behind. During the Civil War, from 1862 to 1865, they served as a military hospital for Union soldiers. Mother Mary Jerome Ely sent fifteen sisters back to nurse the wounded, and the place was known as Saint Joseph's Military Hospital.

After the war, the old convent became a restaurant and hotel, and kept the Mount Saint Vincent name. After a fire in 1881, it was rebuilt. The Sisters, it's said, didn't want their name over a place they considered unsanctified, so the new building was named after the old tavern of the Revolutionary era: McGown's Pass Tavern.

So the name you heard at this stop has layers. A Revolutionary-era tavern gave its name to a pass and a hill, the hill held a convent, the convent became a hospital, the hospital became a hotel, and the hotel took the tavern's name again.
=== DEEPER The Stream That Was Here First | nature
You heard that the Loch's water comes from the city supply, and that the stream is artificial. But it follows a real one.

Before the park, a natural brook ran through this area, called Montayne's Rivulet. Its name is spelled several ways in old records. It was one of the very few natural streams on the land that became Central Park.

Olmsted and Vaux dammed and reshaped it, sending its water northeast through a chain of water features. First comes the Pool, near 100th Street, then the Loch, through the ravine, and finally the Harlem Meer. They were built between 1862 and 1865, with dams and weirs. Before the park, the low ground where the Meer is now was fed by more than one stream, including Harlem Creek, flowing down from the north. This whole corner wasn't in the original park plan at all. The park first ended at 106th Street, and the land up to 110th Street was added in 1863.

Outside the park, the old stream was buried in sewers as the city grew over it. And for decades, the pool and rink built in the 1960s at the foot of the ravine cut off the natural link between the Loch and the Meer. The new complex you'll see at the next stop reopens that link, so water can run from the ravine to the lake again, as the designers intended.
=== STOP Harlem Meer & the Conservatory Garden
=== DEEPER The Duke at the Circle | culture
Before you leave, walk to the plaza at the park's northeast corner, at Fifth Avenue and 110th Street. Here stands a monument to Duke Ellington, who, as you'll hear on the Harlem tour, became famous playing at the Cotton Club.

The memorial was the idea of Bobby Short, the cabaret singer and pianist who for decades played the Café Carlyle on the Upper East Side. Short conceived it in 1979, did the fundraising himself, and chose the sculptor, Robert Graham, a Mexican-born artist based in Los Angeles, whose other works include the giant bronze fist in Detroit honoring the boxer Joe Louis. The memorial took nearly twenty years. Ellington had died in 1974. Short lived to see it finished, and died in 2005. When it was dedicated, on July 1, 1997, it was the first statue honoring Ellington anywhere in the United States, and it was hailed at the time as the city's first monument to a Black artist.

Look up. It's about twenty-five feet tall. At the top, Ellington stands beside a grand piano. They're held up by three columns, and on top of each column stand three nude women, nine in all: the Muses of Greek myth, goddesses of the arts, balancing the piano and its player on their heads. It's a bold, slightly startling piece, as unconventional as Ellington's music was in its day.

The circle itself is now called Duke Ellington Circle. It sits where Central Park, Fifth Avenue, Harlem, and East Harlem all meet, which suits a composer who wrote for every corner of the city. And a few blocks south, across Fifth Avenue, are the Museum of the City of New York and El Museo del Barrio, at the northern end of Museum Mile.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Dancers and the Children | look
You heard that the Conservatory Garden's north garden has a fountain with three dancing maidens, and the south garden a statue of two children. Both have stories.

The dancers are a bronze cast of Three Dancing Maidens, by the German sculptor Walter Schott, completed before 1910. They came from Yonkers, just north of the city. The family of Samuel Untermyer, the lawyer who famously questioned J. P. Morgan before Congress in 1912 about the power of the big banks, bought the piece from Schott's workshop and set it in the gardens of their estate overlooking the Hudson, gardens that are now a public park. The sculpture came to Central Park and was placed here in the late 1940s. Walk around it, and watch the three girls lean back as they dance in a ring, their dresses swirling as if caught in mid-turn.

Then find the small pool in the south garden with its two bronze children. This is the Burnett Fountain, by the American sculptor Bessie Potter Vonnoh, a memorial to Frances Hodgson Burnett, the author of The Secret Garden. The children are Mary and Dickon, from the novel. In the book, Dickon charms wild animals by playing a pipe, and the statue shows him playing one.

The Secret Garden is a story about a neglected, walled garden that two children bring back to life. That's worth keeping in mind here, because this garden, too, was once neglected and forgotten.
=== DEEPER The Gardener Who Brought It Back | people
You heard that the Conservatory Garden was restored in the 1980s. The person who led that work was Lynden Miller.

In 1982, the garden had long been neglected and was battered by vandalism. Miller took on its rescue for the young Central Park Conservancy, and it took five years of replanting, rebuilding, and fundraising. The work was completed in 1987.

Her argument, which she made for the rest of her career, was that beautiful, well-tended plantings change how people feel about a public place. A garden that looks cared for draws people in, and a park full of people feels safer. She later laid out the case in a book, Parks, Plants, and People.

She went on to put that idea to work across the city. She designed the gardens of Bryant Park when it reopened in 1992, after the rescue you may have heard about on the Midtown tour. She also worked at the New York Botanical Garden, Battery Park City, Hudson River Park, Fort Tryon Park, the planted mall on Park Avenue at 97th Street, and the British Garden in Hanover Square downtown.

The Conservatory Garden has since had another major restoration, completed in 2025. If you come in late April or early May, walk the two long rows of crabapple trees that line the central lawn, when they're in full pink and white bloom. Much of what you see began with a decision, more than forty years ago, that a forgotten garden at the top of the park deserved the same care as anything at its famous southern end.
