=== STOP Rockefeller Center
=== DEEPER The Garden Under the Plaza | nature
You heard that John D. Rockefeller Junior leased this land from Columbia University. How did a university come to own a slice of Midtown? The answer is a garden.

In 1801, when this was open country far north of the city, a doctor named David Hosack bought about twenty acres here and planted the Elgin Botanic Garden, the first public botanical garden in the United States. Hosack taught botany and medicine, and he's remembered for another reason too: he was the doctor who attended Alexander Hamilton after his duel with Aaron Burr in 1804. At Elgin, he grew a great variety of plants, native and foreign, with greenhouses for tender species, so that medical students could learn to recognize the plants their medicines came from.

The garden was expensive, and Hosack couldn't keep paying for it. In 1810, he sold it to New York State. In 1814, the state handed the land to Columbia, in place of money it had promised the college. The garden faded, and as the city grew north, the land became valuable beyond anyone's imagining. By the 1920s, it was covered with brownstones, and when the opera plan came along, Columbia was the landlord.

Columbia kept the land through the building of Rockefeller Center and for half a century afterward. In 1985, it finally sold the ground under the center for four hundred million dollars.

So the next time you walk among the flower beds of the Channel Gardens, remember that long before there was a Rockefeller Center, this ground was a garden.
=== DEEPER Who Were the Men on the Beam? | legends
You heard about Lunch atop a Skyscraper, the photograph of eleven ironworkers eating lunch on a beam high above the city. It's one of the most famous pictures ever taken in New York, and almost everything about it is a mystery.

Start with how it happened. The usual date is September 20th, 1932, when the building's frame was nearing the top. The beam was real, and so was the drop, but the moment is believed to have been arranged. Rockefeller Center was trying to rent office space in the depths of the Depression, and it ran its own publicity operation, with photographers on hand to show off the project.

Then, who took it. The picture is most often credited to Charles Ebbets, who did publicity photography for the center. But other photographers were up on the steel that day too, and nobody can say for certain whose shutter clicked.

And who's in it. Over the years, many families have looked at those faces and recognized a father, an uncle or a grandfather. In 2012, an Irish documentary followed claims that two of them came from the west of Ireland, and Native American families have claimed others. Most of the eleven have never been identified for certain, and that may be part of the picture's power. They aren't famous men. They're the men who built the city.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Wisdom and the Channel | look
Stand in front of 30 Rockefeller Plaza and look up over the main entrance. The bearded figure leaning out of the clouds, holding a giant compass, is Wisdom, carved by the sculptor Lee Lawrie, who also made the Atlas on Fifth Avenue. He's measuring out the universe, and the panels on either side, called Light and Sound, suit a building made for radio. Below him runs a line from the book of Isaiah: Wisdom and knowledge shall be the stability of thy times. It was a hopeful thing to carve over a door in the depths of the Depression.

Now turn toward Fifth Avenue and walk through the gardens that slope down to the plaza. They're called the Channel Gardens, and the name is a little joke. On one side stands La Maison Française, built for French businesses, and on the other, the British Empire Building. Between France and Britain lies a channel, and so does this one. Look above their doors for the gilded reliefs celebrating each country.

The center was built with an art program unlike any other commercial development of its time. Dozens of artists made murals, mosaics, sculpture and metalwork for its buildings. Look up as you walk, at the doorways, the lobbies and the tops of the walls, and you'll find gods, workers, airplanes, radio waves and planets, a whole Depression-era vision of the modern world in stone and metal.
=== DEEPER The Showplace of the Nation | culture
You heard that Radio City Music Hall opened in 1932 as the largest indoor theater in the world. It was the dream of one showman, Samuel Rothafel, known to everyone as Roxy.

Roxy had made his name running grand movie palaces, and he promised this one would top them all. It opened on December 27th, 1932, with an enormous stage show, a parade of acts that included the dancer Martha Graham and the comic dancer Ray Bolger, later the Scarecrow in The Wizard of Oz. More than a hundred thousand people asked for tickets to the opening, for a hall with about six thousand seats. Within two weeks, the hall was showing movies too, and for more than four decades, it paired first-run films with lavish stage shows.

Its most famous performers came from Saint Louis. In 1925, a choreographer named Russell Markert put together a precision dance troupe called the Missouri Rockets, every dancer kicking in perfect unison. Roxy brought them to New York, where they became the Roxyettes, and when he opened the Music Hall, he brought them with him. In 1934, they became the Rockettes. Their eye-high kicks in a long straight line, and their Christmas show, have made them one of the city's great traditions.

Step inside if you can, to see the great auditorium with its sunburst of arches, and the Art Deco lounges and furniture by the designer Donald Deskey. In 1978, when the hall was in danger of closing, a public campaign saved it, and its interior was named a city landmark.
=== STOP St. Patrick's Cathedral
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Red Hats | look
Go inside, and look for something hanging high overhead: wide, flat red hats with long tassels. They're galeros, the hats once given to cardinals of the Catholic Church, and each one here belonged to a cardinal archbishop of New York.

The tradition goes back centuries. When a cardinal died, his red hat was hung from the ceiling of his cathedral, above his tomb, and left there. It was said that it would hang until it rotted and fell, a reminder that all earthly glory passes away. In the late 1960s, as part of a reform of the church's ceremonies, the Vatican stopped giving out the galero altogether.

Four hang here. The oldest belonged to John McCloskey, who in 1875 became the first American named a cardinal. The others belonged to his successors John Farley, Patrick Hayes and Francis Spellman, the powerful archbishop who led New York's Catholics from 1939 to 1967. During the cathedral's restoration in the 2010s, the old silk hats were taken down, carefully conserved and hung back up, which isn't quite the tradition, but saved them from falling to dust.

Beneath the altar, in the crypt you heard about, these cardinals lie with other archbishops of New York. So when you look up at the hats, you're looking at the cathedral's own history: an immigrant church that built this place, and the men who led it as it rose to power in the city.
=== DEEPER A Ceiling That Was Meant to Be Temporary | architecture
You heard that construction began in 1858. The cathedral didn't open until 1879, with work halted for years during the Civil War, and money was short the whole way. The evidence is over your head.

James Renwick designed the cathedral in the manner of the great Gothic cathedrals of Europe, with pointed arches, ribbed vaults and soaring piers, all clad in white marble outside. In a true medieval cathedral, the vaulted ceiling is stone. Stone vaults are enormously heavy, and they push outward on the walls, which is why medieval builders added flying buttresses, the stone arms that brace the walls from outside. Renwick planned a masonry ceiling too. But the money wasn't there, so the vault you see overhead was built as a lighter, cheaper ceiling of plaster on wood and lath, meant as a stopgap.

The stopgap became permanent. And because a plaster vault weighs so little, the cathedral could do without the great flying buttresses that brace the stone vaults of Europe's cathedrals.

The restoration in the 2010s took on that old ceiling. Workers raised scaffolding to the vaults, repaired the cracks, and kept the plaster fixed to its original timber laths.

So St. Patrick's is a little like the city that built it: a grand European idea, put up by an immigrant community on a budget, and made to last anyway.
=== DEEPER The Bishop on Television | people
In the early 1950s, one of the most popular stars on American television was a Catholic bishop who preached here.

Fulton Sheen was an auxiliary bishop of New York, a gifted speaker with piercing eyes, a flowing cape and a showman's timing. In 1952, he began a weekly television program called Life Is Worth Living. There was no choir and no altar, just Sheen in a study with a blackboard, talking for half an hour about love, fear, communism and the meaning of suffering, with jokes thrown in. He was scheduled on Tuesday nights against the biggest star on television, the comedian Milton Berle, and few expected him to last. Instead, he drew millions of viewers of every faith, won an Emmy as television's most outstanding personality, and in accepting it, is said to have thanked his writers: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John.

Sheen later became bishop of Rochester, and then an archbishop. When he died in 1979, he was buried in the crypt beneath the altar here, among the archbishops of New York. But he had grown up in Peoria, Illinois, and the diocese there, which was leading the effort to have him declared a saint, wanted his body. After years of legal fighting, with his family taking Peoria's side, the Archdiocese of New York gave way, and in June 2019, his remains were moved to the cathedral in Peoria.

So for forty years, the crypt beneath this altar held one of the first stars of television.
=== STOP MoMA & Paley Park
=== DEEPER The Stork Club | culture
You heard that Paley Park stands on the site of the Stork Club. For more than three decades, it was the most famous nightclub in America.

It was run by Sherman Billingsley, an Oklahoman who had been a bootlegger, and it began as a speakeasy during Prohibition. In 1934, it moved to this block of 53rd Street and became the place where movie stars, socialites, politicians and writers all wanted to be seen. Getting in was the game. A doorman decided who passed the gold chain at the entrance, and the inner room, the Cub Room, had its own gatekeeper, nicknamed Saint Peter.

The club's most powerful regular was Walter Winchell, the gossip columnist and radio broadcaster, followed by tens of millions of Americans. He held court at Table 50 in the Cub Room, and a mention in his column could make or break a career. Billingsley and Winchell made each other famous.

Then, in October 1951, the singer and dancer Josephine Baker, an American star who lived in France, came in with friends, ordered dinner, and by her account waited about an hour for food that never came. She was sure she was being refused service because she was Black. Winchell had been in the room, and Baker publicly accused him of doing nothing. Winchell said he had left before the trouble began, and turned on her in his column. The NAACP picketed the club, and the quarrel played out in the newspapers for months.

The club's glamour faded in the years that followed. It closed in 1965, the building was torn down, and the site became the little park where you can sit today.
=== DEEPER The Fire at the Museum | history
On April 15th, 1958, the Museum of Modern Art caught fire.

Workers were renovating on the second floor, and a fire broke out among their materials, by the most common account from a workman's cigarette. Thick smoke poured up through the building. On the floor above hung a major exhibition of the paintings of Georges Seurat, including one of the most famous pictures in the world, A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, lent by the Art Institute of Chicago. Paintings were rushed to safety, and La Grande Jatte ended up next door in the Whitney Museum, which then stood beside the Modern. It came through undamaged, but it was the first time Chicago had ever lent the painting, and it hasn't traveled since.

One workman died, and more than two dozen people, many of them firefighters, were hurt. The greatest loss to the collection was a painting. In 1955, the museum had bought one of Claude Monet's huge late paintings of water lilies, about eighteen feet long. It was destroyed. A smaller Monet came out scorched, looking, as one writer later put it, like a toasted marshmallow. The museum's conservators worked on it for three years before declaring it beyond repair.

The museum didn't give up on Monet. Soon afterward, it bought another of his enormous water lily paintings in Paris, a triptych of three panels that together stretch more than forty feet. That's the painting you'll usually find in the galleries today, one of the most popular in the museum. If you stand in front of it, you're looking at the museum's answer to the fire.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Two Gardens | look
Two of the best outdoor rooms in Midtown are a block apart, and they're related.

The first is behind the museum: the Abby Aldrich Rockefeller Sculpture Garden, named for one of the museum's founders. It was designed by the architect Philip Johnson, who also ran the museum's architecture department, with the landscape architect James Fanning, and it opened in 1953. Johnson laid it out like an open-air room, paved in marble, with pools of water, trees, and sculptures set against the sky and the surrounding towers. In 1964, he enlarged it, working with the landscape firm Zion and Breen. If you go in, notice how the walls, the water and the trees make you forget the street outside.

The second is Paley Park, which opened three years later, designed by the same landscape firm, led by Robert Zion. You can feel the family resemblance: a small room cut off from the city, with water, a canopy of trees, and chairs you can move. Zion was a champion of what came to be called pocket parks, tiny parks tucked between buildings, and this one became the model for them.

What both gardens understand is that a city space doesn't have to be big. It needs walls to hold you, something to look at, the sound of water, shade, and a place to sit. Those are simple ingredients, and they're why these two small gardens are still full on a sunny day, more than half a century later.
=== STOP Park Avenue: Lever House & the Seagram Building
=== DEEPER No, No, No, No, No | people
You heard that Phyllis Lambert persuaded her father to hire Mies van der Rohe. Here's how she did it.

In 1954, Lambert was twenty-seven and living in Paris when she saw the design her father, Samuel Bronfman, the head of Seagram, had in mind for the company's new headquarters on Park Avenue, a conventional tower by a commercial firm. She was appalled. She sat down and wrote him an eight-page letter, and by her own account, it began with the words No, no, no, no, no. The rest was a passionate argument that a company's building is a public statement that will stand for generations, and that Seagram should build something great.

Her father did something unusual. Instead of brushing her off, he put her in charge of finding a new architect. She came back to New York and spent weeks meeting the leading architects of the day, with advice from Philip Johnson, and chose Mies, then in his late sixties and working in Chicago. Johnson joined him on the project, designing interiors including the Four Seasons restaurant.

Lambert didn't stop there. She went on to study architecture herself and became an architect, a preservationist and a fierce critic of careless development. In 1979, she founded the Canadian Centre for Architecture in Montreal, one of the world's great architecture museums.

The Seagram Building turned out to be what she had promised her father: a landmark copied around the world, and a reminder of what one stubborn letter can do.
=== DEEPER The Paintings That Never Hung | culture
The Four Seasons restaurant, inside the Seagram Building, was meant to be a complete work of modern design: Mies's building, Philip Johnson's rooms, and on the walls, paintings by one of the greatest living painters.

Around 1958, Mark Rothko accepted a commission to paint a series of large murals for the restaurant. He threw himself into the work, producing dozens of dark, brooding canvases in deep maroons and blacks. But he had doubts. He told a writer he met on a ship to Europe that he had taken the job with strictly malicious intentions, hoping to paint something that would ruin the appetite of every rich diner who ever ate in that room.

Then he went to dinner at the restaurant himself. By most accounts, the expensive, showy atmosphere confirmed his fears, and he decided that his paintings would never hang there. He withdrew from the commission and kept the murals. Years later, he gave nine of them to the Tate in London, on condition that they hang together in a room of their own. They arrived in London on the same day in 1970 that Rothko died in New York.

The restaurant got along without him. For decades, a different masterpiece hung in its corridor: a huge stage curtain painted by Pablo Picasso for a ballet, Le Tricorne. In 2014, after a dispute with the building's owner, the curtain was moved to the New-York Historical Society.

The Four Seasons left the building in 2016, and its famous rooms have since been taken over by new restaurants. But the story of the Rothkos is a reminder that some art refuses to be decoration.
=== DEEPER The Woman Behind the Glass | people
You heard that Lever House was designed by Gordon Bunshaft of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill. For decades, his was the only name most people knew. Working beside him was Natalie de Blois.

De Blois was one of very few women in American architecture when she joined the firm in the 1940s, and she rose to become one of its senior designers. On Lever House, she worked closely with Bunshaft, and colleagues and historians have since credited her with a large share of the design. But when the building opened in 1952 and the praise poured in, it went to Bunshaft and the firm. That was how big firms worked in those days, and it was doubly true for a woman.

She went on to help design other corporate towers of the era, including the Pepsi-Cola Building, a few blocks up Park Avenue, and, by most accounts, the Union Carbide Building, the tower at 270 Park Avenue you heard was torn down for JPMorgan Chase. Later she taught architecture in Texas and spoke out for women in the profession, and she has slowly been written back into the story.

Lever House itself had to fight to survive. Its low base and open courtyard used only a fraction of what the zoning allowed, which made it a tempting target for a bigger tower. Preservationists rallied, and in 1982, when the building was barely thirty years old, the city named it a landmark. It's still here, still green glass, still showing how much a building can give back to the street.
=== DEEPER Trees for the Sons | history
Look at the planted islands running down the middle of Park Avenue. They gave the avenue its name.

This was once Fourth Avenue, and for much of the nineteenth century, its northern stretch was a railroad right-of-way, with trains running through an open cut and tunnels. As the tracks were covered over, the avenue got landscaped medians on top, and with them a grander name: Park Avenue. Over time, the name spread south along the old Fourth Avenue, all the way down to Union Square, and the medians became some of the most carefully tended strips of green in the city, with tulips in spring.

Every December, the medians get something else: a long line of Christmas trees, lit for almost fifty blocks. The tradition began in 1945. After the Second World War, a group of Park Avenue mothers arranged for lighted fir trees along the avenue, in memory of the sons the war had taken. The trees came back the next year, and they've come back ever since.

Today, about a hundred and twenty trees are shipped down from Nova Scotia each year, and the lights are switched on at a ceremony at the Brick Presbyterian Church, uptown on Park Avenue. Most people walking past in December think they're just decorations. They began as a memorial.
=== STOP Gilded Age Fifth Avenue
=== DEEPER Alva's Ball | people
You heard that Vanderbilt family mansions lined Fifth Avenue for blocks south of here. The most famous party ever thrown in one of them was a social coup.

In the early 1880s, the Vanderbilts were among the richest people in America, but old New York society looked down on them as new money, descendants of a rough ferry captain. Society's gatekeeper was Caroline Astor, known simply as the Mrs. Astor, and her circle, the one later nicknamed the Four Hundred, as you may have heard at Gramercy Park, kept the Vanderbilts out.

Alva Vanderbilt, the wife of the Commodore's grandson William Kissam Vanderbilt, set out to change that. She had a new château built on Fifth Avenue at 52nd Street, designed by Richard Morris Hunt, and on March 26th, 1883, she threw a costume ball to show it off, for more than a thousand guests. Society talked of nothing else for weeks. Guests came as kings, queens and figures from history, and Alva's sister-in-law came dressed as Electric Light.

Now the story, as it's usually told, and it may have been polished. Mrs. Astor's daughter, Carrie, was dying to go, and had been rehearsing a dance for the ball with her friends. But no invitation came. Alva let it be known that she couldn't invite someone whose mother had never paid a call on her. So Mrs. Astor swallowed her pride, had her carriage driven to Alva's door, and left her calling card. The invitation arrived the next day.

Whether or not it happened just that way, the ball worked. The Vanderbilts were in. Alva's château is long gone, but her ambitions were just getting started.
=== DEEPER The Duke and the Dollar Princess | politics
A few blocks south, at Fifth Avenue and 53rd Street, stands Saint Thomas Church. In 1895, in the church that stood on that corner before the present one, Alva Vanderbilt married off her daughter.

Consuelo Vanderbilt was eighteen, and in love with an American. Alva had other plans. In the Gilded Age, rich American families married their daughters to European aristocrats: the families gained titles, and the aristocrats gained fortunes to prop up their estates. The brides were called dollar princesses. Alva chose the ninth Duke of Marlborough, who needed money for Blenheim Palace. Consuelo resisted, and by her own later account, her mother kept her shut away and pressured her until she gave in. On November 6th, 1895, she married the duke there, reportedly in tears, while crowds jammed Fifth Avenue to watch. Her father settled a fortune in railroad stock on the marriage.

The marriage was miserable. The couple separated and later divorced, and when the marriage came before a church court for annulment in 1926, Alva testified that she had forced her daughter into it.

Alva, meanwhile, had become a different person. Earlier in 1895, she had divorced William Vanderbilt, a scandal in itself, and she soon married Oliver Belmont. After his death in 1908, she poured her energy and money into the fight for women's votes. In 1909, she founded the Political Equality Association, and as you may have heard on the Lower East Side, she backed the striking shirtwaist makers that winter. Later, she bankrolled the most militant wing of the suffrage movement, the National Woman's Party, and served as its president.

The mother who forced her daughter into a loveless marriage ended her life as a champion of women's independence. History doesn't always run in a straight line.
=== DEEPER The Hope Diamond in the Mail | companies
Look for the Harry Winston salon, at Fifth Avenue and 56th Street. The jeweler who founded it once sent the most famous diamond in the world through the mail.

Harry Winston rose from his father's small jewelry shop to become known as the king of diamonds, trading in many of the world's great stones. In 1949, he bought the Hope Diamond, a deep blue stone of about forty-five carats, along with the rest of the jewels of the Washington socialite Evalyn Walsh McLean, its last private owner. For years, he took it on tour to charity events. Then he decided it belonged to the public, and gave it to the Smithsonian Institution.

How do you deliver a priceless diamond to Washington? Winston put it in a box, wrapped it in plain brown paper, and sent it by registered first-class mail. The postage came to two dollars and forty-four cents, plus a hundred and forty-two dollars and eighty-five cents to insure it for a million dollars. It rode to Washington in a mail car on the train, and a letter carrier delivered it to the museum on November 10th, 1958. The Smithsonian still has the wrapping paper, stamps and all.

The Hope Diamond comes with a legend: that it carries a curse. Its owners did include a French king who lost his head and a socialite whose family suffered terrible losses. But the curse story seems to have been largely spread by jewelers and newspapers to make the stone more intriguing, and it may have helped sell it. Winston owned it for nearly a decade and lived a long and prosperous life.

Today, the diamond is one of the most visited museum objects in the world, and it got there for the price of a postage stamp.

=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Woman Leading Sherman | look
You heard that the gilded statue of General Sherman, by Augustus Saint-Gaudens, is considered one of the finest equestrian statues in America. Look at the figure walking ahead of his horse.

She's Victory, striding forward with a palm branch in one hand and her other arm raised, wings spread, leading Sherman on. Saint-Gaudens worked on the monument for more than a decade. He had Sherman himself sit for a portrait bust in 1888, and the general's lean, weathered face came from those sittings. The horse was modeled by another sculptor, Alexander Phimister Proctor, a specialist in animals. Look down at its hooves: one of them treads on a branch of Georgia pine, a reminder of Sherman's march through Georgia to the sea.

For Victory, the sculptor's principal model was Hettie Anderson, a Black woman who was one of the most sought-after artists' models in New York in the 1890s. She posed for Saint-Gaudens over several years, and her face and figure shaped one of the most admired allegories in American sculpture. It's often said that she also posed for the figure of Liberty on the gold twenty-dollar coin that Saint-Gaudens designed a few years later, though that's harder to pin down. For a long time, her name was left out of the story, and only recently has she been given her due.

Think about the pairing for a moment. Sherman's armies helped bring an end to slavery. Leading him in bronze and gold, at the corner of Central Park, is the likeness of a Black woman.

The monument was shown in Paris at the World's Fair of 1900 before it was unveiled here in 1903. Its bright gilding has divided people almost from the start. Some find it glorious, others gaudy, and each time it's been regilded, the argument has started all over again.
=== DEEPER The Fountain of Abundance | people
You heard that the fountain in front of the Plaza was paid for by a bequest from Joseph Pulitzer. Its making was haunted by a death.

Pulitzer, whose newspaper campaign paid for the Statue of Liberty's pedestal, as you may have heard near City Hall, died in 1911 and left money in his will for a fountain at this corner of Central Park, in the spirit of the great fountains of Paris. The design went to Thomas Hastings, of Carrère and Hastings, the architects of the New York Public Library, who drew a series of stone basins stepping down toward the plaza. For the figure on top, the commission went to Karl Bitter, a Vienna-born sculptor who had become one of the busiest in New York. You may have seen his bronze doors at Trinity Church.

Bitter chose Pomona, the Roman goddess of abundance, with a basket of fruit in her arms. In April 1915, he had just finished the plaster model when he was struck by a car and killed. He was forty-seven.

His widow and Hastings chose another sculptor, Isidore Konti, to carry the figure through to bronze, working with Bitter's young assistant, Karl Gruppe, and the fountain was dedicated in 1916. In the 1930s, Gruppe led the parks department's campaign to restore the city's neglected monuments, cleaning and repairing statues all over town.

So the goddess of abundance at the edge of the park is a collaboration across a death: Bitter's model, finished by his colleagues, in a fountain paid for by a newspaperman's will.
=== DEEPER Zelda in the Fountain | legends
Here's a story New Yorkers love to tell about the Pulitzer Fountain. It may even be true.

In 1920, F. Scott Fitzgerald published his first novel, This Side of Paradise, became famous almost overnight, and married Zelda Sayre of Montgomery, Alabama, in the rectory of St. Patrick's Cathedral, down Fifth Avenue. The two of them became the golden couple of the Jazz Age, and they lived the part: riding on the roofs of taxis, dancing on tables, getting asked to leave hotels. And according to legend, one night Zelda, fully dressed, jumped into the fountain in front of the Plaza, with Scott perhaps right behind her.

Did it happen? Nobody has pinned it down. Other versions put Zelda's famous plunge in the fountain at Union Square or in Washington Square Park, and in her hometown, people tell a similar story about a fountain there. What's certain is that the Plaza meant something to Scott. In the middle of the quarrel, the narrator, Nick Carraway, realizes that it's his thirtieth birthday. If you've read the book, look up at the windows and picture that hot afternoon.
=== STOP Carnegie Hall & Billionaires' Row
=== DEEPER Jazz Comes to Carnegie Hall | culture
You heard that everyone from Mahler to Benny Goodman has played here. Goodman's night is the famous one. But he wasn't the first to bring this kind of music here.

On May 2nd, 1912, James Reese Europe led his Clef Club Orchestra onto this stage for a Concert of Negro Music, a benefit for the Music School Settlement for Colored People. Europe was a Black bandleader and composer who had organized the Clef Club, a union and booking agency for Black musicians in New York. His orchestra had more than a hundred players, with banjos, mandolins and pianos, and it played to a sold-out hall with a racially mixed audience. Whether you call the music jazz or the ragtime that came just before it, it was the first time that sound filled this hall. Europe went on to lead the band of the 369th Infantry, the Harlem Hellfighters, in the First World War, carrying the new American music to France.

Twenty-six years later, on January 16th, 1938, Benny Goodman, the King of Swing, brought his band here. Swing was dance music, played in ballrooms and clubs, and many people thought it had no place in a concert hall. Goodman's musicians included the Black pianist Teddy Wilson and vibraphonist Lionel Hampton, at a time when most bands were segregated, and he brought in guests from Count Basie's and Duke Ellington's bands. Before the concert, asked how long an intermission he wanted, Goodman is said to have answered: I don't know. How long does Toscanini take? When the band tore into Sing, Sing, Sing, the hall went wild.

The recording of that night, released years later, became one of the best-selling jazz albums ever made. Five years later, Duke Ellington premiered his long jazz symphony, Black, Brown and Beige, on this stage. Jazz has had a home at Carnegie Hall ever since.
=== DEEPER The Studios Upstairs | people
Look up above the hall. For more than a century, the building's upper floors were full of artists.

A few years after the hall opened, two towers of studios were added, finished in the 1890s, with big windows and skylights for working artists. They became one of the great creative addresses in New York. Over the years, the tenants included painters, sculptors, dancers, singers, actors, teachers and photographers. Marlon Brando lived in one as a young actor. Leonard Bernstein lived in another early in his career, before he became the most famous conductor in America. Students climbed the stairs for lessons, while the stars performed down below.

The best-known of the last tenants was Bill Cunningham, the photographer who for decades rode his bicycle around Manhattan photographing what people actually wore on the street, for the New York Times. He lived and worked in a tiny studio here, crammed with filing cabinets full of negatives, for about sixty years.

In 2010, Carnegie Hall moved out its remaining residents to turn the towers into rehearsal rooms, classrooms and offices for its education programs. Some of the longtime tenants fought it, and the papers wrote about the end of a bohemian era. Today, young musicians practice where the artists once lived, which seems only fitting in a building where the answer to the old joke is practice.
=== DEEPER Music Hall, Founded by Andrew Carnegie | names
Look above the entrance arches, and you'll find words carved in stone: Music Hall, founded by Andrew Carnegie. That was the building's first name.

Its architect, William Tuthill, was not a famous one. He was in his thirties, a gifted amateur cellist who sat with Carnegie on the board of the Oratorio Society, and before he drew the hall, he toured Europe to study the concert halls most admired for their sound. Many people credit his musician's ear for the acoustics you heard about.

When the hall opened in May 1891, it was simply the Music Hall. The opening was a five-day festival. On the first night, after a speech by the Episcopal bishop of New York, Walter Damrosch conducted the orchestra, and then one of the most famous composers in the world stepped onto the stage: Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky, who had come from Russia to conduct his own music. Two days later, on his fifty-first birthday, he conducted his Third Suite.

Tchaikovsky kept a diary of his American trip. It's full of wonder at the city and the warmth of its people, and full of homesickness too. He wrote that he seemed to be far more famous in America than in Europe. And he could be blunt: sitting through a long choral work by Berlioz on opening night, he found it tedious until the very end.

The plain name didn't last. The hall's board persuaded a reluctant Carnegie to let them use his. The story goes that to European performers, a music hall meant a place for comic songs and variety acts, not symphonies. By the mid-1890s, the Music Hall had become Carnegie Hall, though the old name is still carved over the door.

Its rooms have since taken on other names, many of them for donors, and the main auditorium is named for Isaac Stern, the violinist who saved the building. But to musicians around the world, the whole place is simply Carnegie, the hall every young performer dreams of playing.
