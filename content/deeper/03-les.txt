=== STOP Mulberry Street & Little Italy
=== DEEPER Birds of Passage | history
Many of the Italians who crowded these streets never meant to stay, and that shaped Little Italy in ways that are easy to miss.

Most came from the poor rural south, where land was scarce and wages were miserable. Young men crossed the Atlantic to earn money for a few years, often in seasonal work, then went home to buy land or pay off debts. Italians called them ritornati, the returned. Americans called them birds of passage. In some years, nearly as many Italians sailed home from New York as arrived, and something like half of all the Italians who came in those decades eventually went back.

Many of the men who stayed found work through a padrone, a labor broker, usually a fellow countryman who spoke English. The padrone met new arrivals, found them beds, and sent them out in gangs to dig railroad cuts, lay track, and build tunnels and reservoirs, taking a cut of their pay along the way. Some were honest, and many were not. Italian laborers did much of the heavy digging for New York's first subway, and for the aqueducts that bring the city its water.

The village loyalties you heard about went deep. Immigrants from the same town lived on the same block, married within it, formed mutual aid societies under the name of their hometown saint, and held their own feast days. Italian had dozens of dialects, and a Sicilian and a Genoese could barely understand each other. In a real sense, many of these people became Italians in America, by being treated as one group by everyone else.

By the 1920s, families had put down roots, children were born here, and the birds of passage had become Italian Americans.
=== DEEPER Joe Petrosino and the Black Hand | people
Around 1900, Little Italy lived in fear of letters marked with a black hand, and the man who fought them became a legend on both sides of the Atlantic.

The Black Hand was a method more than an organization. A shopkeeper, a banker or a successful family would receive a letter demanding money, signed with a drawing of a black hand, a dagger or a skull. Those who didn't pay might see their shop bombed or a child kidnapped. Most of the extortionists were small independent gangs, preying on their own countrymen, and most victims were too frightened to go to the police. Even the great tenor Enrico Caruso reportedly received a Black Hand letter.

Joseph Petrosino was born near Salerno and came to New York as a boy. He joined the police in 1883, at a time when there were almost no Italian speaking officers, and rose to detective. In 1905 he was put in charge of a new Italian Squad, a handful of Italian American detectives who went undercover, worked their own neighborhoods, and sent hundreds of criminals to prison or back to Italy.

In 1909, Petrosino sailed secretly to Sicily to collect criminal records that would let the United States deport Italian criminals who had lied about their pasts. The secret leaked to the press. On the evening of March 12th, 1909, he was shot dead in a square in Palermo. His killers were never convicted. His funeral back in New York drew a crowd estimated at a quarter of a million people.

Just a few blocks northwest of here, at the corner of Lafayette and Kenmare Streets, a small triangular plaza carries his name: Petrosino Square.
=== DEEPER Who Made the First Pizza? | legends
Just north of here, on Spring Street, Lombardi's proudly calls itself America's first pizzeria, licensed in 1905. It's one of the best-loved origin stories in American food. How well does it hold up?

The standard version says that Gennaro Lombardi, an immigrant from Naples, got a license to sell pizza in 1905 at 53 Spring Street, and that his shop trained a whole generation of pizza makers, including the founders of famous pizzerias like Totonno's in Coney Island and John's on Bleecker Street.

The training part seems to be true. The license is the problem. In 2019, a pizza historian named Peter Regas, after digging through old city records and Italian language newspapers, reported that he couldn't find any such license, and that Lombardi had only arrived in the country in 1904. He also found ads showing that other Neapolitans were already selling pizza in New York, including a pizzaiolo named Filippo Milone, who appears to have run pizzerias in the neighborhood a few years earlier, possibly including the one at 53 Spring Street that Lombardi later took over. And there are hints of pizza being sold in New York as far back as the 1890s.

None of that makes Lombardi's pizza any worse. Its coal oven still turns out the classic New York pie: thin, blistered, and big enough to fold. But the honest version is that pizza arrived in New York with Neapolitan immigrants, probably in more than one shop, before anyone thought to write down who was first.

The rest of the Italian American table took shape here too. In southern Italy, meat was a luxury. In New York, with steady wages, it became everyday food, and dishes like spaghetti with meatballs and veal parmigiana grew out of that new abundance.
=== STOP Old St. Patrick's Cathedral
=== DEEPER Dagger John and the Armed Churchyard | politics
The brick wall around this churchyard is wrapped in a story about the most formidable Catholic leader New York ever had.

In the 1840s, Irish Catholic immigrants were pouring into the city, and many native-born Protestants feared them as poor, drunk, and loyal to the Pope rather than to America. Nativist politicians campaigned against them. In the spring of 1844, nativist mobs in Philadelphia burned two Catholic churches, and people were killed in the rioting. New York's nativists, who had just won the mayor's office, planned a large rally, and many expected the churches here to be next.

New York's bishop, John Hughes, was an Irish immigrant who had worked as a gardener before becoming a priest. People called him Dagger John, partly for the cross he drew before his signature, and partly for his temperament. Hughes let it be known that if a single Catholic church were burned in New York, the city would become a second Moscow, a reference to the fire that consumed Moscow when Napoleon invaded. Armed parishioners, by many accounts in the thousands, guarded the churches. The rally fizzled, and no church burned.

The story usually told here is that men stood guard behind this very wall, with muskets ready. Hughes's warning is well documented. Whether the wall was really built as a fortification is less certain, but it certainly served as one.

Hughes went on to become New York's first archbishop. He founded the college that became Fordham University, built a network of Catholic schools, and in 1858 laid the cornerstone of the new St. Patrick's Cathedral on Fifth Avenue, far uptown in what many people called Hughes's folly. The city grew right up to it.
=== DEEPER Pierre Toussaint, the Hairdresser of Lower Manhattan | people
Pierre Toussaint, whom you heard about, may one day be New York's first saint, and his life is worth knowing in full.

He was born into slavery in 1766 in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, now Haiti. In 1787, as revolution brewed on the island, his owner, Jean Bérard, brought him to New York along with a few other enslaved people. Toussaint was apprenticed to a hairdresser, and he became one of the most fashionable hairdressers in the city, going from house to house to dress the elaborate hair of wealthy women.

Then Bérard's fortune collapsed, and Bérard died on a trip back to the island. Toussaint, still legally enslaved, used his own earnings to support Bérard's widow for years, paying her bills and keeping up appearances. Before she died, she freed him, in 1807.

As a free man, Toussaint bought the freedom of his sister and of Juliette Noël, whom he married. They took in his orphaned niece, and they took in other orphans and young men who needed work. He gave money to Catholic charities and orphanages, and helped fund the building of this cathedral. During epidemics of yellow fever, when many who could fled the city, he is said to have nursed the sick in their homes.

He went to Mass every day for some sixty years, at St. Peter's Church on Barclay Street, near today's World Trade Center. He died in 1853 and was buried here, in the churchyard, next to his wife. In 1990 his remains were moved to the crypt under the high altar of the Fifth Avenue cathedral, a place usually reserved for archbishops. In 1996, Pope John Paul the Second declared him Venerable, a step on the road to sainthood.
=== DEEPER Under the Floor: The Catacombs | underfoot
Beneath this church is something almost nobody expects to find in Manhattan: catacombs.

When the cathedral was built, its basement was laid out with burial vaults, and its churchyard with graves. For decades, prominent Catholic families bought vaults here: merchants, politicians, and some of the church's early bishops. The most famous family down there is the Delmonicos, whose restaurant invented fine dining in New York; about twenty members of the family are buried in their vault. The burial rooms run in narrow brick corridors lined with marble plaques, lit, on the candlelight tours that the parish began offering in recent years, by flickering lamps.

Then there's the bishop under the door. John Dubois, New York's third Catholic bishop, was French-born, and he never won over his largely Irish flock. The story goes that he asked to be buried beneath the entrance, so that the people who had walked all over him in life could keep doing it after his death. It's a good story, and the bishop is indeed said to lie near the front door, but the famous line about being walked over may be a later embellishment.

The churchyard outside the wall is one of the oldest Catholic burial grounds in the city. Its graves stopped taking new burials in the 1800s, as the city banned burials in lower Manhattan for health reasons and moved the dead to big new cemeteries in Brooklyn and Queens. That's why the Catholic dead of later generations lie in Calvary Cemetery, in Queens, and why this patch of old trees and leaning stones feels like a time capsule.

If you take one of the catacomb tours, look closely at the names on the vault doors. They are a who's who of early Catholic New York.
=== STOP The Bowery
=== DEEPER Mose the B'hoy, and a Songwriter's Last Days | culture
In the 1840s, the Bowery gave American popular culture its first great urban hero, and twenty years later it took one of its greatest songwriters.

The hero was Mose. He was a Bowery B'hoy, as they said it, a working class young man in a red flannel shirt and a tall hat, with his hair slicked into curls called soaplocks, a volunteer fireman who loved a fight and loved his girl, Lize. Mose first appeared on stage in 1848, in a play called A Glance at New York, and audiences went wild. The actor Frank Chanfrau played him in sequel after sequel. Mose was brash, funny, loyal and proud, and the real Bowery boys in the audience saw themselves. He was the ancestor of every streetwise New York character since, from the Dead End Kids to sitcom cab drivers.

The songwriter was Stephen Foster, the author of Oh! Susanna, Camptown Races, and Beautiful Dreamer, the most famous American composer of his time. Foster sold his songs outright for small sums and earned little from their success. By the early 1860s, he was living alone in New York, drinking heavily and selling songs for a few dollars each. In January 1864, in a cheap hotel on the Bowery, he fell in his room and was badly hurt. He died a few days later at Bellevue Hospital, at the age of thirty seven. In his pocket was a worn purse with thirty eight cents and a scrap of paper on which he had written five words: dear friends and gentle hearts. It may have been the start of a song.

The Bowery's reputation was summed up by a hit song of 1891, called simply The Bowery, which warned that they say such things and they do strange things on the Bowery, and ended, I'll never go there anymore.
=== DEEPER Under the El: Skid Row and a Missing Subway | history
For nearly eighty years, an elevated railway ran above the Bowery, and when it came down, it took a promise with it.

The Third Avenue El was finished over the Bowery in 1878. Its tracks covered almost the whole width of the street, dropping ash, oil and noise on the sidewalks below. Rents fell, and the Bowery filled with the businesses that cheap rents attract: pawnshops, saloons, and lodging houses. By the early twentieth century, the Bowery had dozens of flophouses that could sleep thousands of men a night. The cheapest spaces were cubicles a few feet wide, with walls that stopped short of the ceiling and chicken wire stretched over the top, rented for a few cents a night.

The men who lived here were not all drunks. Many were laborers between jobs, seamen ashore, old men on tiny pensions, and veterans. During the Depression, the Bowery's missions served huge breadlines, and after World War Two, the street became the national symbol of men who had fallen through the cracks.

In 1955, the El was torn down, and light fell on the Bowery for the first time in generations. The city had promised a replacement: a new subway under Second Avenue, first planned back in 1929. Money for it was raised in a bond issue in 1951, and then spent on other projects. Construction finally started in the 1970s, and stopped during the city's fiscal crisis, leaving sealed tunnel sections buried in East Harlem and downtown. Only in 2017 did the first piece of the Second Avenue subway open, on the Upper East Side. Down here, the promise is still waiting.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: A Mural Wall and a Graffiti-Covered Bank | look
Walk a few blocks north along the Bowery, and two buildings tell the story of how the street went from skid row to art destination.

At the northwest corner of the Bowery and Houston Street, look for the big painted wall. In 1982, the young artist Keith Haring painted a mural there, a riot of his dancing cartoon figures. It was soon painted over. In 2008, a developer who owned the wall had Haring's mural recreated, and since then the wall has been repainted every few months by street artists from around the world. Whatever is on it today is a temporary exhibition, and you're seeing it for a limited run.

A little farther north, at 190 Bowery, at the corner of Spring Street, stands a heavy granite bank building from 1898, built for the Germania Bank. For decades it looked abandoned, its doors and walls thick with graffiti. In fact, it was a home. In 1966, the photographer Jay Maisel bought the whole building, six floors and about seventy rooms, for around a hundred thousand dollars, and lived and worked there with his family for nearly fifty years, letting the graffiti accumulate outside. In 2015, he sold it for about fifty five million dollars, which says everything about what happened to the Bowery and to New York real estate in between.

On the way, notice how many restaurant supply stores still line the street, with stacks of pots and steel tables on the sidewalk. And look up at the facades. Above the storefronts, many buildings still carry the cornices and window decorations of the 1800s, from the years before the El darkened the street.
=== STOP The Tenement Museum
=== DEEPER The Families of 97 Orchard | people
The Tenement Museum doesn't tell history through famous people. It tells it through the families who actually lived in this building, and a few of their stories are worth knowing before you go in.

In 1869, an Irish couple, Joseph and Bridget Moore, lived here with their children. That year, their infant daughter Agnes died of malnutrition. The Moores had come from a country devastated by famine and found poverty here too, and anti-Irish prejudice everywhere.

In 1874, during the depression that followed the Panic of 1873, a German Jewish shoemaker named Julius Gumpertz walked out one morning and never came back. His wife, Nathalie, raised their children alone, working as a dressmaker in the apartment, and years later, she won an inheritance from her husband's family in Germany and moved her family uptown.

In the 1890s, Harris and Jennie Levine ran a small garment shop in their apartment, with several workers sewing in the same rooms where the family ate and slept. It was a sweatshop in the literal sense: home and work in one hot, crowded space.

In 1918, a Lithuanian Jewish family, the Rogarshevskys, lost the father, Abraham, to tuberculosis, the great killer of the tenements. And in the 1930s, during the Great Depression, a Sicilian Catholic family, the Baldizzis, lived here until the landlord evicted everyone in 1935. Their daughter Josephine, who grew up in the apartment, came back decades later to help the museum recreate it, remembering where the furniture stood and how her mother cooked.

Together, these families tell the American story in one stairwell: different languages, the same narrow rooms, and the same hope of moving out and up.
=== DEEPER How a Tenement Worked | architecture
Look up at the buildings on Orchard Street, and with a little knowledge you can read how they were built and lived in.

Almost every tenement here sits on a standard city lot, twenty five feet wide and a hundred feet deep. Builders covered as much of it as the law allowed. A typical building of the 1860s like number 97 had four apartments on each floor, two in front and two in back, with a narrow staircase up the middle. In each apartment, only the front room had windows to the outside. The bedrooms in the middle were dark.

Water at first came from a pump in the backyard, and the toilets were outhouses back there too. Over the decades, laws forced upgrades: a cold water tap in each hallway, then, in the early 1900s, toilets in the hallways, shared by two families. Bathtubs were rare. People went to the public baths that the city built after 1900, some of which still stand around here.

Look at the fronts of the buildings. The iron fire escapes were required by law after the 1860s, and families used them as balconies, places to sleep on hot summer nights, and shelves for laundry and food. Up at the top, the decorative cornices of pressed metal were bought from catalogs and bolted on to make cheap buildings look prosperous. Many facades also have stone or cast decorations around the windows, chosen from builders' pattern books.

The ground floors were usually shops or saloons, often a few steps down from the street, and the cellars held workshops, storerooms, and sometimes more people. At number 97, a German family named Schneider ran a beer saloon in the basement for years in the 1860s and 1870s, and the museum has rebuilt it. It was a place to drink, eat, cash a paycheck, and hear the news from home.
=== DEEPER The Kosher Meat War of 1902 | politics
In May 1902, the women of the Lower East Side went to war over the price of meat, and they wrote a new chapter in American protest.

The trigger was a sudden jump in the price of kosher beef, from about twelve cents a pound to eighteen, blamed on the big meat packers. For families who already lived on the edge, it meant no meat at all on the Sabbath table. On May 14th, a group of neighborhood women, led by two housewives named Fanny Levy and Sarah Edelson, met near Pike and Monroe Streets and agreed: nobody would buy kosher meat until the price came down.

The next morning they took to the streets. Women organized door to door, patrolled the butcher shops, and stopped other women from buying, sometimes by pulling meat out of their hands and throwing it into the street. Some shop windows were broken. Newspapers reported crowds in the thousands, and police arrested dozens of women.

The boycott spread to Brooklyn, Harlem, Newark, Boston and Philadelphia. The women formed a committee, held mass meetings in synagogues, and pressed rabbis to back them. By early June, prices had come down somewhat, and the boycott ended.

Historians see it as a dress rehearsal for what came next on these streets: the rent strikes of the early 1900s, the great garment strikes, and the idea that immigrant women, without votes or money, could organize and win. The tactics they invented, from picket lines at shop doors to neighborhood committees, would be used again and again.
=== STOP Eldridge Street Synagogue
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Inside the Sanctuary | look
If the museum is open, go inside and climb to the main sanctuary. Here is what to look for.

At the far end, on the east wall facing Jerusalem, is the ark, the great carved cabinet where the Torah scrolls are kept, with a lamp burning before it. In the middle of the room is the bimah, the raised platform where the Torah was read aloud, so that the reading happened in the midst of the congregation rather than at the front.

Look up and around. On three sides runs the women's balcony. In Orthodox practice, men and women prayed separately, and the women's gallery let them see and hear everything from above. The walls and ceilings are painted with stenciled patterns, and in places, painted marble and painted woodwork imitate costlier materials that the congregation couldn't afford. The brass chandeliers were built to burn gas and were later wired for electricity.

Then look down. Near the old wooden pews, the floorboards are worn into shallow hollows. Guides here will tell you they were worn by the feet of generations of men swaying as they prayed, the traditional motion called shuckling. Whether every hollow came from prayer, or some from simple foot traffic, it's hard to stand there and not imagine the crowds of 1900.

Finally, turn around and face the rose window above the entrance. When light comes through Kiki Smith's window of stars, it fills the back of the sanctuary with blue. The restorers decided to leave some of the old damage visible, a patch of original paint here, a worn surface there, so that the building shows its age honestly. It's not a replica of 1887. It's a survivor.
=== DEEPER Two Catholic Brothers and a Moorish Synagogue | architecture
One of the surprises of the Eldridge Street Synagogue is who designed it: two Catholic brothers from Germany.

Peter and Francis William Herter were young immigrant architects who had designed only a few buildings in the city when the congregation hired them in 1886. They had never designed a synagogue. They went on to a long career, mostly designing the very kind of building that surrounds this one: tenements, many of them still standing on the Lower East Side.

For the synagogue, they drew on a style that was hugely popular for synagogues in Germany and Central Europe in the nineteenth century: the Moorish Revival, with horseshoe arches, stripes and geometric ornament borrowed from the Islamic architecture of Spain. Great synagogues in Berlin, Vienna and Budapest had been built in this style, and so had New York's Central Synagogue on Lexington Avenue, finished in 1872 for a congregation of established German Jews.

For a Jewish congregation, the style did several things at once. It set the building apart from Christian churches, it recalled Jewish life in medieval Spain, and it announced, with all its towers and finials, that this congregation was here to stay.

But look closely at the facade and you'll see the Herters mixed their sources freely. The rose window is Gothic, the arches Moorish, and the round-arched windows Romanesque. Architects of the time weren't worried about purity. They wanted a building that would stand out on a street of plain brick tenements and make a statement. They succeeded: more than a century later, this building still stops people on the sidewalk.
=== DEEPER The Chief Rabbi's Funeral | history
The world that built this synagogue was proud, pious and divided, and one day in 1902 shows all three.

In 1888, a group of Orthodox congregations on the Lower East Side brought a famous scholar from Vilna, Rabbi Jacob Joseph, to be the chief rabbi of New York's Orthodox Jews. They hoped he would bring order to a chaotic religious scene, especially in the supervision of kosher meat. It went badly. When the rabbi's backers tried to pay for supervision with a small fee on kosher meat, many immigrants resented it, other rabbis refused to accept his authority, and the effort fell apart. Rabbi Joseph spent his last years ill and poor.

When he died, in July 1902, the neighborhood gave him one of the largest funerals New York had ever seen. Tens of thousands of mourners followed his coffin through the streets. As the procession passed a big printing press factory on Grand Street, workers there threw scrap metal and other objects from the windows and sprayed the crowd with water. When mourners fought back, the police waded in, and many officers beat the mourners instead of the attackers. Around two hundred people were hurt.

The Jewish community was outraged, and for once it was united. Mass meetings demanded justice. The mayor appointed an investigating committee, which criticized the police harshly.

Historians see the riot as a turning point. It showed the Lower East Side's Jews that they needed to organize politically to protect themselves, and they did, with organizations, voting blocs and leaders who could stand up to City Hall.
=== STOP Seward Park & the Forward Building
=== DEEPER Abraham Cahan's Newspaper | people
The Forward was the voice of the Lower East Side, and for nearly half a century it was the voice of one man, Abraham Cahan.

Cahan was born in 1860 near Vilna, in the Russian Empire, and fled to America in 1882 as a young radical with the police on his trail. He learned English fast, taught it to other immigrants, and began writing in both languages. His novella Yekl, a story of a Jewish immigrant couple pulled apart by Americanization, was turned many decades later into the film Hester Street. His novel The Rise of David Levinsky, about a poor immigrant who becomes a rich garment manufacturer and finds himself empty inside, is still read as one of the great immigrant novels.

As editor of the Forward from the early 1900s, Cahan made a radical choice: he wrote in plain, everyday Yiddish, the language people actually spoke, rather than the formal Yiddish of intellectuals. He mixed socialism with practical advice, gossip with literature, and he understood that an immigrant newspaper's job was to help readers live in America.

He also gave the paper its home. The ten-story Forward Building went up in 1912, towering over the tenements of East Broadway. Its facade carries four portraits of socialist thinkers: Marx and Engels, whom you heard about, and the German labor leaders Ferdinand Lassalle and Wilhelm Liebknecht.

Cahan ran the paper into the 1940s and died in 1951. The Forward moved uptown in 1974. A Chinese church owned the building for years, and then it became apartments. The newspaper itself survived, shrinking, and today it publishes online in English and Yiddish.
=== DEEPER Clara Lemlich and the Uprising | politics
The garment strike of 1909 began with a young woman pushing her way to a stage, and it changed the American labor movement.

Clara Lemlich was a Ukrainian Jewish immigrant in her early twenties who worked in the shirtwaist shops, where young women sewed the fashionable blouses of the era for long hours and low pay, often paying for their own needles and thread and being fined for mistakes. She had already been beaten on a picket line by thugs hired by her employer.

On November 22nd, 1909, the union held a mass meeting at Cooper Union. Speaker after speaker, many of them men, urged caution. Then Lemlich climbed onto the platform and spoke in Yiddish. She said she was a working girl, one of those on strike against intolerable conditions, that she was tired of talk, and that she moved for a general strike. The hall erupted.

The next day, thousands of workers walked out, and within days, the strike had grown to some twenty thousand, maybe more, mostly young Jewish and Italian women. They picketed through a freezing winter. Hundreds were arrested. Wealthy women, including the banker's daughter Anne Morgan and the suffragist Alva Belmont, joined them, raising money and showing up at night court. Newspapers called these supporters the mink brigade.

By February 1910, most shops had settled, with shorter hours and better pay, and the union's membership soared. But some of the biggest shops, including the Triangle Waist Company, refused the demand for safer conditions. A year later, the Triangle fire killed one hundred forty six workers. Clara Lemlich spent the rest of her life organizing, for suffrage, against high rents, and for the cause of working people.
=== DEEPER Lillian Wald's Nurses on the Rooftops | people
A few blocks south of here, on Henry Street, a young nurse from Rochester changed the way America takes care of its poor and its children.

In 1893, Lillian Wald was teaching a home nursing class on the Lower East Side when a little girl led her to a tenement where the girl's mother lay sick in a filthy room. Wald later said that this was her baptism of fire. She and a fellow nurse, Mary Brewster, moved into the neighborhood to live among their patients. With money from the banker Jacob Schiff, she opened a house at 265 Henry Street, which became the Henry Street Settlement.

Wald's nurses went into tenements to care for the sick at home, charging little or nothing. To save time and avoid endless stairs, they often traveled from building to building across the rooftops. Wald coined the term public health nurse for what they did. She pushed the city to put nurses in the public schools, and the experiment worked so well that school nursing spread across the country.

She had an eye for causes. She campaigned against child labor and was among those who first proposed a federal Children's Bureau, created in 1912. She championed playgrounds like Seward Park, supported unions and women's suffrage, and opposed America's entry into the First World War. The settlement started a theater, the Neighborhood Playhouse, and music classes that grew into a music school.

The Henry Street Settlement still operates today, out of the same row of houses, and the Visiting Nurse Service that grew out of Wald's rooftop nurses is still caring for New Yorkers at home.
=== STOP Katz's, Russ & Daughters & Houston Street
=== DEEPER Pastrami, Pickles and the Egg Cream | food
The foods that made this neighborhood famous each have a story, and a few have a legend attached.

Pastrami came from Romania and Moldova, where pastrama was a way of preserving meat, often goose or mutton, by salting, seasoning and drying it. In New York, cheap beef took over. Brisket or navel is cured in brine for weeks, rubbed with pepper and coriander, smoked, then steamed until tender. A popular story says a butcher named Sussman Volk introduced pastrami to New York in 1888, after a Romanian friend gave him the recipe in exchange for storing his luggage. It's a nice tale, and impossible to confirm.

Pickles were everywhere. Barrels of sour and half-sour cucumbers sat on the sidewalks of Essex and Orchard Streets, and pickle men sold them from the brine with their bare hands. A few pickle sellers still carry on, and every fall the neighborhood holds a pickle festival.

Bagels came with the Jews of Poland. For much of the twentieth century, New York's bagels were made by hand by a small union of Jewish bakers, who guarded their trade fiercely and often passed their jobs down from father to son. Machines eventually broke their hold.

And then there's the egg cream, a fizzy drink of milk, seltzer and chocolate syrup, containing neither egg nor cream. Nobody knows where the name came from. A candy store owner on Second Avenue named Louis Auster is often credited with inventing it in the 1890s, but there are rival claims and rival theories, including one that the name is a garbled Yiddish phrase.

Pick one of each, and you have a Lower East Side lunch that has barely changed in a century.
=== DEEPER Names: Loyalists, War Heroes and SoHo | names
The street names around here are a map of history, if you know how to read them.

Before the Revolution, much of this land was the farm of the De Lancey family, wealthy merchants and politicians. James De Lancey sided with the British, and after the war, New York State confiscated his estate and sold it off in lots. His name survives anyway, on Delancey Street, and so does his orchard, on Orchard Street.

Rivington Street is named for James Rivington, who printed the leading Loyalist newspaper in British-occupied New York. There's a twist. Historians have long suspected that Rivington was secretly passing information to George Washington's spies, and after the war, he stayed in New York unharmed, which suggests that someone was protecting him.

Then look at the streets between the Bowery and Allen Street: Chrystie, Forsyth, Eldridge and Allen. They were named in 1817 for young American officers who died in the War of 1812, a war now mostly forgotten, which once mattered enough to rename a neighborhood.

Houston Street, as you heard, honors William Houstoun, and New Yorkers still pronounce it his way. Kenmare Street, a few blocks west, was named for the Irish hometown of the mother of Big Tim Sullivan, the Tammany boss of the Bowery. Even the neighborhoods have invented names. SoHo, south of Houston, was coined by a city planner in the 1960s. Nolita, north of Little Italy, came from real estate agents in the 1990s. And Loisaida, the Puerto Rican name for the Lower East Side, was spread by the poet Bimbo Rivas in the 1970s.
=== DEEPER Ludlow Street, from the Jail to the Velvet Underground | culture
Ludlow Street, the narrow street beside Katz's, has had more lives than most streets in the city.

At its southern end, near Broome Street, stood the Ludlow Street Jail, a county jail for debtors and civil prisoners. Its most famous inmate was Boss Tweed, who died there in 1878. The jail closed in the 1920s, and a high school was built on the site.

In the tenement years, Ludlow Street was as crowded as any in the neighborhood, lined with shops on the ground floors and sweatshops above. In the 1960s, its cheap, run-down apartments drew a different kind of immigrant: young artists and musicians. In 1965, the Welsh viola player John Cale shared an apartment at 56 Ludlow Street, and that's where he and a young songwriter named Lou Reed rehearsed with friends and began forming the Velvet Underground, one of the most influential rock bands ever.

Through the 1980s and 1990s, Ludlow Street became one of the centers of a downtown scene of small bars, galleries and music clubs, with names like Max Fish and Pianos, where bands played to a few dozen people and sometimes went on to fame.

Today, the street's mix is typical of the new Lower East Side. A hundred-year-old tenement may hold a cocktail bar on the ground floor and expensive apartments above, next to a new glass hotel, across from a shop that has sold the same goods for decades. Walk its length, and you pass through a century and a half of the neighborhood's history in about five minutes.

=== DEEPER Fiorello La Guardia, the Little Flower | people
The man who built this market was the most popular mayor in New York history, and almost nothing about him was typical.

Fiorello La Guardia, whose first name means little flower, was born in Greenwich Village in 1882, but grew up mostly in Arizona, where his father was an army bandmaster. As a young man, he worked at American consulates in Hungary and in the port city of Fiume, then came back to New York and worked as an interpreter at Ellis Island while studying law at night. He spoke Italian, Yiddish, German and Croatian, and he campaigned in all of them.

He was short, loud and tireless, a Republican who backed Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal and fought Tammany Hall. Elected mayor in 1933, he served three terms. He smashed slot machines with a sledgehammer for the newsreels, chased gangsters out of the city's markets, rebuilt parks and bridges with federal money, and gave his name eventually to an airport. During a newspaper delivery strike in 1945, he read the Sunday comics aloud on the city's radio station, doing the voices himself, so the children wouldn't miss them.

The pushcarts were one of his targets. He thought they were unsanitary, chaotic and a traffic hazard, and he also wanted to give vendors a better life. His answer was a string of enclosed public markets, with stalls, running water and refrigeration: this one on Essex Street, opened in 1940, La Marqueta in East Harlem under the railroad viaduct, and the market on Arthur Avenue in the Bronx, which is still the heart of the Bronx's Little Italy.

Some vendors hated leaving the streets. But the markets lasted, and several of them still operate.
=== DEEPER Building the Williamsburg Bridge | history
The Williamsburg Bridge was built fast and cheap, nearly burned before it opened, and nearly fell apart eighty years later.

Its chief engineer, Leffert Buck, had built bridges across canyons in South America, and he designed this one for heavy traffic: two tracks for elevated trains, two for trolleys, roadways and walkways. To save money and time, he used steel towers instead of masonry ones, and a stiff, boxy truss to carry the deck. Critics hated how it looked. But it opened in 1903 as the longest suspension bridge in the world.

In November 1902, with the cables in place, a fire broke out in the wooden scaffolding at the top of the Manhattan tower. Crowds watched it burn from the streets below, and engineers feared that the heat would ruin the cables. The fire burned itself out, the damaged wires were checked and repaired, and the bridge opened a year later.

The bridge's real problem came later and more slowly. The cables had been made of wire that wasn't galvanized, without a protective coating of zinc, and over the decades, water and salt went to work. By the 1980s, inspectors found cables badly rusted and steel beams rotted through. In April 1988, the city closed the bridge to cars and trains, at a moment's notice, fearing it could not safely carry them. Some engineers argued it should be torn down and replaced. Instead, the city rebuilt it, piece by piece, over many years, while keeping it partly open.

Today the bridge carries the J, M and Z trains, cars, and some of the busiest bike lanes in the country. Walk or bike up its long ramp and you'll understand why: the view of Manhattan from the top is worth every step.
=== DEEPER Sonny Rollins on the Bridge | culture
In 1959, one of the greatest jazz musicians in the world disappeared from the scene, and he could be found, if you knew where to look, on the walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge.

Sonny Rollins was already a star of the tenor saxophone, with acclaimed albums behind him, when he decided he wasn't good enough. He stepped away from performing and recording to work on his playing. He lived in an apartment on Grand Street, near here, and he worried that practicing for hours a day would disturb his neighbors, including a woman who was expecting a baby. So he took his horn up onto the bridge.

For about two years, day and night, in all kinds of weather, Rollins practiced on the walkway, high above the East River, with the subway trains rumbling past. He said later that the bridge was the perfect place: he could play as loud as he wanted, and the noise of the trains and boats pushed him to play bigger. Passersby occasionally stopped to listen. Most had no idea who he was.

When he came back, in 1962, he named his comeback album The Bridge. It's considered one of the great records of its era.

Rollins played into his eighties and became one of the last living giants of his generation of jazz. So as you walk up the bridge's ramp, imagine the sound of a tenor saxophone floating over the rumble of the trains.
