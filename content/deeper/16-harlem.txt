=== STOP Marcus Garvey Park
=== DEEPER Red, Black and Green | politics
You heard that Garvey filled Harlem's streets with parades. The greatest came in August 1920, when the UNIA opened its first International Convention of the Negro Peoples of the World. Delegates came from across the United States, the Caribbean, Central America and Africa. They marched through Harlem in uniform, with bands and banners, and that night about twenty-five thousand people packed the old Madison Square Garden to hear Garvey speak.

Over the following weeks, the convention adopted a Declaration of Rights of the Negro Peoples of the World, protesting lynching, segregation and colonial rule. It named Garvey Provisional President of Africa, a continent he never set foot on. And it chose colors for the movement: red, black and green. Look around Harlem, on flags, murals and vendors' tables, and you'll still see them. That flag is Garvey's, known today as the Pan-African flag.

Not every Black leader admired him. A socialist magazine edited by A. Philip Randolph ran a campaign called Garvey Must Go. W. E. B. Du Bois, who later lived on Sugar Hill, at the end of this walk, wrote in 1924 that Garvey was either a lunatic or a traitor. Garvey answered with insults of his own, and the feud divided Harlem for years.

After his deportation, Garvey kept organizing, first from Jamaica and then from London, but the movement never regained its size. He died in London in 1940. As the story is usually told, newspapers had already printed his obituary by mistake, describing him as broke and forgotten, and reading it brought on the stroke that killed him.

He was not forgotten. In 1964, his body was brought home to Jamaica, which named him its first National Hero. And in January 2025, President Joe Biden granted Marcus Garvey a posthumous pardon, more than a century after his conviction.
=== DEEPER The Tower and the Submarine | people
Look up at the fire watchtower again. It's often credited to James Bogardus, the cast iron pioneer you may have heard about in SoHo. But in 1855, when the city held a competition for a bell tower on this hill, the winning design came from a little-known German-born engineer named Julius Kroehl.

Kroehl's real ambitions lay underwater. During the Civil War, in Brooklyn, he began building an iron submarine called the Sub Marine Explorer, about thirty-six feet long, for a company that hoped to harvest pearl oysters. Its crew could fill a chamber with compressed air, open hatches in the floor, and reach the sea bed while the craft rested on the bottom. In 1866, it was taken apart, shipped to Panama, and put back together in the Pearl Islands, in the Pacific.

The dives worked, at first. But in September 1867, Kroehl died in Panama, of what was recorded as a fever. Some researchers now suspect decompression sickness, the bends, which nobody then understood. When others took the submarine out again, they fell ill too, and it was left on a beach.

There it stayed, rusting between the tides. Local legend had it that it was a Japanese submarine from the Second World War. Then, in 2001, the maritime archaeologist James Delgado identified it as Kroehl's Explorer, one of the oldest submarines known to survive anywhere.

So the man behind this tower, built to watch the skies over Harlem for smoke, also built a machine to walk the bottom of the sea.
=== DEEPER Harlem's Summer of Soul | culture
In the summer of 1969, while the world watched astronauts land on the moon, this park hosted one of the great music festivals of the decade. Then the world forgot it.

The Harlem Cultural Festival was a series of free concerts, spread over six weekends, organized and hosted by a singer named Tony Lawrence. The lineup was astonishing: a nineteen-year-old Stevie Wonder, Nina Simone, B. B. King, Gladys Knight and the Pips, Sly and the Family Stone, the Fifth Dimension, Hugh Masekela, Mongo Santamaría, and the gospel queen Mahalia Jackson, who shared the microphone with a young Mavis Staples to sing Precious Lord, Take My Hand. By some counts, about three hundred thousand people came over the summer.

A television producer named Hal Tulchin filmed nearly all of it, and tried to sell it as the Black Woodstock. Hardly anyone was interested. The tapes sat in his basement for about fifty years.

In 2021, the drummer and producer Ahmir Questlove Thompson turned that footage into a documentary, Summer of Soul. It won the Academy Award for best documentary feature, and a forgotten summer in Harlem became famous all over again.

Watch it, and you'll see this park packed with people in the summer heat. In one scene, people in the crowd are asked what they think of the moon landing. Several shrug. The money, they say, would be better spent on the people of Harlem.
=== STOP 125th Street & the Apollo Theater
=== DEEPER The Book Signing at Blumstein's | history
Look across 125th Street and a little to the east, to number 230. For decades, this was Blumstein's, the biggest department store in Harlem.

You heard about the Don't Buy Where You Can't Work campaign. Blumstein's was its most famous target. In the summer of 1934, picketers marched outside, urging shoppers to stay away from a store that took Black customers' money but wouldn't hire Black salesclerks. Blumstein's gave in and hired Black saleswomen, and the victory spread the campaign up and down the street.

Twenty-four years later, on a Saturday afternoon in September 1958, a young minister named Martin Luther King Junior sat at a table inside Blumstein's, signing copies of his first book, Stride Toward Freedom, about the bus boycott in Montgomery, Alabama. A woman stepped up and asked whether he was Martin Luther King. When he said he was, she plunged a steel letter opener into his chest.

King was rushed to Harlem Hospital, on Lenox Avenue, with the blade still in him. Surgeons found its tip resting against his aorta, the body's main artery, and removed it with great care. The papers reported that if he had so much as sneezed, he would have died. His attacker, Izola Ware Curry, was found to be mentally ill and committed to a hospital. King said he felt no ill will toward her.

Ten years later, in Memphis, on the night before he was killed, King remembered that day. Many letters came to him in the hospital, he said, but the one he would never forget came from a ninth-grade girl at White Plains High School. She wrote that she was so happy he hadn't sneezed. Then he told the crowd all that he would have missed if he had. The street outside now carries his name.
=== DEEPER Two Rumors | politics
Twice in the twentieth century, a false rumor set 125th Street alight.

The first came on March 19th, 1935, at a Kress five-and-ten store on this street. A sixteen-year-old Puerto Rican boy named Lino Rivera was caught slipping a penknife worth ten cents into his pocket. Store workers grabbed him, there was a struggle, and the police took him out through the back and let him go. But shoppers had seen the struggle, and word spread that the boy had been beaten to death. When a hearse happened to pull up nearby, it seemed to prove it. By nightfall, crowds were smashing store windows along 125th Street. Three people died.

Mayor Fiorello La Guardia appointed a commission to find out why, with the sociologist E. Franklin Frazier leading its research. Its answer was not the rumor, but years of discrimination in jobs, housing, relief and policing. La Guardia never released the report, but the Amsterdam News, Harlem's newspaper, printed it.

Eight years later, in August 1943, in the lobby of the Hotel Braddock on 126th Street, a white police officer shot a Black soldier, Private Robert Bandy, in the shoulder during a scuffle. A rumor flew that the soldier had been killed, in uniform, defending his mother. Again the windows went. This time, six people died.

That week, a young Harlem writer buried his father. In his essay Notes of a Native Son, James Baldwin recalled that the funeral fell on his nineteenth birthday, and that they drove his father to the cemetery through a wilderness of smashed plate glass. For Baldwin, his father's bitterness and the fury in the streets were part of the same story.
=== DEEPER The Executioner's Broom | people
You heard about the Executioner, who sweeps unlucky acts off the Apollo stage. Amateur Night itself was the creation of Ralph Cooper, an actor, dancer and emcee, who had run an amateur show at the Lafayette Theatre and brought it here in 1934, with a radio broadcast that carried it far beyond Harlem. He hosted it, on and off, for many years.

The most famous Executioner was a tap dancer named Howard Sims, called Sandman because he danced on sand scattered over a board, so that his feet hissed and scraped like brushes on a drum. He knew the crowd from the other side. As the story goes, he was booed off this stage several times before he ever won. Later, the theater hired him to chase away the acts the crowd rejected, with a broom or a long hook, to the audience's delight.

Winning was never easy. One night in 1964, a young guitarist named Jimi Hendrix took first prize, a few years before the world knew his name.

And the Apollo is where Harlem says goodbye. When James Brown died on Christmas Day, 2006, his body was carried up 125th Street in a white carriage drawn by horses, and laid in a gold casket on this stage. Thousands lined up to file past the hardest-working man in show business, in the house where he had recorded Live at the Apollo.
=== STOP The Hotel Theresa
=== DEEPER The Stockbroker's White Tower | architecture
Look up at the Theresa. It went up in 1912 and 1913 for Gustavus Sidenberg, a German-born stockbroker, and was designed by the brothers George and Edward Blum, who designed many of Manhattan's apartment houses in those years. At thirteen stories, it towered over the brownstones and tenements of 125th Street, and for decades it was one of the tallest buildings in Harlem.

The Blums clad it in white brick and white terra cotta, clay that was molded, glazed and fired, so that it kept its brightness and could be washed clean. On a gray day, it still seems to glow. It was built as a grand hotel for a Harlem that developers expected to stay white, and prosperous.

The neighborhood changed around it, but the hotel's rules didn't. For more than two decades, the Theresa turned Black guests away, even as Harlem became the capital of Black America. Then, in the late 1930s, a Black businessman named Love B. Woods bought it, and in 1940 the whites-only rule was gone. Black travelers were still refused at most hotels downtown, but Harlem now had a first-class hotel of its own, and one of its tallest buildings was finally one its people could walk into.

After the hotel closed, the building was turned into offices, and it still is. In 1993, the city named it a landmark.
=== DEEPER The Chickens of the Theresa | legends
You heard that Fidel Castro moved his whole delegation here in September 1960, after a dispute with his Midtown hotel. Here's the dispute, and the legend that grew out of it.

The hotel was the Shelburne, on Lexington Avenue. According to Castro, its managers demanded ten thousand dollars in cash, in advance, to cover any damage. He called it an insult, swept out, and threatened to pitch tents in Central Park. Instead, late that night, his motorcade drove up to 125th Street, and the Theresa took them all in.

New York's tabloids had a field day. Stories spread that the Cubans had been thrown out of the Shelburne for keeping live chickens in their rooms, killing them, plucking them and cooking them on the spot. In some versions, the chickens were for religious rites. And in the papers, at least, the chickens followed the Cubans to Harlem, along with tales of feathers in the hallways of the Theresa.

Nobody has ever produced good evidence for any of it, and historians have called the stories politically motivated. But a picture of bearded revolutionaries cooking chickens in a hotel room was too good to let go, and the tale is still told, sometimes as fact.

What is documented is how well the move worked for Castro. By choosing Harlem, he turned a hotel bill into a statement about race in America, and the newspapers could hardly stop writing about it. He came back to Harlem decades later, in 1995, to speak to a packed house at Abyssinian Baptist Church, a few blocks north of here.
=== DEEPER The Wave of a Hand | politics
You heard that Malcolm X rented offices here. His rise as a leader in Harlem began on a spring night in 1957.

Malcolm was then minister of the Nation of Islam's Temple Number Seven, on Lenox Avenue at 116th Street. One evening in April, police officers were clubbing a man on a Harlem street when a member of the temple, Johnson Hinton, objected. The officers turned on him, beat him badly with their nightsticks, and took him to the station house.

Within hours, Malcolm was at the station, demanding to see him. Outside, dozens of the temple's men stood in silent, disciplined ranks. Behind them, a crowd of Harlem residents grew into the hundreds, and by some accounts into the thousands. The police let Malcolm see Hinton, who was badly hurt, and agreed to send him to Harlem Hospital. Malcolm stepped outside and gave a small signal with his hand. The ranks turned and marched away, and the crowd melted into the night.

A police official who watched it reportedly said that no one man should have that much power. Harlem had a new leader, and the police had a new worry. Hinton survived, and a jury later awarded him damages from the city.

Seven years later, after his split with the Nation of Islam and a pilgrimage to Mecca, Malcolm set up his new organization in this building, and he often spoke at rallies on the corner below, at 125th Street and Seventh Avenue, Harlem's open-air forum.
=== STOP The Schomburg Center
=== DEEPER The Collector's Day Job | people
You heard how a teacher's remark set Arturo Schomburg on his life's work. What paid for it was a day job. For more than twenty years, Schomburg worked at the Bankers Trust Company, downtown, where he rose to supervise the bank's mail for Latin America and the Caribbean. In the evenings, on weekends and on his vacations, he hunted through bookshops, auctions and dealers' catalogs, at home and abroad.

He had come to New York as a young activist. In the 1890s, he joined the movement to free Cuba and Puerto Rico from Spain, and helped found a club called Las Dos Antillas, the Two Antilles. In 1911, with the journalist John Edward Bruce, he founded the Negro Society for Historical Research, to gather the very evidence his teacher had said didn't exist. And in 1925, he wrote an essay called The Negro Digs Up His Past, arguing that Black history had been buried, ignored, or credited to others, and had to be recovered.

By the time the library bought his collection, for ten thousand dollars, it held some ten thousand items: books, manuscripts, prints and pamphlets, including rare works by Black writers going back to the sixteenth century, like the Latin poems of Juan Latino, a scholar of African descent who taught in Spain.

Selling it didn't mean letting go. In 1932, Schomburg became curator of his own collection, here, and stayed until his death in 1938. The library that grew from it now holds millions of items, and bears his name.
=== DEEPER The Theater in the Basement | culture
This library was never just a reading room. In 1920, it got a new head librarian, Ernestine Rose, a white woman who set out to make it a branch that truly served Harlem. She hired Black librarians at a time when the city's library system had almost none. One was Catherine Latimer, the first Black librarian in the New York Public Library. Another was Nella Larsen, who went on to write two acclaimed novels of the Harlem Renaissance, Quicksand and Passing.

The branch hosted lectures, readings and exhibitions of Black artists. And in its basement was a small theater. In 1940, the playwright Abram Hill and the actor Frederick O'Neal founded the American Negro Theatre there, a company where Black actors could play real people, not caricatures.

That basement stage launched remarkable careers. As Harry Belafonte told it, he was working as a janitor's helper when he was given two tickets to one of its shows as a tip, and he was hooked. A teenage dishwasher from the Bahamas named Sidney Poitier auditioned and was thrown out, for his thick accent and halting reading. He practiced by reading newspapers aloud and imitating radio announcers, came back, and got in. Ruby Dee, Alice Childress and Earle Hyman worked there too. One of the company's productions, Anna Lucasta, moved to Broadway in 1944 and became a long-running hit.

Not bad for a theater under a library.
=== DEEPER Madam Walker's Townhouse | companies
Walk around the corner onto 136th Street. Just behind the Schomburg is the Countee Cullen branch library, named for the poet. It stands where a grand townhouse once belonged to one of the richest Black women in America.

Sarah Breedlove was born in Louisiana in 1867, the first child in her family born into freedom. Orphaned at seven, married at fourteen, and widowed at twenty, she spent years as a washerwoman. Then, struggling with hair loss, she began developing hair care products, and as Madam C. J. Walker, she built a business that sold them across the country through thousands of Black women she trained as sales agents, giving them incomes of their own. She's often called the first self-made female millionaire in America, though just how rich she was is still debated.

Walker and her daughter bought the townhouse here around 1913. Later she built a mansion on the Hudson, Villa Lewaro, designed by Vertner Tandy, the first Black architect licensed in New York State. She died there in 1919.

Her daughter, A'Lelia Walker, made the 136th Street house famous. In 1927, she opened part of it as a salon for writers and artists, and called it the Dark Tower, after a column Countee Cullen wrote. Langston Hughes remembered A'Lelia as the joy goddess of Harlem's 1920s. The Dark Tower lasted only a couple of years, undone by money troubles, and A'Lelia died in 1931. The house was later torn down, and the library took its place.
=== STOP Abyssinian Baptist Church
=== DEEPER Keep the Faith, Baby | politics
You heard that Adam Clayton Powell Junior was expelled from Congress in 1967. Here is how he fell, and how Harlem stood by him.

In Washington, Powell was a crusader. For years, he tacked what became known as the Powell Amendment onto bill after bill, barring federal money from segregated schools and programs. Southern members hated it, and some liberal allies complained that it sank bills Black Americans needed. Powell kept at it.

He also loved the high life: fine clothes, nightclubs, trips abroad at public expense, and a retreat on the island of Bimini, in the Bahamas. Then, in 1960, in a television interview, he called a Harlem widow named Esther James a bag woman, someone who carried bribes for crooked police. She sued him for libel and won. Powell refused to pay. The courts held him in contempt, and in New York he faced arrest, except on Sundays, when the law didn't allow it. So for a time, Harlem's congressman came home only on Sundays, preached from this pulpit, and left before Monday.

In 1967, the House voted to exclude him. Harlem's answer was to reelect him that spring, in a special election, and again the next year. In 1969, the Supreme Court ruled that the House had no right to bar a member the voters had duly elected. But his power was gone. In 1970, he narrowly lost the Democratic primary to a young state assemblyman named Charles Rangel, who went on to represent Harlem in Congress for more than forty years. Powell died in 1972.

Through all his troubles, he had a farewell line for crowds and reporters, and it became his motto: Keep the faith, baby.
=== DEEPER The Preacher's Son at the Organ | culture
Abyssinian helped raise one of the great entertainers of the jazz age. Thomas Waller was born in Harlem in 1904. His father, Edward, was a lay preacher at this church, a strict man who reportedly called jazz music from the devil's workshop. His mother loved music and encouraged him, and as a boy he played the organ here for services.

By fifteen, he was playing the organ at the Lincoln Theatre on 135th Street, accompanying silent films. After his mother died, he left home, and fell in with the masters of Harlem stride piano, especially James P. Johnson, who took him on as a pupil.

By the 1930s, Fats Waller was a star: a dazzling pianist, the composer of songs like Ain't Misbehavin' and Honeysuckle Rose, and a comedian who could turn a sappy ballad into a joke with a raised eyebrow and a muttered aside. As one story goes, in Chicago in 1926, gunmen shoved him into a car, and he was sure he was about to be killed. Instead, they delivered him to Al Capone's birthday party, where he played for three days, and went home with his pockets full of cash.

In December 1943, on his way home from California, he died of pneumonia on a train near Kansas City. He was thirty-nine. His funeral was held here, and more than four thousand people came. Adam Clayton Powell Junior gave the eulogy. Fats Waller, he reportedly said, always played to a packed house.
=== DEEPER Paint Over the Billboards | people
The Powells were not Abyssinian's last famous pastors. In 1989, Calvin Butts, who had come to the church as a young assistant minister in the 1970s, became its pastor, and he led it for more than thirty years.

Butts believed in direct action. In 1990, he led church members around Harlem with buckets of white paint, covering billboards that advertised cigarettes and liquor, ads he said targeted Black neighborhoods.

His bigger project was rebuilding. In the 1980s, Harlem was full of abandoned buildings, many of them taken over by the city after landlords walked away. The church founded the Abyssinian Development Corporation, which restored and built hundreds of apartments, and in 1999 brought a big Pathmark supermarket to 125th Street, at a time when Harlem had few places to buy fresh food. For many years, Butts was also president of a state college on Long Island, while still preaching here on Sundays.

He died in 2022. His work is part of the Harlem revival you heard about at the start of this walk, with all its arguments. To some, the new housing and stores were the neighborhood rescuing itself. To others, they opened the door to rising rents that pushed longtime residents out.
=== STOP The Savoy Ballroom & Lenox Avenue
=== DEEPER The Hellfighters Come Home | history
On February 17th, 1919, Lenox Avenue saw the greatest homecoming in Harlem's history. The 369th Infantry Regiment, Black soldiers from New York, had come home from the First World War.

The regiment began as the 15th New York National Guard, and many of its men were from Harlem. In France, the American army wouldn't let them fight beside white troops, so it lent them to the French army. They spent longer in the front lines than almost any other American unit, and it's said the Germans gave them the name that stuck: the Hellfighters.

The most famous was Private Henry Johnson, a former railroad porter from Albany. One night in May 1918, a German raiding party attacked his outpost. Wounded again and again, Johnson fought them off with grenades, the butt of his rifle and a knife, and saved a fellow soldier from being carried off. France awarded him its Croix de Guerre. His own country took almost a century: in 2015, long after his death, President Obama awarded him the Medal of Honor.

That February day, the regiment marched up Fifth Avenue behind its famous band, led by James Reese Europe, whom you may have heard about at Carnegie Hall on the Fifth Avenue tour. At 110th Street, the parade swung over to Lenox Avenue, and Harlem went wild. The band struck up a popular tune, Here Comes My Daddy Now, and families broke into the ranks to embrace their men. Johnson rode in an open car, to the loudest cheers of all.

Three months later, Europe was dead, stabbed by one of his own drummers after a quarrel at a concert in Boston. The regiment lives on in its armory, a short walk east of here, on Fifth Avenue at 142nd Street.
=== DEEPER The Postman Who Taught the World to Swing | people
You heard that Frankie Manning invented the Lindy Hop's first air steps here. His later life is one of Harlem's great second acts.

In the 1930s, the best dancers at the Savoy formed a troupe managed by a Savoy bouncer named Herbert White, known as Whitey. Whitey's Lindy Hoppers toured the country and Europe, danced in shows and nightclubs, and in 1941 appeared in a Hollywood comedy, Hellzapoppin', in a few minutes of high-speed, airborne dancing, choreographed by Manning, that still looks impossible.

Then came the war. Manning was drafted, and by the time he came home, the swing era was fading. He kept a troupe going for a while, then gave up and took a job at the post office, where he worked for about thirty years.

In the 1980s, young swing dancers in California and Sweden, studying old films, went looking for the people in them. They found Frankie Manning in New York, in his seventies, and asked him to teach. He said yes. He taught all over the world, choreographed the Broadway revue Black and Blue, which won him a Tony Award in 1989, and created dances for Spike Lee's film Malcolm X.

On his birthdays, he would dance with one partner for every year of his age. He died in 2009, a month before his ninety-fifth birthday, and the celebration planned for it went ahead in his honor. Today, Lindy Hop is danced in cities all over the world, largely because a retired postal worker from Harlem agreed to teach it.
=== DEEPER Too Late for Dinner | food
After the clubs and ballrooms closed, Harlem got hungry. And one of its favorite answers became chicken and waffles.

The story usually told goes like this. In 1938, a restaurant called Wells opened on Seventh Avenue, in the low 130s, a short walk from here. Musicians and dancers finishing their last sets came in at three or four in the morning, too late for dinner and too early for breakfast. So the kitchen gave them both: fried chicken from the dinner menu, with a waffle from the breakfast menu. Wells grew into a supper club with a big dining room, and stayed in business for decades, famous for that plate.

As with most good food stories, the details are disputed. Sources disagree about who exactly founded Wells, and when it finally closed. A dancer named Dickie Wells, no relation, who ran his own Harlem nightspot, sometimes gets the credit instead. And chicken and waffles were eaten together long before, in Pennsylvania Dutch country, where stewed chicken and gravy were spooned over waffles.

What Harlem can claim is the version most people know today: crisp fried chicken, a waffle and syrup, eaten in the small hours, after the music. Harlem is widely credited with spreading it to soul food restaurants across the country. Order it nearby, and you're eating a small piece of the swing era.
=== STOP Strivers' Row
=== DEEPER The Builder of Liberty's Base | companies
You heard that a developer named David King built these blocks. King was one of the most successful builders in New York. His firm built the pedestal of the Statue of Liberty, whose fundraising you may have heard about in the Financial District, and Stanford White's Madison Square Garden.

In 1891, King set out to build something new for Harlem: not a row of houses for single buyers, but a planned enclave on two blocks, marketed as the King Model Houses, with three of the best architects in the city, and shared service alleys to keep deliveries and horses off the street.

His timing could hardly have been worse. Harlem was overbuilt, and in 1893 the country fell into a deep depression, set off by a financial panic. The houses didn't sell. King defaulted, and the Equitable Life Assurance Society, which had lent him the money, ended up with most of them.

The insurance company held on for a generation. As Harlem around it became Black, Equitable would sell or rent the houses only to white families, and some stood empty for years. Finally, in 1919, it gave in, and opened them to Black buyers. They came, as you heard, the doctors, lawyers and musicians of Black Harlem, and bought the houses white buyers had passed over.

So the blocks King built for the white upper middle class became, through his failure and an insurance company's stubbornness, the showplace of Black Harlem.
=== DEEPER Doctor Wright's Firsts | people
You heard that the surgeon Louis T. Wright lived on Strivers' Row. His career was a string of firsts, each won against resistance.

Wright was born in Georgia in 1891, and graduated from Harvard Medical School in 1915, near the top of his class. In the First World War, he served as an army doctor in France, was gassed, and introduced a better method of vaccinating troops against smallpox.

In 1919, he joined the staff of Harlem Hospital, the first Black doctor appointed there. Some white colleagues protested, but Wright stayed, and over the next three decades he helped make Harlem Hospital a place where Black doctors and nurses could train and rise. He became its director of surgery.

In 1929, he became the city's first Black police surgeon. He was an expert in head injuries and fractures, and designed a brace for broken necks. In 1948, he led the team that first tested the new antibiotic Aureomycin on human patients.

Outside the hospital, he chaired the board of the NAACP for years, and fought segregation in American medicine.

And he passed his calling on. His daughter, Jane Cooke Wright, joined him at Harlem Hospital, and went on to become a pioneer of cancer chemotherapy, testing drugs on cells from a patient's own tumor to find which would work best.
=== DEEPER Rent Party Tonight | culture
Strivers' Row was the exception. A few blocks away, most Harlemites lived in crowded apartments, and paid dearly for them. In the 1920s, landlords knew Black tenants had few other places to go, and studies found that Harlem families paid more than white New Yorkers for the same kind of housing. Many took in lodgers to make ends meet.

And many threw parties. A house-rent party worked like this. You printed a little card, often with a rhyme on it, inviting guests to a social whist party on Saturday night. Guests paid a small admission at the door, maybe a quarter, then paid again for plates of fried chicken, chitterlings and potato salad, and for drinks, bootleg during Prohibition. By morning, with luck, the rent was covered.

The music was the draw. Harlem's stride pianists, James P. Johnson, Willie The Lion Smith, and a young Fats Waller, went from party to party through the night, competing to outplay each other on whatever upright piano the host had. The rent party was a proving ground for a whole style of jazz piano. Langston Hughes went to many of them, and wrote about them fondly in his memoir, The Big Sea.

So while the strivers held their formal dinners behind these handsome doors, much of Harlem was paying its rent with music.
=== STOP Sugar Hill & Hamilton Grange
=== DEEPER A Refuge for a Disappointed Politician | legends
You heard that Hamilton built the Grange as a country retreat. In a letter to a friend at the end of 1802, he explained why, with a wry line: A garden, you know, is a very usual refuge of a disappointed politician. His party had lost power, his reputation was battered, and he threw himself into planning gardens and orchards, asking friends for seeds and plants.

Out of that garden grew Harlem's favorite tree story. As it's usually told, Hamilton planted a grove of thirteen sweet gum trees near the house, one for each of the original states, a living monument to the union he had worked to build. In some versions, the saplings were a gift from George Washington.

It's a lovely story, and that may be all it is. The National Park Service, which runs the Grange, treats it as a claim, not a fact. Even in the 1800s, people argued about it in the newspapers, and one account had Hamilton's own son saying the trees were already standing when his father bought the land. Whatever their origin, by 1908 the old grove was gone.

The legend outlived the trees. In recent years, new sweet gums have been planted beside the house. Look for their star-shaped leaves, which turn red, orange and purple in the fall, and the spiky seed balls that litter the ground beneath them. Whoever planted the first ones, Hamilton would surely have enjoyed the story.
=== DEEPER The Double and the Fix | history
Look up the hill at City College's Gothic towers. In 1950, the college's basketball team did something no team had done before, or has done since.

The City College Beavers were New York kids, Jewish and Black players from the city's public high schools, coached by Nat Holman, who had been a star of the early professional game. In March 1950, playing at Madison Square Garden, they won the National Invitation Tournament, then the most prestigious in the country. Days later, they won the NCAA tournament too, beating the same team, Bradley University, in both finals. No other team has won both in the same season, and under today's rules, no team can.

Less than a year later, it fell apart. In February 1951, several City College players were arrested. They had taken money from gamblers to shave points: to win by less than the betting line, or to lose games they were expected to win. The scandal spread to other colleges, including Long Island University, New York University, Bradley and Kentucky, and dozens of players were caught up in it.

The players involved were barred from the NBA. City College gave up big-time sports and dropped to the small-college level, where it remains. For years, the double championship was a source of pride the college hardly mentioned. Now it's remembered as both, a glorious season and a warning.
=== DEEPER Sundays at the Triple Nickel | culture
You heard that Paul Robeson, Count Basie and Joe Louis lived at 555 Edgecombe Avenue, about twenty blocks north of here, at 160th Street. People in the neighborhood call it the Triple Nickel. And it still has music in it.

Every Sunday afternoon, for more than thirty years, a pianist and actress named Marjorie Eliot has opened the door of her apartment there, 3F, to anyone who wants to come in, for a free afternoon of jazz. Musicians play in her parlor, chairs fill the rooms and the hallway, and visitors from around the world sit beside neighbors from down the hall. There's no ticket. You can leave a donation if you like.

She began the concerts after the death of her son, as a way to turn grief into something she could share. Reports over the years agree that she has hardly ever missed a Sunday.

If you're here on a Sunday, check the time, then walk up Edgecombe Avenue in the afternoon and follow the sound of the piano. It's a living piece of the Sugar Hill story: music made at home, for whoever comes to listen.
=== DEEPER From Sugar Hill to Rapper's Delight | names
The name Sugar Hill, for the sweet life on this ridge, has traveled far. In 1979, across the Hudson in Englewood, New Jersey, a singer turned record producer named Sylvia Robinson started a record label and named it after this neighborhood: Sugar Hill Records.

That year, she brought together three young rappers, called them the Sugarhill Gang, and released a track nearly fifteen minutes long, called Rapper's Delight. It became the first rap record to reach the American Top 40, and for millions of listeners, it was the first rap they had ever heard. Three years later, the label put out Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five's The Message, a stark portrait of life in a struggling New York.

So the name that once meant the comfortable life of Black Harlem's elite carried the new sound of the Bronx out to the world.
