=== STOP City Hall Park & Newspaper Row
=== DEEPER The Commons: Poles, Prisons and a Fountain | history
Before City Hall was built, this open ground at the edge of town was where colonial New York put the things it didn't want in its crowded streets, and where it came to argue.

In the 1730s the city built its almshouse here, the poorhouse, and a workhouse and a jail soon joined it. One of those buildings, the New Gaol, became something darker during the Revolution. The British used it as the provost prison, where captured American officers and suspected rebels were packed into filthy rooms under a provost marshal named William Cunningham, whom the survivors remembered with hatred for the rest of their lives. After the war the same building was refitted as the city's Hall of Records, and it stood in the park until 1903.

The Commons was also where the city did its politics in the open air. Crowds gathered here to protest the Stamp Act in 1765, and the next year the Sons of Liberty raised a tall pole on the Commons, a liberty pole, to celebrate the act's repeal. British soldiers cut it down. The patriots raised another, and another. Five liberty poles went up here between 1766 and 1776. Look for the tall flagpole on the west side of City Hall, near Broadway. It is a replica of the last of them, put up in 1921.

Now look at City Hall itself. You heard the story about its cheap brownstone back. Today there is no brownstone at all. By the 1950s the soft Massachusetts marble had decayed so badly that the whole building, back included, was refaced in Alabama limestone, copying the original carving.

Finally, find the fountain near the south end of the park. On October 14th, 1842, the city celebrated the arrival of clean water from the Croton River, about forty miles north, through a new aqueduct. A fountain here sent a jet of water high into the air while crowds cheered. For a city that had been drinking from wells and polluted ponds, it was a turning point. Today's fountain was rebuilt in 1999, when the park was restored, in the style of the ones that stood here in the 1800s.
=== DEEPER Two Subways Under Your Feet | underfoot
Twice, New York's subway was born right under this park.

The first time was a stunt. In 1870, an inventor named Alfred Ely Beach, the publisher of the magazine Scientific American, opened a demonstration subway under Broadway, along the west side of the park, from Warren Street to Murray Street. It was one block long. A giant fan blew a single passenger car through the tunnel, then reversed to pull it back. The waiting room had paintings, a grand piano, and a tank of goldfish. A popular story says Beach dug it in secret because Boss Tweed, who had transit schemes of his own, would never have allowed it. Beach did keep the work quiet, but the tale of a secret tunnel dug under Tweed's nose is mostly legend. Either way, the line never won the approval and money it needed to grow, and it closed within a few years. Workers building a real subway under Broadway broke into its forgotten tunnel in 1912.

The second time was for real. On October 27th, 1904, the city's first subway line opened, and its showpiece station was right below City Hall. City Hall station, designed by Heins and LaFarge, had graceful vaulted ceilings of Guastavino tile, skylights, brass chandeliers, and leaded glass. But it sat on a tight curve, and as trains grew longer, the curved platform couldn't serve them safely. Few riders used it anyway, because the Brooklyn Bridge station next door was busier and more convenient. It closed at the end of 1945.

You can still see it. Ride a downtown 6 train to the last stop, Brooklyn Bridge City Hall, and stay on board when everyone gets off. The train turns around through the old loop, and for a few seconds you glide past the lit platform and its arches, a perfect 1904 station that no passenger is allowed to use.
=== DEEPER How the Tweed Ring Worked | politics
The courthouse behind City Hall is the most famous monument to a system of theft, and the system deserves a closer look.

William Magear Tweed rose through the volunteer fire companies and Tammany Hall, the Democratic machine that won elections by doing favors for poor and immigrant voters: a job, a bucket of coal, a word with a judge. By the late 1860s, Tweed and three allies controlled the city's money. Peter Sweeny ran the city treasury and the parks. Richard Connolly, known as Slippery Dick, was the comptroller who approved every payment. And the mayor was Abraham Oakey Hall, a witty dresser known as Elegant Oakey.

The method was simple. Anyone who did business with the city was told to pad the bill, eventually to more than double the real cost, and hand most of the difference back. The courthouse was the masterpiece. Bills for chairs, carpets, thermometers and plastering reached absurd sums. Estimates of what the ring stole in all run from about thirty million to two hundred million dollars, at a time when a laborer earned a dollar or two a day.

It fell apart because of a ledger. In 1871, insiders with a grudge passed copies of the city's books to the New York Times, which published them that July. Thomas Nast's cartoons in Harper's Weekly had already made Tweed a symbol of greed. Tweed is supposed to have said that he didn't care what the papers wrote, because his voters couldn't read, but they could see the pictures. Nobody knows whether he really said it.

Tweed was convicted, escaped from custody in 1875, and fled to Cuba and then Spain, where, the story goes, officials recognized him from one of Nast's cartoons. Sent back to New York, he died in the Ludlow Street Jail in 1878. His courthouse was finished after his fall, and after a careful restoration around 2000, its interior is one of the finest in the city.
=== DEEPER Moon Men, a Pedestal and the Newsies | culture
Park Row was the loudest street in America for much of the nineteenth century, and three of its stories show how the newspaper business worked.

First, the penny press. In 1833, a young printer named Benjamin Day started the Sun and sold it for a penny, when most papers cost six cents. Instead of long political essays, the Sun printed crime, scandal and human interest, sold on the street by boys. In 1835 it ran a series claiming that the astronomer John Herschel, using a giant new telescope, had seen life on the moon, including furry people with bat wings. It was a hoax, mostly the work of a reporter named Richard Adams Locke, and sales soared.

Second, the pedestal. In 1885, the Statue of Liberty sat in crates, because the American committee had run out of money for its base. Joseph Pulitzer used his paper, the World, to shame the rich and appeal to everyone else, promising to print the name of every donor, however small the gift. More than a hundred and twenty thousand people gave, most of them less than a dollar, and the pedestal got built.

Third, the newsboys. In 1899, the World and William Randolph Hearst's Journal refused to undo a price increase they had imposed on the boys who bought papers by the hundred to resell. Thousands of newsboys went on strike for about two weeks, blocking delivery wagons and the Brooklyn Bridge, led by a boy known as Kid Blink. The papers kept their price, but they agreed to buy back the papers the boys couldn't sell, a real win. A century later the story became the Disney film and Broadway musical Newsies.

In the 1890s, the World and the Journal both ran a comic about a bald slum kid in a yellow nightshirt, the Yellow Kid, after Hearst lured his creator away. The tug of war over that cartoon helped give their kind of sensational reporting its name: yellow journalism.
=== STOP The Woolworth Building
=== DEEPER Five and Ten: How Woolworth Changed Shopping | companies
Frank Woolworth's story is the story of how American shopping was reinvented, and it began with a clearance table.

In the 1870s, Woolworth was a clerk at a dry goods store in Watertown, in upstate New York, where his bosses set out a table of odds and ends, all priced at five cents. It sold out. In 1879 Woolworth borrowed money and opened his own five cent store in Utica. It failed within months. His second try, in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, worked, and soon he added a ten cent line.

What made it work was a bundle of ideas we now take for granted. Goods sat on open counters where anyone could pick them up, instead of behind a counter where a clerk had to fetch them. Every price was fixed and marked, so there was no haggling. Woolworth bought in huge quantities, straight from manufacturers, and squeezed costs everywhere. Relatives and former partners opened chains of their own, and in 1912 five of them merged with Woolworth's into a single company with nearly six hundred stores. That is the company that paid for this tower.

The stores' lunch counters later became part of American history in a way Woolworth never imagined. On February 1st, 1960, four Black college students sat down at the whites-only lunch counter of the Woolworth's in Greensboro, North Carolina, and stayed after they were refused service. Within weeks, sit-ins had spread across the South. A section of that counter is now in the Smithsonian.

Woolworth died in 1919, six years after his tower opened. The company he built outlived him by most of a century and spread around the world. In Britain, the Woolworths name survived on high streets until 2009.
=== DEEPER The Race to the Top | architecture
The Woolworth Building was built in a race for height, and the engineering under its Gothic skin is as impressive as the skin.

The first plans, in 1910, called for a building of about four hundred feet. Then they kept growing. Woolworth wanted to beat the Singer Building, a few blocks south, and then the Metropolitan Life tower on Madison Square, which at seven hundred feet was the tallest building in the world. Cass Gilbert's final design passed both, at about seven hundred ninety two feet.

To stand a tower that tall on lower Manhattan's soft ground, the engineers, led by Gunvald Aus, carried the foundations down through sand and groundwater to bedrock, more than a hundred feet below the street. They used pneumatic caissons, huge boxes pumped full of compressed air to hold back the water, in which men dug in shifts and risked the bends. Above ground, the steel frame was stiffened with heavy arched bracing against the wind, the real enemy of tall buildings.

Then Gilbert dressed the steel in terracotta: hollow blocks of glazed clay, cast in molds, light enough to hang on a frame and easy to shape into tracery, pinnacles and gargoyles. It was also fireproof, a big selling point in an age of great fires. The main color is a warm cream, with touches of blue, green and gold higher up, so the detail would read from the street far below.

Inside, the tower ran its own power plant and some of the fastest elevators of the day. It was a small city: Woolworth's headquarters, a bank, shops, offices for hundreds of companies, and a swimming pool in the basement. For decades an observation deck near the top drew paying visitors, until it closed during the Second World War.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Marble, Mosaics and an Emperor | look
If you can get into the lobby, on a tour or with a peek through the doors, here is what to look for, beyond the famous little caricatures.

The walls are lined with veined marble from the Greek island of Skyros. Overhead, the vaulted ceilings glitter with glass mosaics in a style borrowed from Byzantine churches, and a stained glass skylight lights the middle of the hall. At the top of the grand marble stair, on the mezzanine, are two painted murals by the sculptor and painter Paul Jennewein, called Labor and Commerce.

The carved brackets where the arches spring from the walls hold more faces than the three you heard about. Besides Woolworth with his nickels and Gilbert with his model, there is the engineer, Gunvald Aus, measuring a girder, and Edward Hogan, the real estate agent who quietly bought up the lots for the site. Medieval masons sometimes carved their patrons and themselves into cathedrals, and Gilbert's sculptors were in on the joke.

Woolworth's own office was on the twenty fourth floor, and it says a lot about him. He admired Napoleon, and he had the room done in the French Empire style, with furniture in the manner of Napoleon's court and a portrait of the emperor. A shopkeeper who had sold things for a nickel liked to work like an emperor.

Outside, stand back across Broadway and look at the facade. Around 1980, in a long restoration, thousands of worn and cracked terracotta pieces were replaced, many with cast concrete made to match. From the sidewalk you can't tell which is which, and that was the point.
=== STOP The Municipal Building & Brooklyn Bridge Gateway
=== DEEPER The Great Consolidation | politics
The Municipal Building exists because of a political decision that's easy to forget: until 1898, New York was a much smaller city.

Before then, New York meant Manhattan and part of the Bronx. Brooklyn was a separate city, one of the largest in the country. Queens was a patchwork of towns and farms, and Staten Island was a rural county. The man who pushed hardest to join them was Andrew Haswell Green, a lawyer and planner who had led the commission that built Central Park. Green argued that the harbor, the railroads and the water supply made the region one place, and that only one government could plan it.

In 1894, voters were asked whether they wanted to consolidate. The vote was advisory, and in Brooklyn it was agonizingly close: yes won by fewer than three hundred votes, out of nearly a hundred and thirty thousand. Many Brooklynites never forgave it, and some still call it the Great Mistake of 1898.

On January 1st, 1898, Greater New York was born, with five boroughs and well over three million people, second only to London among the cities of the world. Its government had to grow fast, and its offices were scattered all over lower Manhattan in rented space. The city needed a headquarters to match its new size, and this is it. Finished in 1914, the Municipal Building gathered dozens of agencies under one roof, with a subway station in its basement.

Look up again at the statue on top, Civic Fame, holding a crown with five turrets, one for each borough. The building was a monument to an idea that had won by a few hundred votes.
=== DEEPER Audrey Munson, the Woman on the Roof | people
The gilded figure on top of the Municipal Building, Civic Fame, was made by the sculptor Adolph Weinman, and his model is usually said to have been Audrey Munson. Her story is one of the strangest in the city's history.

Munson was spotted on Fifth Avenue as a teenager, around 1906, by a photographer, and soon became the most sought-after artists' model in New York. Sculptors called her the American Venus. You have probably walked past her without knowing it. She is said to be the model for the gilded figure on top of the Maine Monument at Columbus Circle and the reclining woman of the Straus Memorial on the Upper West Side, along with dozens of other statues around the country.

She also acted in silent films. In one of them, Inspiration, in 1915, she appeared nude, which is often described as a first for an American feature film.

Then her life fell apart. In 1919, the landlord of the boarding house where she and her mother lived, a doctor named Walter Wilkins, murdered his wife, reportedly because he was obsessed with Munson. She was never accused of anything, but the scandal ended her career. After years of poverty and illness, she was committed to a state psychiatric hospital in upstate New York in 1931, at the age of thirty nine. She stayed there for sixty five years and died in 1996, at a hundred and four.

Her grave went unmarked for decades, until admirers gave her a headstone in 2016. Her face and figure, meanwhile, never left the city. When you look up at Civic Fame, holding the five boroughs aloft, you may be looking at her.
=== DEEPER The Bridge Terminal That Vanished | history
Stand near the foot of the Brooklyn Bridge and imagine a great train shed here, because for about sixty years, this was one of the busiest transit hubs in the world.

The bridge was built for more than walkers and carriages. Starting in 1883, cable-hauled trains shuttled across it between a terminal here at Park Row and another in Brooklyn. Later, elevated trains from Brooklyn ran straight over the bridge into the terminal, and trolley cars crossed on tracks of their own. At rush hour, the Manhattan terminal was famous for its crush of commuters climbing the stairs.

Cars changed everything. The elevated trains stopped crossing in 1944, the trolleys a few years later, and the old terminal was torn down. In the early 1950s the bridge was rebuilt for cars alone, with three lanes in each direction, and its approaches here were widened into the ramps you see snaking around you. Joseph Pulitzer's World Building, with its gilded dome, once the tallest building in the city, was demolished in 1955 to make room for one of them.

The walkway stayed, raised above the traffic, as John Roebling had designed it. For decades walkers and cyclists squeezed past each other on it, until 2021, when the city turned one car lane into a protected bike lane and gave the promenade back to people on foot.

One more change happened after September 11th, 2001. Police headquarters stands just east of here, and Park Row, the old Newspaper Row, was closed to private cars for security. Walking it today, you are on what was once the noisiest street of the nineteenth century, and is now one of the quietest in lower Manhattan.
=== STOP African Burial Ground
=== DEEPER The Fight Over the Excavation | history
The memorial here exists because New Yorkers fought for it, and that fight changed how America treats the graves of its enslaved ancestors.

When human remains began turning up in 1991, the federal agency building the tower, the General Services Administration, was under pressure to stay on schedule. Archaeologists worked fast, sometimes in rough conditions, and many Black New Yorkers were alarmed. Remains were being removed and stored with little consultation with the descendant community, and no one had a plan for what would happen to them. Protests grew. Activists, clergy, scholars and politicians, including Mayor David Dinkins, called for a halt.

In 1992, Congressman Gus Savage of Illinois, who chaired the House subcommittee that oversaw federal buildings, held a hearing and helped stop work on a planned pavilion on part of the site, so that part of the burial ground was left undisturbed. The remains already excavated went to Howard University in Washington, where a team led by the anthropologist Michael Blakey studied them for about a decade, with the descendant community helping to shape the questions the research asked.

In the fall of 2003, the ancestors came home. A procession left Howard University and traveled through Baltimore, Wilmington, Philadelphia, Newark and Jersey City, with ceremonies along the way, before crossing the harbor to Manhattan. The remains were placed in hand-carved mahogany coffins made in Ghana and lined with kente cloth, and reburied here on October 4th, 2003, beneath the grassy mounds in the memorial.

The site became a national monument in 2006, and the memorial was dedicated the following year. If the visitor center inside 290 Broadway is open, it tells this story in much more detail.
=== DEEPER What the Ancestors' Bones Tell | people
The researchers who studied the remains here called the people buried here the ancestors, and their bones told stories that no written record kept.

Start with the children. About four in ten of the people found here were children, many of them infants, a sign of how hard life was for enslaved families in the colonial city. Among the adults, the skeletons showed the marks of brutal labor: enlarged and torn muscle attachments, spines compressed by heavy loads, and joints worn far beyond their years. Many showed signs of malnutrition and disease.

Some graves hold individual stories. One young woman, known by the number of her grave as Burial 25, had a musket ball lodged in her rib cage, and her face had been struck with great force. She seems to have died violently. Another woman, Burial 340, had front teeth filed into points, a custom in parts of Africa, which suggests she was born there and survived the Middle Passage. She was buried wearing a strand of glass beads and cowrie shells around her waist.

Most of the dead were wrapped in shrouds held with pins and laid in wooden coffins with their heads to the west. Some were buried with coins over their eyes or with shells, as offerings. And on the lid of one coffin, Burial 101, someone had hammered iron tacks into the heart-shaped design that many see as the Sankofa symbol.

Chemical tests of teeth helped show who had been born in Africa and who in New York, and the answer was both: a community of Africans and their New York born children and grandchildren, burying their dead together, outside the town that depended on their work, for about a hundred years.
=== DEEPER Half-Freedom: The Land of the Blacks | history
Long before this was a burial ground, the land north of the Dutch town was farmed by Africans who had won a limited kind of freedom, and their story is one of the most surprising in early New York.

In 1644, eleven enslaved men owned by the Dutch West India Company petitioned for their freedom. They had worked for the company for close to twenty years, building its fort and clearing its land. The director, Willem Kieft, granted what historians call half-freedom. They could live as free people, marry, own land and work for themselves, but they owed the company a yearly payment of grain and a hog, and labor whenever it was demanded, and the grant said their children would remain enslaved.

The company gave these men and others farms on the land north of town, roughly from where you are standing up toward Washington Square, along the road that became the Bowery. The area became known as the Land of the Blacks. Their names survive in the records: Paulo Angola, Simon Congo, Anthony Portuguese, Big Manuel and Little Manuel, names that point to Angola, the Congo, and the Portuguese Atlantic world those men had been taken from. Some historians think the Dutch placed them there on purpose, as a buffer between the town and the Lenape after the bloody wars of the 1640s.

Under English rule, things got worse. After the revolt of 1712, a new law barred free Black people from owning land, and by then most of the farms had already passed into white hands. Slavery in the city grew harsher, and this ground, just outside the town, became the place where its Africans, free and enslaved, buried their dead.
=== STOP Foley Square & Five Points
=== DEEPER The Pond Under the Courthouses | underfoot
Everything around you stands on a lost lake, and the ground still remembers it.

The Collect Pond was fed by underground springs, and next to it rose a hill later called Bunker Hill. The Dutch called the pond the Kalck Hoek, probably for the heaps of oyster shells on its shore, and the English turned that into the Collect.

By the 1790s, tanneries, breweries and slaughterhouses lined its banks and drained into it. One plan would have cleaned it up and made it the centerpiece of a park. Instead, starting around 1811, the city cut down Bunker Hill, dumped the earth into the pond, and filled it in. The new land was cheap, but it didn't stay put. Buried springs and rotting fill made cellars flood and buildings sag, and anyone who could afford to leave did. That's how Five Points began.

The city built its new jail on the filled pond in 1838, a massive building in the style of an Egyptian temple. New Yorkers called it the Tombs, for its look, and people joked that it was sinking into the old pond, which it was. Its successor, a few steps north on Centre Street, still goes by the same nickname.

Now a legend. Across Centre Street is Collect Pond Park, rebuilt in 2014 with a shallow pool to recall the water that was here. Its history includes a famous story: that in 1796 the inventor John Fitch tested a steamboat on the Collect, years before Robert Fulton's success on the Hudson, while a young Fulton watched. It's a wonderful story, but it seems to have been published decades later by Fitch's admirers, and no evidence from the time has been found to back it up. Even the park's own sign says so.
=== DEEPER Gangs of New York, Checked | legends
Five Points' reputation comes largely from one book, Herbert Asbury's The Gangs of New York, published in 1928 and later the basis of Martin Scorsese's film. Asbury was a gifted storyteller. How well do his stories hold up?

Take the Old Brewery, a former brewery on the edge of the filled pond that had become a crumbling tenement. Asbury wrote that for fifteen years it averaged a murder a night. The building was real and wretched, but there's no record of anything like that many murders. In 1852, a group of Methodist women bought it, tore it down while crowds watched, and built a mission on the site.

The gangs were real too, but not quite the armies Asbury described. The Plug Uglies were mostly a Baltimore gang. The Dead Rabbits seem to have been as much a newspaper label as a club. What is well documented is the Dead Rabbits Riot of July 4th, 1857, when gangs tied to rival political factions fought for two days around Bayard Street, leaving several people dead. Gangs like these were the street muscle of politics. They guarded polling places, scared off rival voters, and fought for their patrons, often in the service of Tammany Hall.

The historian Tyler Anbinder, digging through census records, court files and savings bank ledgers, found a different Five Points. Most residents worked hard at legal jobs, as laborers, seamstresses, shoemakers and peddlers, and Irish immigrants here saved and sent remarkable sums home to their families in Ireland. Crime and misery were real, but so was a community.

Even Abraham Lincoln came to see it. In 1860, on the trip to New York that included his famous speech at Cooper Union, he visited the Sunday school at the Five Points House of Industry, a reform mission, and gave a short talk to the children.
=== DEEPER Master Juba and the Dance Halls | culture
In the 1840s, Five Points had a nightlife that drew sailors, slummers and famous visitors, and in one of its dance halls, American dance was being invented.

The best known hall was Almack's, run by a Black proprietor named Pete Williams on Orange Street, now Baxter Street. It was named, as a joke, after a very exclusive ballroom in London. In 1842, Charles Dickens was taken there on his tour of the slum, and in his book American Notes he described a young Black dancer whose feet seemed to be everywhere at once, spinning, leaping, shuffling and finishing with a flourish to roars from the crowd. Most historians believe that dancer was William Henry Lane, known as Master Juba.

Juba was then a teenager, probably born free around 1825. In 1844 he met John Diamond, a celebrated white dancer from the minstrel stage, in a series of challenge matches billed as contests for the championship, and Juba came away with a reputation as the best dancer of his time. He is believed to have blended Irish jigs and reels with African American step dancing, and that mix of fast, percussive footwork is counted among the roots of tap dance.

He was also one of the first Black performers to tour with a white minstrel company and get top billing. In 1848 he went to London, where critics raved. He died there, still young, around 1852.

The dance halls were rough places, but they were also among the few spaces in the city where Black and Irish New Yorkers, sailors and laborers, drank and danced together. Respectable New York was horrified, and fascinated, and kept coming downtown to look.
=== DEEPER Foley Square: Big Tom, Spies and a Monument | look
Look around Foley Square and let it tell you a few more stories.

The square is named for Thomas Foley, known as Big Tom, a saloon keeper and Tammany Hall district leader in the early 1900s. He was the political mentor of Al Smith, the Lower East Side boy who became governor of New York and, in 1928, the first Catholic nominated for president by a major party. The little park in the middle of the square is named for a very different figure, Thomas Paine.

The courthouse with the gilded pyramid, now named for Thurgood Marshall, has seen some of the most famous trials of the twentieth century. In 1949, eleven leaders of the American Communist Party were tried here in a trial that lasted most of the year. Alger Hiss, the State Department official accused of spying for the Soviet Union, was convicted of perjury here in 1950. And in 1951, Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were tried here for passing atomic secrets to the Soviets, convicted, and sentenced to death. Historians still argue over the case, especially over how much Ethel knew.

Now find the tall black granite sculpture in the middle of the square. It's called Triumph of the Human Spirit, by the artist Lorenzo Pace, unveiled in 2000. Its shape comes from a traditional antelope headdress of the Bamana people of Mali, a symbol of farming and work, and it rises from a base that suggests the hull of a ship. It honors the Africans buried at the African Burial Ground, a block away, and the millions carried across the Atlantic. Pace has said it was inspired by a family heirloom: a lock and key that had been used on his great-great-grandfather, enslaved in Alabama.
=== STOP Mott, Pell & Doyers Streets
=== DEEPER Exclusion, Laundries and Paper Sons | history
To understand old Chinatown, you have to understand the law that shaped it for sixty years.

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred Chinese laborers from entering the country, and later laws tightened it. Chinese immigrants already here couldn't become citizens. Only a few groups were exempt, including merchants, students and diplomats, so the community stayed small and overwhelmingly male. Many men lived for decades with their wives and children on the other side of the Pacific.

People found ways around it. In 1906, the San Francisco earthquake and fire destroyed that city's birth records. Many Chinese men then claimed to have been born in San Francisco, which made them citizens, and citizens could bring in their children. Some reported the births of sons who didn't exist, then sold those places to young men in China, who memorized thick coaching books about their new families before facing interrogation by immigration officers. They were called paper sons. Many Chinese American families today carry surnames that were bought on paper.

Shut out of most trades, Chinese New Yorkers built businesses where they could, above all hand laundries, thousands of them across the city. In 1933, the city proposed licensing rules that would have required laundry owners to be citizens, which the law barred most Chinese from becoming, and to post a large bond. Laundry workers formed their own group, the Chinese Hand Laundry Alliance, hired lawyers, and got the rules softened, an early act of Chinese American civil rights organizing.

Exclusion ended in 1943, when China was an ally in the war, but the new quota allowed only a hundred and five Chinese immigrants a year. The real change came with the immigration reform of 1965.
=== DEEPER The Tong Wars, Fact and Legend | legends
Doyers Street's nickname, the Bloody Angle, comes from real violence. But tong war stories have grown in the telling, so let's sort them out.

The tongs were sworn brotherhoods. In New York the two biggest were the On Leong, whose stronghold was Mott Street, and the Hip Sing, on Pell Street. The On Leong boss, Tom Lee, a merchant who had been made a deputy sheriff, was called the Mayor of Chinatown in the newspapers. His great rival was a young Hip Sing leader known as Mock Duck. They fought over the neighborhood's gambling halls and other rackets, and over who would collect the payments to protect them.

The worst single attack is well documented. On August 7th, 1905, during a crowded performance at the Chinese Theatre on Doyers Street, a burst of firecrackers distracted the audience, and gunmen among them opened fire on On Leong men. At least three people were killed. A few years later, the theater was turned into a rescue mission.

Now the legends. Mock Duck was said to wear a shirt of chain mail, and to fight by crouching in the street, shutting his eyes, and firing two pistols in every direction. Those stories come from reporters and later writers who loved a colorful villain, and they may be partly true. Then there are the secret tunnels. You may hear that tong gunmen escaped through tunnels under Doyers Street. There is a real underground passage at the bend of the street, now a corridor of small shops that leads toward Chatham Square. But no one has shown that it was a gangsters' escape route.

Truces were signed in the early 1900s, the violence faded by the 1930s, and the tongs settled into life as merchant associations. Their buildings still stand on Mott and Pell Streets.
=== DEEPER Chop Suey to Soup Dumplings | food
Chinatown has been feeding outsiders for well over a century, and its menus are a history of the neighborhood.

The first Chinese food in New York was Cantonese, because nearly all the early immigrants came from the Taishan area of Guangdong. Around 1900, New Yorkers went wild for chop suey, a stir-fry of meat and vegetables. A popular legend says it was invented in New York for the Chinese statesman Li Hongzhang, who visited in 1896. It wasn't. A similar dish of odds and ends already existed in the Taishan region, and Chinese American cooks were serving it before Li arrived. But his much-publicized visit helped set off a craze, and chop suey houses spread across the city.

Nom Wah, at the bend of Doyers Street, opened in 1920 as a bakery and tea parlor known for its mooncakes and almond cookies. It still serves dim sum, the Cantonese meal of small plates eaten with tea.

After 1965, newcomers arrived from Hong Kong, Taiwan, and later Fujian and other provinces, and the menus exploded. In the 1970s, New Yorkers discovered the fiery cooking of Hunan and Sichuan, and menus filled with General Tso's chicken, a dish created in Taiwan by a Hunanese chef named Peng Chang-kuei and reinvented, much sweeter, in New York. Fujianese immigrants opened restaurants and markets along East Broadway, and later waves brought hand-pulled noodles and soup dumplings.

As you walk, read the windows. Roast ducks and strips of red-glazed char siu pork hang in rows. Tanks hold live fish and crabs. Bakeries sell egg tarts and pineapple buns, which contain no pineapple at all. They're named for their crackly sugar crust, which looks like a pineapple's skin. Every generation that settled here is still on the menu.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: An Old House, an Arch and a Statue | look
Three landmarks around here bookend Chinatown's history. Look for them as you walk toward the Bowery.

First, at the corner of the Bowery and Pell Street, find the narrow brick house with dormer windows at 18 Bowery. It's the Edward Mooney House, built in the 1780s by a meat merchant, just after the British left New York. It is the oldest surviving townhouse in Manhattan, and it has been a store, a hotel, a saloon, a pool hall and a bank. When it was built, the land around it was still mostly farms.

Second, at Chatham Square, look at the Kimlau Memorial Arch. Its architect, Poy Gum Lee, was born in New York, spent years working in China, and helped complete the great mausoleum of Sun Yat-sen, the founder of the Chinese republic, in Nanjing. Back home, he became Chinatown's architect, and the arch, finished in the early 1960s, is his modern take on a traditional Chinese gateway. It honors Benjamin Ralph Kimlau, a Chinese American bomber pilot killed in the Pacific in 1944, and all the Chinese Americans who died in the war.

Third, the statue of Lin Zexu, the Qing official who tried to stop the British opium trade. It was put up in 1997 by the Fujianese community, because Lin was from Fujian, and its base reads, in English and Chinese, Pioneer in the war against drugs. It went up the same year Britain handed Hong Kong back to China, the territory Britain had taken in the Opium War that Lin's crackdown set off.

On the way, notice the red and green balconies and the pagoda-style roofs added to ordinary tenements in the twentieth century. Some were built with pride, and some to give tourists the Chinatown they expected to see.

=== DEEPER Chinatown Organizes | politics
Right here at the foot of the Manhattan Bridge, two protests changed how Chinatown saw itself.

Look for the tall brick apartment tower and the statue of Confucius at the corner of the Bowery and Division Street. That's Confucius Plaza, a big housing complex finished in the mid 1970s. When it was going up, in 1974, almost none of the construction workers were Asian American, on a project built in the middle of Chinatown. Young activists formed a group called Asian Americans for Equal Employment and picketed the site for weeks, and some were arrested. In the end, the contractor agreed to hire Asian American workers. The group grew into Asian Americans for Equality, still an important organization in the neighborhood. The statue of Confucius, a gift from the community, came a couple of years later.

The second protest was much bigger. In 1982, Chinatown had hundreds of garment shops employing around twenty thousand workers, nearly all of them immigrant women, sewing clothes for the fashion industry uptown. When the shop owners refused to sign the union's new contract, the workers of the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union, Local 23-25, took to the streets. On June 24th, 1982, as many as twenty thousand of them rallied in Columbus Park and marched through Chinatown. It was one of the largest protests in the neighborhood's history, by women who had often been dismissed as too timid to organize. Within weeks, the holdout shops signed.

Both protests mark the moment when a neighborhood that had long let old merchant associations speak for it began to speak for itself, in English and Chinese, out in the street.
=== DEEPER The Bridge That Taught Engineers a Lesson | history
The Manhattan Bridge looks like a sturdy workhorse, but its design launched a line of ideas that ended in one of the most famous engineering failures in history.

The engineer behind its final design was Leon Moisseiff, an immigrant from Riga who had come to New York as a young man. Moisseiff championed deflection theory, a mathematical way of working out how a suspension bridge's cables and deck share the load. It showed that a long, heavy deck didn't need to be as stiff as engineers had assumed, so bridges could be lighter, slimmer and cheaper. The Manhattan Bridge, opened in 1909, was among the first designed this way, and the approach spread around the country.

Each new bridge pushed it further. In 1940, a bridge across the Tacoma Narrows in Washington State, built to Moisseiff's design, opened with an extraordinarily thin deck. In the wind it rose and fell so much that people called it Galloping Gertie. Four months after it opened, in a wind of about forty miles an hour, it twisted itself apart and fell into the water, all caught on film. Engineers learned to take aerodynamics seriously, and Moisseiff's reputation never recovered.

The Manhattan Bridge had a problem of its own. Its subway tracks run along the outer edges of the deck, so when a train crosses, that side dips. Decades of trains twisted the deck and cracked the steel. Starting in 1982, the city spent more than twenty years and hundreds of millions of dollars rebuilding it, closing subway tracks for years at a time. Ride across on the train and you may still feel the deck lean.
=== DEEPER Canal Street's Bazaar | companies
Canal Street has been a street of bargains for as long as anyone can remember, and its merchandise tells the story of the city's economy.

For much of the twentieth century, the stretch of Canal west of here was a famous jumble of surplus and hardware shops. You could buy war surplus, plastic sheeting, electronics, tools, and odd lots of almost anything. Artists from nearby SoHo shopped here, and the art supply store Pearl Paint, at 308 Canal Street, served generations of painters and students from the 1930s until it closed in 2014.

Closer to the Bowery, Canal became a jewelry district, with rows of small shops glittering with gold. And by the 1990s, Canal Street was the city's best-known market for counterfeits: fake designer handbags, watches and sunglasses, sold from folding tables and from back rooms behind ordinary storefronts. Brands sued sellers and even landlords, the police staged raids, and much of the trade moved out of sight and online. You may still be offered a handbag in a whisper.

Now look at the corner of the Bowery and Canal for the bank building with the dome, at 58 Bowery. It was built in 1924 for the Citizens Savings Bank, a bank for working people, designed by Clarence Brazer in a style borrowed from the Italian Renaissance, with a dome you can see from far down the street. It became a city landmark in 2011 and is still a bank. Buildings like this were meant to promise permanence and safety to small savers, many of them immigrants, who might otherwise have kept their money sewn into a mattress.
