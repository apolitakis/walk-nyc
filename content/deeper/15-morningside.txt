=== STOP Morningside Park
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Two Friends Holding Hands | look
At the foot of the park, where Manhattan Avenue meets Morningside Avenue at 114th Street, a bronze pair stands on a stone pedestal: George Washington and the Marquis de Lafayette, in uniform, clasping hands.

The sculptor was Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, who designed the Statue of Liberty, and if you took the Union Square tour, you may have met his Lafayette there. This group is a twin. The original was commissioned by Joseph Pulitzer, the publisher of the New York World, the same Pulitzer who raised the money for Liberty's pedestal, as a gift from Americans to France. It was unveiled in Paris in 1895, in the Place des États-Unis.

New York's copy came five years later, in April 1900. It was the gift of Charles Broadway Rouss, a merchant who had made his fortune selling dry goods on Broadway, and who reportedly loved the street so much that he took its name as his middle name.

Look at the two faces. The friendship was real. Lafayette arrived in America in 1777 as a nineteen year old volunteer, and Washington, who had no children of his own, treated him almost as a son. Lafayette's own father had been killed in battle when the boy was not yet two. Years later, Lafayette named his only son Georges Washington.
=== DEEPER The General in the President's House | people
You heard that the 1968 protesters took over the president's office in Low Library. Twenty years earlier, Columbia had a very different kind of president. Look up to the top of the cliff, at Morningside Drive and 116th Street. The house on that corner is the official home of Columbia's presidents, and from 1948 to the start of 1953, it was the home of General Dwight Eisenhower.

Eisenhower had led the Allied armies in Europe in the Second World War, and he was perhaps the most admired man in America. Columbia's trustees offered him the presidency, and he took it. He was an odd fit. He was a soldier, not a scholar, and many professors never warmed to him, or to his friends among oilmen and businessmen. A story often told on campus has the physicist Isidor Rabi, a Nobel Prize winner, correcting him when he spoke of the faculty as employees of the university: the faculty, Rabi said, are the university.

Still, Eisenhower left his mark. In 1950 he founded the American Assembly, a forum where leaders from business, government and universities met to work through national problems, and he called it the most important step he took as Columbia's president. Within months, President Truman called him back to Europe to command the new NATO forces, and Columbia gave him leave. In 1952 he ran for president of the United States, and he won. He resigned from Columbia the day before his inauguration. Six years later, as president, he came back uptown to break ground for Lincoln Center, as you may have heard on the Upper West Side tour.
=== DEEPER Across 125th Street | politics
You heard that Columbia's plan for a gym in this park died in 1968. Forty years later, the university looked north instead, across 125th Street, to Manhattanville, a valley of warehouses, garages and auto shops in West Harlem. In the 2000s, Columbia set out to build a new campus there, on seventeen acres, in a project budgeted at more than six billion dollars.

Most owners sold. A few refused, among them Nick Sprayregen, whose family's Tuck-It-Away storage warehouses stood on the site. The state agreed to take their land by eminent domain, after declaring the area blighted, and the owners went to court. In December 2009, a divided appeals court ruled for them. In June 2010, the state's highest court unanimously reversed that ruling, and the United States Supreme Court declined to hear the case.

Columbia signed a community benefits agreement with local groups, promising about one hundred and fifty million dollars for West Harlem over the years, and it designed the new campus to be more open than the old one. The first buildings, by the architect Renzo Piano, opened in 2017, with glass-walled ground floors meant to welcome the neighborhood in.

Some neighbors see Manhattanville as the gym fight ending better this time. Others see the same story, told again. Either way, the cliff you're standing beneath is no longer the only line between Columbia and Harlem.
=== STOP The Cathedral of St. John the Divine
=== DEEPER The Chapel That Left | history
Before Ralph Adams Cram turned this cathedral Gothic, it held a jewel in a very different style. At the 1893 World's Fair in Chicago, Louis Comfort Tiffany showed off an entire chapel, a glittering Byzantine interior of mosaics, colored glass and carved stone, designed to show what his studios could do. Crowds lined up to see it.

A wealthy widow, Celia Whipple Wallace, bought the chapel for fifty thousand dollars and gave it to this cathedral, which was just rising. It was installed in the crypt, beneath the eastern end, and it was one of the first parts of the building to be used.

But when the design turned Gothic, the Byzantine glitter fell out of favor, and the chapel was left in the basement, neglected. In 1916, Tiffany bought it back and moved it to Laurelton Hall, his estate on Long Island. Laurelton Hall burned in 1957, and pieces of the chapel ended up in storage and in packing crates. They were finally gathered and reassembled at the Charles Hosmer Morse Museum of American Art in Winter Park, Florida, where the chapel opened to the public in 1999, for the first time since the Chicago fair.

So one of this cathedral's first treasures now glows about a thousand miles to the south. If you're ever in central Florida, you can stand in the room New Yorkers once worshipped in, in the crypt of this cathedral.
=== DEEPER The Man on the Wire | people
You may have heard on the Financial District tour about Philippe Petit, the French high-wire artist who walked between the Twin Towers in 1974. Petit has a second New York home: this cathedral.

Not long after the towers, Petit strung a wire inside the cathedral and walked high above the nave without permission. The cathedral's dean, James Parks Morton, made him artist in residence on the spot, reportedly to keep the police from arresting him. He has held the title for decades.

In September 1982, construction on the cathedral started up again after some forty years, and Petit opened the celebrations in his own way. He walked a wire stretched from a building across Amsterdam Avenue to the cathedral's west front, about a hundred and fifty feet above the street, while the crowd watched from below. He has walked inside the cathedral several times since, including in 1992, for the hundredth anniversary of the cornerstone, and in August 2024, at the age of seventy four, he performed here again, in a show marking fifty years since his walk between the towers.

Morton, his patron, ran the cathedral from 1972 until 1996, and filled it with artists, musicians, scientists and causes. The Blessing of the Animals you heard about is part of the spirit he brought: a cathedral that belongs to the whole city, not just its Episcopalians.
=== DEEPER Farewells and Poets | culture
The cathedral's vast nave has made it the city's stage for its biggest goodbyes. On May 27th, 1974, Duke Ellington's funeral was held here. More than twelve thousand people came, filling the nave and the streets outside, and Ella Fitzgerald sang. If you heard about Ellington on the Fifth Avenue and Central Park tours, this is where his story in New York ended.

In May 1990, the memorial service for Jim Henson, the creator of the Muppets, filled the cathedral with more than five thousand mourners. Henson had asked, it's said, that no one wear black. Big Bird came, and sang It's Not Easy Being Green, Kermit's song, and many in the cathedral wept.

The writers are remembered here too. Since 1984, the cathedral has kept an American Poets' Corner, modeled on the famous one in Westminster Abbey in London, where the names of great American writers are carved in stone. The first, in 1984, included Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson. Look for it in a side bay of the nave, if the cathedral is open.

And look up as you leave the crossing, where the great central tower was meant to rise. Instead there's a dome of Guastavino tile, the thin, fireproof tiles you may remember from Grand Central on the Midtown tour. It was built in 1909 as a temporary covering, until the tower could be finished. More than a century later, it's still there.
=== DEEPER The Orphans' House | names
Long before the cathedral, these grounds belonged to an orphanage, and its building is still here. On the cathedral grounds, look for a much older building in the Greek Revival style, with a portico of stone columns. It was finished in 1843 as the Leake and Watts Orphan House, designed by Ithiel Town, and it's the oldest building on Morningside Heights.

The orphanage's double name hides a story. As it's usually told, a wealthy New Yorker named John George Leake, who had no children, left his fortune to the son of a friend, Robert Watts, on the condition that the young man take the name Leake. The heir died soon after, and his father, John Watts, used the inheritance, and money of his own, to found a home for orphans, carrying both families' names.

When the diocese bought the land for its cathedral, the orphanage moved north to Yonkers, where its successor still works with children today. The old building stayed behind, with the cathedral rising around it.
=== STOP Columbia University
=== DEEPER The Buckwheat Field | history
You heard in the introduction to this walk that Washington's army won a small victory here in September 1776, at the Battle of Harlem Heights. Here's how it went.

It was the morning of September 16th, the day after the British had landed at Kips Bay and sent the Americans fleeing north in disarray. Washington sent a company of scouts, Knowlton's Rangers, under Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Knowlton, to probe the British lines. They ran into British light infantry and fell back. As the British came on, their buglers sounded the call a fox hunter blows when the fox is on the run, a deliberate insult. One of Washington's officers wrote that nothing had ever stung him like it.

Washington set a trap: a feint from the front, while Knowlton's men circled around to hit the British from the side. The flanking attack went in too early, and both Knowlton and Major Andrew Leitch were mortally wounded. But the fight grew, and in a buckwheat field in the blocks where Barnard College now stands, up toward 120th Street, the Americans drove back the British regulars. Then Washington called off his men before British reinforcements could arrive. For an army that had only known retreat, it was a huge lift.

On the Broadway side of the campus, on the wall of Mathematics Hall, look for a bronze plaque placed in 1897 by the Sons of the Revolution, with figures of Knowlton and Leitch. And one more connection: Knowlton's Rangers had included a young captain named Nathan Hale, who had set out on a spying mission a few days before the battle. As you may have heard on the Financial District tour, the British hanged him six days after it.
=== DEEPER The College Across Broadway | people
Look across Broadway at the gates of Barnard College. In the 1880s, Columbia would not admit women. Its president, Frederick Barnard, argued for years that it should, and when he died in 1889, the trustees still said no.

That same year, a young New Yorker named Annie Nathan Meyer, who had been allowed to take Columbia's exams but not to attend its classes, raised the money to found a separate college for women, affiliated with Columbia, and named it for Barnard. There was a twist in her politics: Meyer opposed giving women the vote, while her own sister, Maud Nathan, was a leading suffragist.

Barnard's most famous student of the 1920s came by way of Meyer's help. Zora Neale Hurston arrived in 1925, the college's only Black student at the time. She joked that she was Barnard's sacred black cow. She studied anthropology with Franz Boas, the great Columbia professor, who sent her back to the South to collect the stories, songs and folk beliefs of Black communities. That work became her book Mules and Men, and it fed the novels, like Their Eyes Were Watching God, that made her one of the great American writers. She graduated in 1928, the first Black woman known to have graduated from Barnard.

Today Barnard is still an independent women's college, with its own faculty and president, but its students take classes across Broadway, and the gates stay open both ways.
=== DEEPER The Beats Meet | culture
As you may have heard on the Greenwich Village tour, the Beat writers made their name downtown in the 1950s. They found each other up here, in the 1940s.

Jack Kerouac came to Columbia in 1940 on a football scholarship, broke his leg in his first season, and drifted away from the team and the college. Allen Ginsberg arrived in 1943, a seventeen year old from New Jersey who planned to become a labor lawyer. Through a charismatic student named Lucien Carr, they met each other, and an older friend of Carr's from Saint Louis, William Burroughs. They spent long nights talking at the West End, a bar on Broadway near 114th Street.

In August 1944, the circle was shattered. Carr killed David Kammerer, an older man who had followed him obsessively for years, in Riverside Park, and dumped the body in the Hudson. Kerouac was arrested as a material witness for helping him get rid of evidence, and Carr pleaded guilty to manslaughter. Kerouac and Burroughs wrote a novel about it, And the Hippos Were Boiled in Their Tanks, which wasn't published until 2008. Ginsberg was later suspended for scrawling obscenities in the grime of his dorm window, but he came back and graduated in 1948.

Out of those years came the friendships, and the restlessness, that produced On the Road and Howl.
=== STOP Pupin Hall & the Atomic Age
=== DEEPER The Football Team and the Graphite | history
You heard that Fermi worked here on chain reactions. To keep one going, you have to slow the neutrons down, and Fermi's material of choice was graphite, the stuff in pencil leads, in enormous quantities. In 1941 and 1942, in Schermerhorn Hall, a few steps from here, his team stacked tons of graphite bricks and uranium into tall piles and measured how the neutrons multiplied.

The work was heavy and filthy, and the physicists weren't built for it. So, as Fermi later told the story, the university hired members of the football team, strong young men who hauled the graphite around as if it were nothing. In 1942 the work moved to Chicago, where Fermi's team built the first nuclear reactor in a squash court under the stands of the university's football stadium, and on December 2nd, 1942, achieved the first controlled chain reaction.

Columbia stayed in the bomb business. A secret laboratory with a deliberately dull name, the Substitute Alloy Materials Laboratories, worked on separating the rare uranium 235 that a bomb needed from the far more common uranium 238, by pumping uranium gas through fine barriers. It was led by Harold Urey, who had won a Nobel Prize for discovering heavy hydrogen right here at Columbia. Some of the work went on in a former car dealership on Broadway at 133rd Street. The method they developed was used in the giant K-25 plant at Oak Ridge, Tennessee.
=== DEEPER The First Lady of Physics | people
One of the physicists in Columbia's secret wartime labs was a young woman from China, Chien-Shiung Wu. She was born in 1912 near Shanghai, the daughter of a schoolmaster who started one of the first schools for girls in China, and she had come to America in 1936 to study at Berkeley, and she joined the project at Columbia in 1944, working on radiation detectors and on uranium separation. She's said to have helped solve a problem that nearly shut down the great reactor at Hanford, in Washington State, where a byproduct, xenon, was soaking up neutrons.

She stayed at Columbia for the rest of her career. In 1956, two young theorists, Tsung-Dao Lee of Columbia and Chen-Ning Yang, asked whether nature truly treats left and right the same, a principle physicists called parity. Wu designed the experiment to find out, cooling radioactive cobalt to nearly absolute zero at a laboratory in Washington. Early in 1957, she showed that parity is broken: in one of nature's fundamental forces, the universe can tell left from right.

Lee and Yang won the Nobel Prize that same year. Wu did not, an omission many physicists still call one of the great injustices in the prize's history. She was honored in other ways. She became the first woman to lead the American Physical Society, and in 2021, she was put on a postage stamp. Her colleagues called her the First Lady of Physics, a title she earned in these buildings.
=== DEEPER The Inventor Who Fought RCA | companies
Michael Pupin's most brilliant student was Edwin Howard Armstrong, a Columbia engineer who changed radio three times. As a student, in 1912, he invented the regenerative circuit, which made faint radio signals loud enough to hear. As an army officer in Paris in the First World War, he invented the superheterodyne receiver, the basis of almost every radio since. And in the early 1930s, in the basement of Philosophy Hall, just across the campus, he invented FM radio, which cut through static and carried music with startling clarity.

His friend David Sarnoff, the head of RCA, watched the demonstrations and decided to put RCA's money into television instead. So Armstrong built his own FM station, on the Palisades at Alpine, New Jersey, and spent years in court. His tower still stands on those cliffs. If you took the Upper West Side tour, you looked across the river at them from Riverside Park. First he fought the inventor Lee de Forest over the regenerative circuit, and lost in the Supreme Court in 1934. Then he fought RCA over FM.

In 1954, worn down by the fight, he died by suicide. His widow, Marion, carried on the lawsuits, and over the next thirteen years she won them, one company at a time. Philosophy Hall is now a National Historic Landmark, for the invention made in its basement, and FM radio is everywhere.
=== STOP Riverside Church
=== DEEPER Shall the Fundamentalists Win? | history
You heard that Harry Emerson Fosdick was forced out of his old pulpit. This is the sermon that did it. In May 1922, preaching as a Baptist guest at the First Presbyterian Church on Fifth Avenue in Greenwich Village, Fosdick asked a question: Shall the fundamentalists win? He argued that Christians could accept evolution and modern scholarship about the Bible without losing their faith, and that the church had room for people who disagreed.

The sermon was printed and mailed to ministers across the country, with the costs paid by an admirer, John D. Rockefeller Junior. Conservatives in the Presbyterian Church, with the support of William Jennings Bryan, pressed Fosdick to either join the denomination and accept its creed or leave. In 1925, he left.

Rockefeller offered him the pulpit of his own church, Park Avenue Baptist. Fosdick reportedly set conditions: the church would accept members without requiring baptism by immersion, it would be open to Christians of any denomination, and it would move away from the wealthy East Side. Rockefeller agreed, and this church was the result, rising near the universities and within sight of Harlem.

For the church's dedication, Fosdick wrote a hymn, God of Grace and God of Glory, and it's still sung in churches around the world. He preached here until 1946, and his radio sermons, on a national program called National Vespers, reached millions of listeners. The connection to Rockefeller ran in the family: Fosdick's brother, Raymond, was one of Rockefeller's closest advisers and later the president of the Rockefeller Foundation.
=== DEEPER Behind the Speech | politics
You heard that Martin Luther King Junior spoke out against the Vietnam War from this pulpit. He had been moving toward it for two years, and his advisers had warned him off. It would anger President Johnson, who had just signed the great civil rights laws, and it could split the movement.

The event was organized by a group called Clergy and Laymen Concerned About Vietnam, and King began by quoting their own statement: A time comes when silence is betrayal. Much of the text was drafted by a young historian and activist, Vincent Harding. In it, King warned against what he called the giant triplets of racism, extreme materialism and militarism, and he called for a revolution of values.

The reaction was savage. The New York Times published an editorial called Doctor King's Error. The Washington Post said he had diminished his usefulness to his cause, to his country and to his people. Life magazine called the speech demagogic slander that sounded like a script for Radio Hanoi. The board of the NAACP voted against merging the civil rights and antiwar movements.

King didn't back down. Eleven days later, he marched at the head of a vast antiwar protest from Central Park to the United Nations, as you may have heard on the Central Park South tour. Today, the speech many papers condemned is studied as one of the most important he ever gave.
=== DEEPER Cherry Trees and Taps | nature
Just north of the church, between it and Grant's Tomb, is a small, quiet park called Sakura Park. Sakura is Japanese for cherry blossom. In 1912, the Japanese residents of New York gave the city a large gift of cherry trees, and on an April day that year, the city held a planting celebration here. It was the same spring that Tokyo's famous gift of cherry trees was planted in Washington. The little park, until then called Claremont Park, took its new name.

Look for the stone lantern, a gift from the city of Tokyo, dedicated in October 1960, with Japan's crown prince and princess, Akihito and Michiko, in attendance. They later became emperor and empress.

In the southeast corner stands a bronze general, Daniel Butterfield, by Gutzon Borglum, the sculptor of Mount Rushmore. Butterfield's later career had a shadow: as a Treasury official in New York under President Grant, he was caught up in the Black Friday gold scandal of 1869. But his lasting claim is musical. In July 1862, in a Union camp in Virginia, he reworked an older bugle call into the tune we know as Taps, to signal lights out. It spread through both armies and became the call played at military funerals across the country.

So in spring, walking from the church to the tomb, you pass through cherry blossoms, under the eyes of the man who gave America its saddest twenty four notes.
=== STOP Grant's Tomb
=== DEEPER The Funeral of 1885 | history
Grant died in July 1885, at a cottage on Mount McGregor in upstate New York, just days after finishing his memoirs. New York gave him the largest funeral the city had ever seen. On August 8th, a procession many miles long, with tens of thousands of marchers, carried his coffin, on a black-draped funeral car drawn by twenty four black horses, from City Hall up to Riverside Park, past crowds often estimated at more than a million people.

Look at who carried him. Among the pallbearers were his old generals, William Tecumseh Sherman and Philip Sheridan, and two Confederate generals, Joseph Johnston and Simon Bolivar Buckner. Buckner had been Grant's friend at West Point. In 1854, when Grant left the army broke and far from home, Buckner lent him money to get back east. In 1862, Buckner had to surrender Fort Donelson to Grant, who demanded unconditional surrender, the words that first made Grant famous. And in the summer of 1885, Buckner came to Mount McGregor to sit with his dying friend. As for Johnston, six years later he was a pallbearer again, at Sherman's funeral in New York. He stood bareheaded in a cold rain out of respect for his old enemy, caught pneumonia, and died a few weeks later.

For twelve years, Grant's coffin rested in a temporary brick tomb near here, while New York struggled to raise the money for this one.
=== DEEPER The President Who Fought the Klan | politics
You heard about Grant the general and Grant the writer. Grant the president was long remembered mainly for the scandals of his administration. In recent decades, historians have looked again, largely because of what he did in the South.

After the Civil War, the Ku Klux Klan and groups like it waged a campaign of terror against freed Black Southerners and their white allies, whipping and murdering people to keep them from voting. In 1870, under Grant, the Fifteenth Amendment, protecting the vote for Black men, was ratified, and the new Department of Justice was created. Congress passed the Enforcement Acts and the Ku Klux Klan Act, and Grant signed them. His attorney general, Amos Akerman, prosecuted hundreds of Klansmen. In October 1871, Grant suspended habeas corpus in nine counties of South Carolina, and federal troops and marshals made mass arrests. Within a year or so, the first Klan was broken.

It didn't last. As the North lost interest and the violence returned, Reconstruction collapsed in the years after Grant left office. But Frederick Douglass, who knew every president from Lincoln on, praised Grant as a steadfast friend of Black Americans.

Above the tomb's entrance, look for the words Let Us Have Peace. It was the slogan of Grant's first campaign for president, in 1868.
=== DEEPER Julia | people
Grant's wife, Julia Dent Grant, lies beside him in the crypt. She grew up at White Haven, her family's plantation outside Saint Louis, where the Dents held people in slavery, and Grant married into that household in 1848. Before the war, struggling as a farmer near Saint Louis, he came to own one enslaved man, William Jones. In 1859, though Grant badly needed money, he freed him rather than sell him.

Julia was born with strabismus, a condition in which the eyes don't line up, and she was self-conscious about it all her life, which is why photographs usually show her in profile. When she was First Lady, she considered an operation, but Grant, as the story goes, told her he had fallen in love with her as she was.

The memoirs he finished as he was dying left her comfortable. She wrote her own memoirs too, the first by a former First Lady, but they weren't published until 1975. She died in 1902 and was placed here beside him. It's said that one reason Grant chose New York for his grave, rather than West Point, was that there, she could not have been buried at his side.
=== DEEPER The Secretary and the Ginkgo | people
You heard that about ninety thousand people paid for this tomb. Raising the money took twelve years, and newspapers in other cities mocked New York for being so slow, some suggesting the body be moved to Washington.

The Grant Monument Association was formed within days of Grant's death, with former President Chester Arthur at its head. Its first secretary, from 1885 to 1892, was Richard Theodore Greener, the first Black graduate of Harvard, a lawyer, scholar, and political supporter of Grant, who did much of the work of the early campaign. During Reconstruction, Greener had been the first Black professor at the University of South Carolina, and he later led the law school at Howard University and served as an American diplomat in Russia.

Now look for two trees near the tomb, inside a small enclosure with a plaque. On his trip around the world in 1879, Grant met Li Hongzhang, the leading Chinese statesman of the day, and the two men became friends. When Li visited New York in 1896, he came to pay his respects at Grant's temporary tomb. And in May 1897, a ginkgo, a tree native to China, was planted near the tomb on Li's behalf, by China's former minister to the United States.

So among all the monuments in this neighborhood, one of the most personal is alive: a tree from one old statesman to another.
=== DEEPER The Student Who Saved the Tomb | history
By the 1980s, this monument was in sorry shape, scrawled with graffiti, littered, and widely seen as unsafe after dark.

Around 1990, a Columbia undergraduate named Frank Scaturro began volunteering here with the National Park Service. He documented the damage and wrote reports, and when he took his complaints public, the Park Service dismissed him as a volunteer, in 1993. He didn't give up. He revived the Grant Monument Association, the same group that had built the tomb, and invited Grant's descendants onto its board. The association prepared a lawsuit to force the federal government to restore the site, and some of Grant's descendants even talked about moving the bodies elsewhere. The New York Times ran an editorial backing him, under the title Dishonor for a Hero President.

It worked. The money was found, and the tomb was cleaned and restored in time for its hundredth anniversary, in April 1997. The National Park Service still runs it, and visiting is free. Scaturro went on to become a lawyer, and he wrote a book arguing that Grant's presidency deserved far more respect than it had been given.

So the next time someone asks you who's buried in Grant's Tomb, you can tell them about the student who made sure it was worth visiting.
