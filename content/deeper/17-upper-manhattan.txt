=== DEEPER Comrades, Then Enemies | history
You heard that George Washington made this house his headquarters in 1776. He knew the man who built it. Twenty-one years earlier, in 1755, Roger Morris and a young Virginia officer named George Washington had served together as aides to the British general Edward Braddock, on his march against the French in western Pennsylvania. The march ended in disaster near Fort Duquesne, where Pittsburgh stands today. Braddock was killed, and Morris was wounded.

If an old story is true, the two men had something else in common. In 1756, Washington passed through New York and spent time with the family of Mary Philipse, an heiress from one of the richest families in the colony. Legend says he fell for her. The evidence is thin: a few teasing letters from a friend, hinting at Washington's interest. Washington's own side of the correspondence has never been found. What is certain is that in 1758, Mary married Roger Morris, and a few years later they built this house.

Then came the Revolution, and the old comrades ended up on opposite sides. The Morrises stayed loyal to the king and left the house in 1775. In 1779, New York confiscated the estates of leading loyalists, theirs among them, and the couple ended their lives in England.

When Washington came back to dine here in 1790, as you heard, he noted in his diary that the house had been confiscated and was now occupied by a farmer. The young officer who had once ridden beside its builder was now the President of the United States.
=== DEEPER Burr's Last Act | people
You heard that Aaron Burr married Eliza Jumel in this house when he was seventy-seven. How did the man who shot Alexander Hamilton end up here?

After the duel in 1804, Burr was indicted for murder in both New York and New Jersey, though he was never tried, and he finished his term as vice president. Then he went west. What exactly he planned there is still argued over: perhaps an invasion of Spanish Mexico, perhaps a new nation carved from the western territories. In 1807 he was arrested and tried for treason in Richmond, Virginia, with Chief Justice John Marshall presiding. He was acquitted, but his reputation was ruined.

He spent the next four years in Europe, trying to interest the British and the French in his schemes, and living for a time in near poverty in Paris. In 1812, he slipped back into New York and quietly practiced law.

Then came the blow he never recovered from. His only child, Theodosia, whom he adored and had educated as carefully as any son, had just lost her own young son. At the very end of 1812, she sailed from South Carolina to join her father in New York. Her ship, the Patriot, vanished. Legends have filled the silence ever since: a storm off Cape Hatteras, wreckers who lured ships onto the shoals, pirates who made her walk the plank. None has ever been proven.

So the man who married the rich widow in 1833 was a lonely survivor, and the couple separated within months. Burr died on Staten Island in 1836, and was buried at Princeton, near his father, who had been president of the college there.
=== DEEPER Madame Jumel's Ghost | legends
Eliza Jumel never won the acceptance she wanted. Rumors about her past followed her all her life: that she had been desperately poor, that she had been a kept woman, that she had married Stephen Jumel for his money. Fashionable New York kept its distance, even as she grew richer, managing her own property and investments.

In 1832, Stephen Jumel was injured in a carriage accident, and died soon after. A darker story spread: that Eliza had torn off his bandages and let him bleed to death. There was never any evidence for it, but the tale has clung to her ever since. She lived on here until 1865, when she died at about ninety.

And then, the story goes, she stayed. The mansion has a reputation as one of the most haunted houses in New York. The best-known tale comes from the 1960s, when a group of noisy schoolchildren waiting outside were told to hush by a woman in old-fashioned clothes on the balcony. Inside, the story goes, they recognized her in a portrait. It was Eliza. Around the same time, a well-known ghost hunter, Hans Holzer, held a séance here, and claimed he'd heard from Stephen himself, repeating the old accusation.

None of it can be checked, of course, and the accounts don't agree. But the museum has made peace with its ghosts. It now hosts evening paranormal investigations, for visitors who'd like to meet Madame Jumel for themselves.

=== DEEPER Where the Yankees Began | history
Look at the hospital buildings around you. Before the medical center rose here in the 1920s, this hilltop was a ballpark. From 1903 to 1912, a wooden stadium called Hilltop Park stood on Broadway between 165th and 168th Streets. It was home to a new American League team, the New York Highlanders, named, it's said, for this high ground.

The Highlanders were not very good, and their park was rough and far from downtown. There, in 1920, they introduced a new star bought from Boston, Babe Ruth. The Yankees began to outdraw their landlords, the Giants told them to find a home of their own, and in 1923 they crossed the river to their new stadium in the Bronx.

The Polo Grounds stood about ten blocks south and east of here, at 155th Street, below the cliff called Coogan's Bluff. There, on October 3rd, 1951, the Giants' Bobby Thomson hit the home run against the Brooklyn Dodgers that won the pennant, the Shot Heard Round the World. Three years later, Willie Mays made his famous over-the-shoulder catch there in the World Series.

The Giants left for San Francisco after the 1957 season. The new Mets played their first two seasons at the Polo Grounds, and in 1964 it was torn down for public housing, the Polo Grounds Towers. New York's baseball story runs right through this neighborhood, from the Yankees' first home to the Giants' last.
=== DEEPER Betty Shabazz | people
You heard that Malcolm X was killed in front of his wife, Betty Shabazz, and their children. That afternoon, she was in the audience with their four young daughters, and she was pregnant with twins. When the shooting began, she pushed the girls to the floor and covered them with her body.

Betty Shabazz had grown up in Detroit, trained as a nurse in New York, and met Malcolm through the Nation of Islam's temple in Harlem. Now, at thirty, she was a widow with four children, and that fall she gave birth to two more girls. === DEEPER Look Closer: Birds on the Shutters | look
As you walk this neighborhood, look at the metal shutters that roll down over storefronts, and at the sides of buildings. You may spot birds, painted larger than life, in brilliant color.

They belong to the Audubon Mural Project, begun in 2014 by a local gallery owner, Avi Gitler, together with the National Audubon Society. That year, the society published a study listing hundreds of North American bird species threatened by a warming climate. The project's goal is to paint every one of them, here, in the neighborhood where John James Audubon spent his last years. Dozens of artists have taken part, and the birds now perch along Broadway and the side streets of Washington Heights and Harlem.

The setting suits them. Audubon bought land along the Hudson here in 1841, when he was already famous for The Birds of America. He called the estate Minnie's Land, after his wife, Lucy, whom the family called Minnie, a Scottish word for mother. Here, with his sons, he worked on his last great project, a book on the mammals of North America. But his health and his mind were failing, and he died here in 1851, before it was done. His sons and a collaborator finished it.

When you've found a painted bird or two, look up. Red-tailed hawks hunt over the parks of upper Manhattan, and gulls and pigeons wheel over Broadway. Audubon, who painted pigeons too, would have recognized them.

=== DEEPER High Bridge or Low? | politics
The bridge you see almost wasn't built like this. The aqueduct's chief engineer, John Jervis, wanted something cheaper: a low bridge, with the water pipes dipping down across the river and climbing up the other side, the water carried back up by its own pressure.

But the Harlem River was a working waterway. People who used it, and people who owned land along it, feared that a low bridge would block their boats and their future trade. They petitioned, and in 1838 the city's aldermen came out against the low bridge. The state legislature settled the matter, requiring a bridge that left at least a hundred feet of clear space above the water for ships to pass.

So Jervis built high. It cost more and took years, and it was far grander than the engineers had wanted. That grandeur is why the bridge still stands, and why it's worth walking across.

The bridge also had to keep up with a thirsty city. At first, the water crossed in two iron pipes, each three feet across. By 1850, they weren't enough, and in the early 1860s a third pipe was added, a giant ninety inches wide. Together, the pipes carried a river of drinking water across this bridge for about a century, until newer tunnels deep underground took over the work.

Look down from the deck at the Harlem River today, with its highway and railroad along the banks. The ships that worried those petitioners are long gone. The bridge they demanded is still here.
=== DEEPER Sunday on the Harlem | culture
You heard that the promenade on the bridge was a favorite Sunday outing. In the later 1800s, the whole valley around it became a playground.

New Yorkers came up by steamboat and by carriage to stroll the bridge, picnic on the cliffs, and eat and drink at the taverns and hotels near each end. Rowing clubs raced on the river below, and crowds lined the banks to watch. The bridge itself became one of the sights of New York, reproduced on popular prints, including ones by Currier and Ives, and later on postcards.

Then, in 1898, the city opened the Harlem River Speedway, a broad dirt road along the river's edge, running north from 155th Street, past the foot of this bridge, toward Dyckman Street. It had one purpose: fast horses. Wealthy owners drove their trotters at full speed in light two-wheeled carts, racing each other while spectators cheered from the slopes. At first, cars weren't allowed.

The age of the horse didn't last. The Speedway was eventually opened to cars, and later rebuilt as part of the Harlem River Drive, the highway you can see and hear along the river today.

But in 2015, when the restored bridge reopened to walkers and cyclists, the old pleasure came back. On a summer Sunday, you'll find families strolling across between Manhattan and the Bronx, stopping to look down at the water, as they did a century and a half ago.
=== DEEPER The Pool in the Reservoir | underfoot
Look at the granite water tower again, and then at the huge public swimming pool beside it. The pool sits where a reservoir used to be.

The tower, finished in 1872, solved a problem. The aqueduct's water flowed by gravity, so it couldn't reach the highest ground of northern Manhattan. Pumps lifted water from a reservoir here up to a tank near the top of the tower, holding about forty-seven thousand gallons, and from that height it could flow down to the houses on the ridge.

In time, newer reservoirs and tunnels took over, and the old reservoir was no longer needed. Robert Moses, the city's parks commissioner, saw an opportunity. In 1934 the Parks Department took over the site, and on July 14th, 1936, Highbridge Pool opened. It was the fifth of eleven giant public pools the city opened that summer, built with workers paid by the federal Works Progress Administration during the Depression. Its outline follows the old reservoir, and it was designed to hold almost five thousand swimmers at once.

For neighborhoods of crowded apartments with no air conditioning, the pools were a blessing, and they still are. The pool and its bathhouse are now a city landmark, like the tower beside them.

On a hot day, listen for the shrieks of kids cannonballing into a basin that once held the neighborhood's drinking water.

=== DEEPER The Fort That Fell | history
You heard that Fort Washington fell in November 1776. Many historians think it should never have been defended at all. After the British drove the army out of New York City, Washington doubted the fort could hold. General Nathanael Greene, commanding across the river at Fort Lee, believed it could, and Washington let him keep almost three thousand men here, under Colonel Robert Magaw.

The British had help from inside. On November 2nd, Magaw's adjutant, William Demont, slipped out and deserted to the enemy, carrying plans of the defenses. He's often called the first traitor of the American Revolution.

On the morning of the attack, Washington crossed the river to see for himself, then went back, and watched from the New Jersey side as the British and Hessians closed in from three directions. By afternoon, the fort was jammed with retreating men, and Magaw surrendered. The British renamed it Fort Knyphausen, after the Hessian general who led the assault. Four days later, they crossed the Hudson and took Fort Lee too, and the Americans fled across New Jersey.

Among those retreating was a writer serving on Greene's staff, Thomas Paine, whose bones you may have heard about in Greenwich Village. A few weeks later, with the cause close to collapse, he published the pamphlet that begins: These are the times that try men's souls. It's said Washington had it read aloud to his troops. Within days of its publication, they crossed the Delaware, and the war turned.
=== DEEPER Ammann's Naked Towers | architecture
You heard that Othmar Ammann was the bridge's chief engineer. He came to America from Switzerland in 1904, and worked for years under the great bridge builder Gustav Lindenthal, on projects like the Hell Gate railroad bridge. Then the two fell out over the Hudson. Lindenthal dreamed of a colossal bridge at 57th Street, carrying trains and cars right into Midtown. Ammann argued for a simpler, cheaper crossing up here, where high cliffs on both banks gave a bridge its height for free.

Ammann won. The new Port Authority put him in charge, and the bridge went up in about four years, opening in October 1931, ahead of schedule and under budget. It hangs from four main cables, each about three feet thick and spun from tens of thousands of steel wires, and its towers rise about six hundred feet above the river.

You heard that the towers were meant to be covered. The covering was designed by Cass Gilbert, the architect of the Woolworth Building: a skin of stone and concrete, with great arched portals for the roadway. Imagine two massive stone gateways standing in the river, and you have the bridge that almost was.

By the time the money ran short, many people had decided they liked the steel. Engineers argued that the bare towers showed exactly how the bridge worked, and that dressing them in stone would be a kind of lie.

Ammann went on to design more of the city's great crossings, among them the Bayonne Bridge, the Bronx-Whitestone and the Throgs Neck, and, at the end of his career, the Verrazzano.

Look up at the crisscrossing steel. It was meant to be the bridge's hidden skeleton. It turned out to be its best feature.
=== DEEPER Cliffhangers Across the River | culture
Look across the Hudson at the cliffs of Fort Lee, New Jersey. A little over a century ago, they were the backdrop of the American movie business.

In the early 1900s, before Hollywood, film companies needed cheap land and varied scenery close to the actors, theaters and money of New York. Fort Lee had all of it, a short ferry ride from Manhattan. From around 1907, film companies crowded into the town, among them the company of William Fox, the man who built the Audubon Ballroom. On any given day, the cliffs, woods and taverns of the Palisades might play the Wild West, a medieval castle or a jungle.

The most famous films made around here were the serials, told in weekly episodes. The Perils of Pauline, in 1914, starred Pearl White as a daring heiress in constant danger. Episodes ended with her in peril, sometimes hanging from a cliff, and it's often said that's where the word cliffhanger comes from. The idea is older: in 1873, the novelist Thomas Hardy left a character dangling from a cliff at the end of an installment of a magazine serial. But the Palisades gave the cliffhanger its most famous face.

Within a decade, the industry moved west, chasing California's sunshine, cheap land, and distance from Thomas Edison's company, which held key patents and sued filmmakers who didn't pay to use them. By the early 1920s, Fort Lee's film boom was mostly over.

But for a few years, Americans learned to love the movies by watching these cliffs.

=== DEEPER Dinner on Horseback | people
You heard that Rockefeller bought the estate of C. K. G. Billings to make this park. Billings deserves a story of his own.

He was a retired Chicago gas company president, and his great passion was horses, especially fast trotters. In 1901 he began building an estate on this hilltop, with stables for his horses, and by about 1907 a turreted mansion called Tryon Hall, in the style of a French château, high above the Hudson.

The ballroom was dressed up as a woodland, the horses were reportedly brought up in the elevators, and the guests sat in the saddle, eating from trays and, it's said, sipping champagne through tubes from bottles in their saddlebags. === DEEPER Look Closer: The Unicorn in Captivity | look
If you go inside the Cloisters, find the room with the Unicorn Tapestries, and look for the most famous of them, The Unicorn in Captivity. The white unicorn sits inside a small round fence, chained to a pomegranate tree, on a field thick with flowers.

The scenes before it tell a hunt. Hunters find the unicorn dipping its horn into a stream to purify the water for the other animals, chase it, trap it and kill it. Then, in this panel, it's alive again, at peace in its garden. Whether this panel was even made for the same set as the others is still debated.

Look closely at its flank. Those red drops aren't blood. They're juice, dripping from the ripe pomegranates above, a symbol of fertility and marriage. Many scholars think this tapestry, and perhaps the whole set, celebrated a wedding.

Now look at the plants. Botanists have identified most of the more than one hundred plants woven into the tapestries, so accurately are they drawn: flowers, herbs and trees, some medicinal, some symbolic, some simply beautiful. The Cloisters' gardens grow many of the same species.

Then look for two letters, an A and an E, tied together with a knotted cord. They appear again and again in the tapestries. Whose initials they were, nobody knows.

The tapestries were woven around 1500, and later belonged to the La Rochefoucauld family in France. During the French Revolution, they were looted from the family's château, and, as the story goes, spent years covering potatoes or wrapping fruit trees against the frost, before the family got them back in the 1800s. John D. Rockefeller Junior bought them in 1922 and hung them in his own home in Manhattan, and later gave them to the Cloisters.
=== DEEPER Captain Molly's Grave | legends
You heard that Margaret Corbin took her husband's place at the cannon here, and was badly wounded. Her story didn't end with the battle, and its strangest chapter came more than two hundred years later.

Corbin was captured with the garrison, then let go. Her wounds left her unable to use one arm, and in 1779 Congress granted her half a soldier's pay for life. She lived near West Point, attached to a unit of disabled veterans, where people called her Captain Molly. She's one of the women whose stories blended into the legend of Molly Pitcher, who carried water to the gunners in battle and then took over a cannon herself.

She died around 1800 and was buried near West Point, in a grave that was soon forgotten. In 1926, the Daughters of the American Revolution had remains believed to be hers moved to the cemetery at West Point, with full military honors, and put up a monument.

Then, in 2016, contractors working beside the monument disturbed the grave. The Army studied the bones, and in 2017 announced a surprise: they belonged to a tall, middle-aged man. Nobody knows who he was, or where Margaret Corbin lies. A search with ground-penetrating radar found nothing. The man was reburied, and Captain Molly's monument still stands, honoring a woman whose grave is lost. Here, at least, the circle at the park's entrance keeps her name on the map.

=== DEEPER Weekend Archaeologists | underfoot
You heard that there's a reconstructed hut in the garden, like the ones Hessian soldiers lived in. It exists thanks to two men with a long iron rod.

In the late 1800s and early 1900s, while upper Manhattan was still half countryside, an engineer named Reginald Pelham Bolton and a fellow history lover, William Louis Calver, spent their weekends digging. With a band of friends, they combed the hills and fields of Inwood and Washington Heights for the past, probing the ground with an iron rod and feeling for buried stone.

They found a great deal. On the slopes nearby, they uncovered more than sixty huts from the British and Hessian camps that occupied this area during the Revolution, with the litter of soldiers' daily life: buttons, buckles, coins, broken bottles, even a corkscrew. They also dug into much older remains, the shell heaps and tools of the Lenape.

They were racing the builders. As the subway arrived and apartment houses spread north, most of their sites vanished under foundations. Around 1915, Bolton rebuilt one hut's foundation here, in the Dyckman garden, so that something would survive, and some of their finds are displayed in the farmhouse.

Look at the hut, and imagine the hillside around you dotted with dozens of them, smoke rising from their chimneys in the winters of an occupied island.
=== DEEPER The Free Bridge | politics
For more than sixty years, if you wanted to leave Manhattan by land, you paid the Philipse family. In 1693, Frederick Philipse, one of the richest men in the colony, built the King's Bridge across Spuyten Duyvil Creek, at the island's northern tip, under a royal grant that let him charge tolls. It was the only bridge off the island.

Local farmers hated the tolls. So in the 1750s, a man named Benjamin Palmer raised money from his neighbors to build a bridge anyone could cross for free. The Philipses wouldn't let him build near their crossing, so Palmer moved downstream and found partners with land on each bank. On the Manhattan side, his partner was Jacob Dyckman, of this farm's family.

The Free Bridge, also called the Farmers' Bridge or Dyckman's Bridge, opened at the end of 1758, and the newspaper announced a party to celebrate. From then on, the two bridges stood only about five hundred yards apart, one charging tolls and one free. It's not hard to guess which the farmers preferred.

Both are gone from sight now. The creek they crossed was rerouted and filled in during the early 1900s, and what's left of the old bridges lies buried under the streets of the Bronx neighborhood that still carries the name Kingsbridge.
=== DEEPER Morir Soñando | food
Walk along Dyckman Street or Broadway, and you're in one of the great Dominican food neighborhoods anywhere outside the Dominican Republic. A few things to look for.

For breakfast, mangú: green plantains, boiled and mashed with butter, topped with tangy pickled red onions. Order it with los tres golpes, the three hits: fried salami, fried cheese and eggs. On a cold day, look for sancocho, a thick stew of meats, root vegetables and plantains, served with rice and avocado. Late on a summer night, find a chimi, the Dominican street burger, a griddled patty piled with shredded cabbage and tomato and a pink, garlicky sauce, on a soft bun.

And to drink, try a morir soñando, which means to die dreaming. It's orange juice and milk, sweetened and poured over ice. That sounds like a recipe for curdling, and the trick is to keep everything cold and stir the juice into the milk slowly. Done right, it tastes like a melted creamsicle.

Dominicans began arriving in large numbers in the 1960s, after the fall of the dictator Rafael Trujillo and the reform of American immigration law in 1965, and these blocks became their capital in the north. You can taste it on every corner.

=== DEEPER In Spite of the Devil | legends
You heard that nobody's quite sure what Spuyten Duyvil means. In 1809, Washington Irving gave it a story, in his comic History of New York, written under the pen name Diedrich Knickerbocker.

As Irving tells it, Peter Stuyvesant, the last Dutch governor, learning of an English threat, sent his trumpeter, Antony Van Corlear, a stout fellow famous for his mighty blasts, to rouse the farmers up the river. Van Corlear reached this creek on a stormy night. There was no ferryman, and the water was wild. So, swearing that he would swim across in spite of the devil, en spijt den Duyvil, he plunged in. Halfway across, the devil, in the shape of a giant fish, seized him by the leg. Van Corlear raised his trumpet, blew one last tremendous blast, and sank. And the creek, Irving says, has carried his oath as its name ever since.

It's pure invention, but it stuck. Irving's book was so popular that knickerbocker became a nickname for New Yorkers, and much later, for a basketball team.

The real origin is still debated. But the danger was real. Before the channel was straightened and tamed, its tides, squeezed between the Hudson and the Harlem River, ran fierce and fast, and boatmen respected them. Stand at the water's edge and watch the current, and you'll see why anyone might think twice before swimming it.
=== DEEPER The Piece of Manhattan That Moved | underfoot
Somewhere across the water to the northeast is a neighborhood that belongs to Manhattan but sits on the mainland. Here's how that happened.

The creek in front of you wasn't always where it is. The original Spuyten Duyvil Creek looped north, around a rocky knob called Marble Hill, named for the white marble in its ledges. In 1895, to give ships a straight path between the Hudson and the Harlem River, engineers cut the Harlem River Ship Canal straight across the bottom of the loop. Marble Hill was suddenly an island, cut off from the rest of Manhattan.

Then, around 1914, the old loop of the creek on its northern side was filled in. Marble Hill was now attached to the Bronx, and to the mainland of North America. But legally, it stayed part of Manhattan, and it still is.

In 1939, the Bronx borough president, James Lyons, staged a stunt: he marched onto Marble Hill, planted the Bronx flag, and called it the Bronx Sudetenland, after the land Hitler had just seized from Czechoslovakia. Residents protested, and the claim went nowhere.

That marble matters here too. Under the low ground of Inwood, along Broadway and Dyckman Street, lies a soft white rock called Inwood marble, which wears away faster than the hard schist of the ridges. That's why the valleys of upper Manhattan run where they do, and why the hill above you stands so high above them.
=== DEEPER Eagles over Manhattan | nature
Keep an eye on the sky over the creek. In the summer of 2002, this park became a nursery for bald eagles.

Bald eagles had vanished from New York City long before. Hunting, lost habitat and the pesticide DDT, which thinned their eggshells until they broke, had driven them close to extinction across much of the country. After DDT was banned in 1972, they began a slow recovery.

That summer, the city's Urban Park Rangers, working with a conservation group, brought four eaglets from Wisconsin and placed them in boxes on a platform high in the trees here. The method is called hacking, borrowed from falconry. The young birds were fed without seeing the people who fed them, so they wouldn't grow attached to humans, and when they were ready to fly, the boxes were opened. The hope was that eagles, which tend to return to breed near where they first took flight, would one day come back to nest.

The project ran for five years, and by one count about twenty eagles were released. None settled in Manhattan right away. But bald eagles have made a remarkable comeback across the region. They're seen along the Hudson, especially in winter, fishing in the open water, and pairs nest again not far from the city.

So if a huge dark bird with a white head glides over the creek, it's part of that comeback, and this park played its part.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Hudson on His Column | look
If the trees allow, look across the creek to the Bronx hilltop just beyond the Henry Hudson Bridge. A tall stone column rises there, and on top stands a bronze figure of Henry Hudson himself.

It took almost thirty years to put him there. The column was planned for the Hudson-Fulton Celebration of 1909, a citywide festival marking three hundred years since Hudson's voyage and about a hundred since Robert Fulton's first steamboat. Ground was broken that year, and the column, about a hundred feet tall, went up in 1912. Then the money ran out before the statue could be made.

The sculptor was Karl Bitter, whose sudden death in 1915 you may have heard about at the Pulitzer Fountain on the Fifth Avenue tour. He left only a model. The column stood empty for decades, until his former assistant, Karl Gruppe, finished the figure, and the monument was finally dedicated in 1938.

So when you look for Hudson up there, you're looking at a monument to persistence. Hudson never saw the end of his own story either. On his next voyage, in 1611, in the bay in northern Canada that now bears his name, his crew mutinied and set him adrift in a small boat, with his young son and a few loyal men. He was never seen again.
