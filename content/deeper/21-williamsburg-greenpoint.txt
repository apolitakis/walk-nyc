=== STOP Domino Sugar & the Sugar Trust
=== DEEPER The Spring in the Scale | companies
You heard that Henry Osborne Havemeyer built one of the most powerful trusts of the Gilded Age. In 1907, his company was caught cheating the government, right here on the Williamsburg docks.

Havemeyer himself argued that high tariffs, the taxes on imported goods, were what made trusts possible. In 1899 he told a federal commission that the mother of all trusts was the customs tariff bill. His refineries paid those taxes too. Every bag of raw sugar that came off the ships owed a duty according to its weight, and each one was weighed on the company's own dock scales, under the eyes of government weighers.

In November 1907, a Treasury special agent named Richard Parr raided the docks. According to testimony at the trials that followed, a secret spring had been fitted to the scales, so that bag after bag was recorded as lighter than it really was. The difference on any one bag was easy to miss. Multiplied over millions of bags, year after year, it added up to a fortune in unpaid duties.

Havemeyer never had to answer for it. He died a few weeks after the raid, in December 1907.

The government went after the company and its men. The company paid back more than two million dollars in duties and penalties. The dock superintendent, Oliver Spitzer, was convicted, along with four checkers who worked under him, and sentenced to two years in prison. Then President William Howard Taft pardoned him, so that he could testify against the men above him. Newspapers reported that Parr himself stood to collect a large share of the money recovered, as a reward.
=== DEEPER Louisine's Pictures | people
Some of the profits from this refinery hang today on the walls of the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Henry Havemeyer's second wife, Louisine Elder, came from the same business. Her family name was part of the refinery's old company name, Havemeyers and Elder. As a young woman in Paris in the 1870s, she met an American painter named Mary Cassatt, and the two became lifelong friends. Cassatt urged her to buy a pastel by Edgar Degas, a painter few Americans had heard of. She did, and she kept buying.

After Louisine married Henry, in 1883, they collected together on a grand scale, with Cassatt as their guide, hunting for pictures across Europe. They bought old masters and modern painters alike: Rembrandt, El Greco, Goya, Courbet, Manet, Monet, Cézanne, and many more works by Degas. When Louisine died, in 1929, she left nearly two thousand works of art to the Met. It remains one of the greatest gifts in the museum's history.

She was much more than a collector. Louisine Havemeyer was a fierce campaigner for women's right to vote. In 1915 she helped organize an exhibition of old and modern masters, Degas and Cassatt among them, to raise money for the cause. In 1919, in her sixties, she joined a protest in front of the White House, where suffragists burned an effigy of President Woodrow Wilson. She was arrested and briefly jailed, and then rode across the country on a train called the Prison Special, with other women who had been jailed for the cause, speaking to crowds along the way. The next year, the Nineteenth Amendment gave American women the vote.

So if you visit the Met, look for the words H. O. Havemeyer Collection on the labels beside some of its greatest paintings. They mean sugar from Williamsburg, and the eye of a woman who refused to stay quiet.
=== DEEPER Twenty Months on Kent Avenue | politics
You heard that the refinery closed in 2004. Its last years were marked by one of the longest strikes the city has seen.

By the 1990s, Domino belonged to a British company, Tate and Lyle, and the Brooklyn refinery was old and costly to run. In June 1999, when their contract ran out, about two hundred eighty workers walked off the job. Their union was Local 1814 of the International Longshoremen's Association, better known for dockworkers.

The company wanted to cut jobs and weaken the seniority rules that protected workers who had been there for decades. The union refused. Week after week, then month after month, the strikers walked the picket line on Kent Avenue, through two winters.

They got little help from the wider labor movement. The national AFL-CIO turned down calls for a boycott of Domino sugar and for a national strike fund, though New York's Central Labor Council raised money locally.

In February 2001, after twenty months, the remaining strikers voted to go back to work, largely on the company's terms. Domino was allowed to cut about a hundred ten jobs.

That same year, Tate and Lyle sold Domino to a group led by Florida sugar growers. They named their new company American Sugar Refining, the old name of Havemeyer's trust. Three years later, they closed the Brooklyn refinery for good.

As you look at the old building, think of the strikers who stood outside it for twenty months, trying to hold on to some of the last factory jobs on the Williamsburg waterfront.
=== STOP Peter Luger & South Williamsburg
=== DEEPER How Peter Luger Does It | food
You heard that the Forman family made Peter Luger the most famous steakhouse in New York. Here's what happens to a steak before it reaches the table, and after.

It begins with the buying, the job Marsha Forman took on. The family has kept it ever since, picking out the top grade of beef, called prime, piece by piece.

Then comes the dry aging. The beef rests, uncovered, in a chilled room for weeks. Moisture evaporates, so the flavor concentrates, and the meat's own enzymes slowly break down the tough fibers. The outside dries into a dark crust that has to be trimmed away, so a dry-aged steak loses a good part of its weight before it's ever cooked, one reason it costs so much.

The porterhouse is broiled under fierce heat, sliced in the kitchen, and carried out on a hot platter. Watch the waiter. One end of the platter is propped up on an upturned plate, so the butter and juices run down to the other end, and the waiter spoons them back over the meat.

The regulars' order has barely changed in decades: thick-cut bacon to start, sliced tomatoes and onions with the house steak sauce, creamed spinach, German fried potatoes, and, for dessert, anything under a mountain of whipped cream, which the menu calls schlag. With the check come chocolate coins wrapped in gold foil.

Not everyone is a believer. In 2019, the New York Times restaurant critic Pete Wells gave Peter Luger zero stars, in a review that became famous in its own right, arguing that the old place no longer lived up to its legend. The regulars kept coming anyway.
=== DEEPER The Candy That Built Pfizer | companies
A mile or so southeast of here, on Flushing Avenue, near the border with Bedford-Stuyvesant, stands the old factory of Pfizer, one of the largest drug companies in the world. Pfizer was born in Williamsburg.

In 1849, two cousins from Germany opened a small chemical business here: Charles Pfizer, who had trained as a chemist, and Charles Erhart, a confectioner, a candy maker. Their first success needed both of their skills. Santonin was a medicine for intestinal worms, a common affliction at the time, and it tasted awful. The cousins blended it with almond toffee flavoring and shaped it into a candy cone, and it sold.

Pfizer grew by mastering chemistry on a large scale. One of its biggest products was citric acid, which soft drink makers bought by the ton. It came from lemons, mostly from Italy, until, around the time of World War One, Pfizer's chemists learned to make it by fermentation, letting a mold feed on sugar in big vats.

That skill turned out to matter enormously. During World War Two, the new antibiotic penicillin could save countless wounded soldiers, but it was hard to grow in quantity. Pfizer bet on deep-tank fermentation, the method it knew from citric acid, and in 1944 it opened a plant that made penicillin that way, in a converted ice factory in Brooklyn. By the company's account, most of the penicillin that went ashore with the Allied troops on D-Day was made by Pfizer.

Pfizer moved its headquarters to Manhattan in the early 1960s and closed its last Brooklyn plant in 2008. The old factory now houses small food companies and other businesses.

As you walk south from here, into the heart of the Hasidic neighborhood, read the street signs. Together they make a roll call of the men who signed the Declaration of Independence.

Rutledge, Heyward, Lynch and Middleton: those are all four of the signers from South Carolina. Hooper, Hewes and Penn are the three from North Carolina. === STOP Brewers' Row & the Brooklyn Brewery
=== DEEPER Cold Beer Before Refrigerators | underfoot
You heard that German immigrants brought lager brewing to Brooklyn. Lager needs cold, and before refrigeration, cold was one of the hardest things a brewer had to find.

Lager is fermented by a yeast that works slowly at low temperatures and settles to the bottom of the tank, unlike the yeasts of English ales, which work warmer and faster. Then the beer is stored cold for weeks or months, while it clears and mellows. The German word lagern means to store.

So the first lager brewers here dug down. Beneath their breweries they built cellars and vaulted tunnels, sometimes several levels below the street, where the temperature stays cool all year. Each winter they packed them with ice: blocks cut from frozen lakes and ponds upstate and along the Hudson, shipped down the river, and kept in insulated ice houses. A big brewery went through thousands of tons of ice a year.

Then, in the 1870s and 1880s, came machines that made cold with compressed ammonia, and brewers were among their first big customers. Freed from the winter and the cellars, breweries could grow into the great brick plants that once lined these streets.

Some of the old cellars are said to survive, forgotten, beneath later buildings in Williamsburg and Bushwick. So as you walk, remember that much of Brooklyn's beer was brewed above ground, but aged below it, under your feet.
=== DEEPER The Beer Ads New York Loved | culture
Brooklyn's brewers didn't just make beer. They made some of the best-loved advertising in America.

Start with Miss Rheingold. Every year from 1940 into the 1960s, the Rheingold brewery picked a handful of young women as finalists and asked the public to vote for the next Miss Rheingold, whose face would smile down from billboards, subway cards and bar signs for a year. Ballot boxes sat in bars and grocery stores. In its peak years, by the brewery's count, the contest drew tens of millions of votes, and it was said that only the race for president drew more. The first winner, in 1940, was Jinx Falkenburg, a model and actress who went on to be a pioneer of radio and television talk shows.

Then came Bert and Harry Piel, two cartoon brothers who sold Piels beer on television beginning in 1955. They were voiced by the comedians Bob Elliott and Ray Goulding, known as Bob and Ray, and viewers adored them. People said they tuned in for the commercials. Whether the brothers sold much beer is another question.

And Schaefer had the scoreboard. At Ebbets Field, the home of the Brooklyn Dodgers, which you may have heard about on the Fort Greene tour, a big Schaefer sign topped the right-field scoreboard. It did double duty: the h in Schaefer lit up when the official scorer ruled a play a hit, and the e lit up for an error. Schaefer also sponsored the Dodgers' broadcasts, so a whole generation of Brooklyn fans heard the name between pitches.
=== DEEPER Last Call for the Local Brands | history
You heard that Schaefer and Rheingold, the last big Brooklyn breweries, both closed in 1976. The end had been coming for a long time.

As the story is usually told, a turning point came in 1949, when a strike shut New York's breweries for weeks. Beer from Milwaukee and Saint Louis poured in to fill the gap, and many New Yorkers who tried Budweiser, Schlitz or Pabst for the first time never went back.

The local brewers tried to survive by getting bigger and more modern. Schaefer built a new brewery in Pennsylvania's Lehigh Valley, which opened in the early 1970s, and soon gave up on Kent Avenue. The names outlived the breweries, as brands bought and sold by bigger companies, and Rheingold has been revived more than once. The old Schaefer site on Kent Avenue became apartment towers called Schaefer Landing, with a ferry stop at the water's edge.

By the 1990s, the Williamsburg waterfront was full of empty industrial buildings. When Steve Hindy and Tom Potter went looking for a home for the Brooklyn Brewery, the one they chose on North 11th Street had been an ironworks, and later a matzo factory. Brooklyn beer came back in a building that had once baked unleavened bread.
=== STOP Bedford Avenue & Hipster Williamsburg
=== DEEPER The Tunnel Under the River | underfoot
The L train that put artists one stop from Manhattan crosses under the East River through a tunnel that opened in 1924. The line ran from 14th Street in Manhattan to Williamsburg, and was later pushed all the way to Canarsie, which is why its official name is still the Canarsie Line. In 2012, its tunnel nearly drowned.

The tunnel is really two tubes, one for each direction, lined with iron and concrete. Along their sides run low concrete walls, called bench walls, packed with the cables that carry power and signals for the trains.

On the night of October 29th, 2012, Hurricane Sandy pushed a surge of seawater into the subway, and the tunnel filled with millions of gallons of salt water. Pumps cleared it, and trains were soon running again, but the salt stayed in the concrete, eating at the cables and the steel inside the walls. Engineers warned that the tunnel had to be rebuilt.

The transit authority decided to close it completely, for fifteen months, starting in April 2019. For Williamsburg, that was alarming. Hundreds of thousands of riders a day depended on the L to cross the river. Businesses braced for the worst, and the city planned extra buses, ferries and a busway across 14th Street in Manhattan. Some called it the L-pocalypse.

Then, in January 2019, just months before the shutdown, Governor Andrew Cuomo announced a new plan, worked out with engineering experts from Cornell and Columbia universities. Instead of tearing out the old bench walls, crews would leave them in place, wrap the weak sections in a strong fiber-reinforced plastic, and hang new cables on racks along the tunnel walls. The work was done one tube at a time, on nights and weekends, and it was finished in April 2020.

The full shutdown never happened. Next time you ride the L under the river, look out the window: those cables on their racks are what kept the trains running.
=== DEEPER Lifting the Giglio | legends
A few blocks east of here, at North 8th Street and Havemeyer Street, stands Our Lady of Mount Carmel, the old Italian parish of the Northside. Every July, for more than a century, the men of the parish have done something astonishing in the street outside: they lift a tower.

The tower is called the giglio, Italian for lily. It's a slender spire, roughly seventy feet tall and weighing a few tons, covered in papier-mâché saints, angels and flowers, with a platform at the bottom big enough for a brass band and a singer. More than a hundred men, called lifters, crouch beneath it with the poles on their shoulders. At a signal from their captain, they raise it off the ground, band and all, and make it dance, turning it, bouncing it, carrying it down the street.

The tradition comes from Nola, a town near Naples, and honors its patron, Saint Paulinus, who was bishop there in the early fifth century. Here is the legend, as Nola tells it. Raiders from North Africa carried off many young men of the town as slaves. A widow begged the bishop to save her son. With nothing left to pay a ransom, Paulinus offered himself in the young man's place, and was taken to Africa as a slave. In time, a king there learned who he was and, moved by his sacrifice, set him free, with the other captives of Nola. When they sailed home, the townspeople met them at the shore carrying lilies.

How much of that happened is anyone's guess. Pope Gregory the Great was already telling the story less than two centuries later, but historians point out that the raiders it describes seem to have reached Italy only after Paulinus had died.

Over the centuries, the lilies grew into towers. Immigrants from Nola and nearby towns brought the feast to Williamsburg, along with a boat, also carried on shoulders, in memory of the voyage home. The streets around the church fill with stands selling zeppole and sausage and peppers. If you're here in July, follow the brass band.
=== STOP McCarren Park & the Pool
=== DEEPER The Boss of Brooklyn | people
You heard that the park is named for Patrick McCarren, a state senator and Democratic boss. Reformers saw him as everything that was wrong with machine politics.

McCarren grew up in Williamsburg and learned a trade as a cooper, a barrel maker, reportedly making barrels for the sugar refineries on the waterfront. For a time he worked as an inspector of oil. But his real talent was politics. He rose through the Democratic clubhouses to the State Assembly and then to the State Senate in Albany, where he served for most of two decades.

By 1903 he was the boss of the Brooklyn Democrats, succeeding Hugh McLaughlin, who had run the party in Brooklyn for decades. McCarren spent his years at the top fending off Charles Murphy, the boss of Tammany Hall in Manhattan, who wanted Brooklyn's Democrats under his thumb. By many accounts he was also a heavy gambler.

He made powerful friends, and his critics said some of them were too powerful. In 1904, Thomas Lawson, a Boston financier who was waging a public war on Standard Oil, charged that McCarren was on the oil company's payroll, at twenty thousand dollars a year, and offered him a hundred thousand dollars if he could disprove it. The charge was never proven, but McCarren never took up the challenge.

He died in October 1909, at Saint Catherine's Hospital in Williamsburg. His funeral was said to be the largest Brooklyn had ever seen, and the city renamed what had been Greenpoint Park in his honor.

So the softball games and lazy afternoons here take place in a park named for a barrel maker who became the boss of Brooklyn.
=== DEEPER The Tree That Grows in Brooklyn | nature
Look along the park's fences, in sidewalk cracks and in the gaps between buildings, for a scrappy tree with long leaves made of many pointed leaflets, arranged in pairs along a stem. It's probably an ailanthus, called the tree of heaven, and it's the tree in the title of the best-known novel ever written about Williamsburg.

A Tree Grows in Brooklyn, by Betty Smith, published in 1943, follows Francie Nolan, a girl growing up poor in Williamsburg in the years before and during World War One. Smith knew that world. She was born in Williamsburg in 1896, to German immigrant parents, and grew up in its tenements. The novel became a huge bestseller, and in 1945 it became the first feature film directed by Elia Kazan.

In the book, the tree is Francie's emblem. It grows out of cement and rubbish, in backyards and vacant lots where nothing else will, and keeps reaching for the sky.

The real tree has a story of its own. The ailanthus is native to China. It reached America in the late 1700s, and in the 1800s it was planted along city streets, prized because it grew fast and could stand smoke and poor soil. Then people soured on it. The male trees' flowers smell rank, and the tree spreads relentlessly, sprouting from its roots and scattering its seeds on the wind. Today it's considered an invasive species, and parks crews work to remove it.

But it almost never gives up. Cut it down, and it sprouts again. In the poorest corners of the old city, it was sometimes the only shade there was. That's what Betty Smith saw in it, and it's how a weed became one of the most famous trees in American literature.
=== STOP Greenpoint & the Eberhard Faber Pencil Factory
=== DEEPER The Five Black Arts | companies
You heard that Greenpoint was a home of the five black arts. The list varies a little from one telling to the next, but it usually runs printing, pottery, glass, cast iron and gas, all trades of fire, coal and soot.

Glass was the most glamorous. In 1860, Christian Dorflinger, a French-born glassmaker, built the Greenpoint Flint Glass Works on Commercial Street, beside the mouth of Newtown Creek, and it turned out some of the finest cut crystal in America. In 1861, Mary Todd Lincoln ordered a set of glassware for the White House from Dorflinger's works, engraved with a version of the presidential seal. It was reordered again and again, and stayed in use for decades, until the administration of Benjamin Harrison replaced it, in 1891.

Dorflinger eventually moved to Pennsylvania, but the skills he brought stayed behind, and Greenpoint became a glass town. By 1883, eighteen of the twenty glass factories in Brooklyn were in Greenpoint. The works Dorflinger founded carried on under later owners, the last of whom moved across the creek to Queens in 1946.

Pottery came close behind. The Union Porcelain Works, which you heard about, made true hard porcelain, the kind produced by the great factories of Europe, at a time when few American firms could. For the Centennial Exhibition of 1876, in Philadelphia, its sculptor, Karl Müller, designed the Century Vase, with bison heads for handles and scenes celebrating American history and invention. The Brooklyn Museum, on the Prospect Park tour, has one in its collection.

Walk these blocks and you'll notice how many of the old brick factory buildings remain, many of them now studios and workshops, still making things.
=== DEEPER Why Pencils Are Yellow | legends
Look again at the terra cotta pencils on the old factory. They're yellow, like most pencils in America, and there's a story behind that color, though it may be more legend than history.

For centuries, the best graphite for pencils came from a single mine at Borrowdale, in the north of England. In the 1800s, a French prospector named Jean-Pierre Alibert found graphite of similar quality far to the east, in Siberia, near the Chinese frontier.

As the story goes, around 1890 a pencil maker in Austria-Hungary, Koh-i-Noor, launched a luxury pencil made with that Siberian graphite, and painted it yellow, the color of China's emperors, to hint at where its graphite came from, and at royalty. It sold well, other makers copied the color to signal quality, and yellow became the color of a good pencil.

Eberhard Faber's best seller fits the story neatly. Its Mongol pencil was yellow, and its name seems to point toward the same faraway East.

It's a good story, and it may even be true, but it's told far more often than it's proven. Whatever the reason, the color stuck. So did another feature: most pencils have six sides, so they don't roll off the desk.
=== DEEPER A Basket on Holy Saturday | food
You heard about the Polish bakeries and delis along Manhattan Avenue. The best way to understand them is through the Polish calendar, which is, in large part, a calendar of food.

On the last Thursday before Lent, called Fat Thursday, the bakeries sell rich, round Polish doughnuts, filled with rose jam or custard and glazed or dusted with sugar, and the lines can stretch down the block.

On Holy Saturday, the day before Easter, families carry small baskets lined with white linen to church to be blessed. Inside are colored eggs, bread, salt, a piece of sausage, horseradish, and often a little lamb made of sugar or butter. The blessed food is eaten at Easter breakfast.

On Christmas Eve, the great family supper begins when the first star appears in the sky. Traditionally it has no meat: beet soup with tiny mushroom dumplings, pierogi, herring, and carp, which many families once bought alive and kept in the bathtub until it was time to cook. Before eating, everyone breaks and shares a thin wafer, often blessed by the parish priest, with good wishes for the year. An extra place is often set at the table, for an unexpected guest.

The rest of the year, the butchers and delis carry the load. Look in the windows for long rows of kielbasa, smoked and fresh, and for thin, dry sausages, hard as sticks, made to carry in a pocket.

The Polish population is smaller than it was. But on a Fat Thursday morning, or the Saturday before Easter, you'd hardly know it.
=== STOP Charles Pratt's Astral Oil & the Monitor
=== DEEPER The Monitor's Last Night | history
You heard that the Monitor fought the Virginia to a standoff in March 1862. It didn't survive the year.

The low hull that made the Monitor such a small target in battle made it a dangerous ship on the open sea. Its deck sat barely above the waterline, and waves washed right over it.

On December 29th, 1862, the Monitor left Hampton Roads, towed by a steamer, the Rhode Island, bound for North Carolina to join the Union fleet off the Carolina coast. The next day, off Cape Hatteras, the wind and the seas rose. Water poured in around the base of the turret and through the hull, faster than the pumps could clear it. That night the crew signaled for help, and boats from the Rhode Island came alongside in the storm and took off most of the men. Sixteen did not get off. In the early hours of December 31st, the Monitor sank.

For more than a century, no one knew exactly where it lay. In 1973, a team led by scientists from Duke University found the wreck, upside down, more than two hundred feet down, and in 1975 it became the first national marine sanctuary in the United States.

Over the following decades, Navy divers and archaeologists brought up the propeller, the engine and, in 2002, the turret itself. They went to the Mariners' Museum, in Newport News, Virginia, where conservators have spent years drawing the salt out of the iron.

Inside the turret, divers found the remains of two sailors. Despite DNA testing, they couldn't be identified, and in 2013 they were buried with full honors at Arlington National Cemetery.

So the ship launched from this shore never came home. But its turret, at least, made it back to dry land.
=== DEEPER The Cheesebox Inventor | people
The Monitor's designer, John Ericsson, was one of the great engineers of his century, and the ship was his comeback, after a disaster that nearly ruined him in America.

Ericsson was born in Sweden in 1803. As a young engineer in England, he built a steam locomotive that competed in the famous Rainhill trials of 1829, and he developed a screw propeller for ships, at a time when most steamships were pushed by paddle wheels. In 1839 he moved to New York.

His first big American project was the Princeton, the Navy's first warship driven by a screw propeller. In 1844, during a demonstration cruise on the Potomac River, with President John Tyler aboard, a giant gun called the Peacemaker exploded, killing the secretary of state, the secretary of the navy and several others. The gun had been designed by the ship's captain, Robert Stockton, not by Ericsson. But Stockton let the blame drift toward Ericsson, who was never properly paid for his work on the ship.

Seventeen years later, when word came that the Confederates were rebuilding the burned hulk of the Merrimack as an ironclad, Ericsson offered the Navy the design you heard about. To build it fast, the work was split up: the turret was made at the Novelty Iron Works in Manhattan, the engines at the DeLamater Iron Works, and the hull here in Greenpoint, at Thomas Rowland's Continental Iron Works, where it all came together. Critics mocked it as a cheesebox on a raft. They stopped laughing at Hampton Roads.

Ericsson spent the rest of his life in New York, working at all hours and inventing constantly, from engines driven by hot air to machines powered by the sun. He died in Manhattan in 1889, and the next year the Navy carried his body home to Sweden aboard the cruiser Baltimore. His statue stands in Battery Park.
=== DEEPER The Plume Under Greenpoint | underfoot
You heard that millions of gallons of oil leaked into the ground under Greenpoint. That oil is still part of life here.

It didn't spill all at once. Over many decades, oil from refineries, storage tanks and pipes seeped down through the soil until it reached the groundwater, where it spread out in a vast underground pool, beneath homes, schools and factories, slowly leaking into Newtown Creek. The company held most responsible was ExxonMobil, heir to the Standard Oil companies that had refined and stored oil here since Charles Pratt's day.

For years after the leak was discovered, the cleanup crawled. Recovery wells were drilled to pump the oil up out of the ground, and millions of gallons have been pulled up that way, but the work has stretched over decades.

In the 2000s, residents and environmental groups, and then the state, took ExxonMobil to court. In 2010, the company agreed to a settlement that called for a more thorough cleanup and put millions of dollars into a fund for the neighborhood's environment. That fund has paid for projects across Greenpoint, from green roofs and street trees to programs on the creek.

So as you walk these streets, remember that the story of Greenpoint's oil isn't only history. Some of it is still being pumped out of the ground beneath your feet.
=== STOP Newtown Creek & the Digester Eggs
=== DEEPER Kills, Creeks and Newtown | names
Newtown Creek is named for a town on the far side, in Queens. The Dutch called the village Middelburgh, and the English renamed it Newtown. In the 1890s it became Elmhurst, reportedly in part to shed its connection with the smelly creek.

The creek once wound inland for miles through salt marshes, and its branches still carry Dutch names. Dutch Kills reaches into Long Island City, and English Kills reaches deep into Brooklyn. Kill is an old Dutch word for a creek or channel, and you hear it all over the region: in the Catskill Mountains, and in the Kill Van Kull and the Arthur Kill, the channels that wrap around Staten Island.

The bridges over the creek honor two Polish heroes of the American Revolution, fitting for the Polish neighborhoods on both banks. The Pulaski Bridge, near the creek's mouth, is named for Casimir Pulaski, a cavalry commander who died fighting for the Americans in 1779. Upstream, the Kosciuszko Bridge honors Tadeusz Kosciuszko, the engineer who designed the American defenses at Saratoga and at West Point. Its new cable-stayed spans replaced a bridge from 1939, which was brought down with explosives in 2017.
=== DEEPER Black Mayonnaise | nature
Below the creek's dark surface lies a layer of sludge so foul that people who study it call it black mayonnaise. It's a century and a half of sewage, oil, coal tar and industrial chemicals, settled on the bottom, in places many feet deep.

Why did this creek suffer so much? Newtown Creek is tidal, but it's a dead end. Near the mouth, the tides move the water in and out, but in the upper reaches the water barely moves, so whatever pours in tends to settle and stay. In summer, the oxygen in the water can drop so low that fish struggle to survive.

And the pouring hasn't stopped. Like much of New York, this part of the city has combined sewers, which carry sewage and rainwater in the same pipes. When a heavy rain overwhelms them, the overflow, sewage included, goes straight into the creek, through outfall pipes along its banks.

Since the creek became a Superfund site, in 2010, federal scientists have been mapping the contamination and working out who should pay to clean it up: companies whose predecessors lined the creek, and the city itself, whose sewers still overflow into it. The cleanup will take many years.

And yet, life is coming back. Look along the shore and the old pilings for cormorants drying their wings, and herons stalking the shallows. Crabs and small fish live in the water, and here and there, local groups have planted marsh grasses, like the ones that once lined the whole creek.
=== DEEPER The Most Beautiful Sewage Plant | architecture
Why would a city hire celebrated architects to design a sewage plant? At Newtown Creek, the answer was the neighbors.

The plant opened in 1967, and for decades it was a bad neighbor, outdated and smelly. When the city set out to rebuild and expand it, in a project that ran from the late 1990s into the 2010s and cost billions of dollars, it hired the firm then called the Polshek Partnership, now Ennead Architects, to make the plant something Greenpoint could live with, and even be proud of. Instead of hiding the digesters, the designers made them the plant's signature.

Here's what happens inside. Sewage from about a million people, in parts of Brooklyn, Queens and Manhattan, flows in through big pipes. Screens strain out the trash, and in settling tanks the heavier solids sink. In aeration tanks, air is bubbled through the water, and bacteria eat most of the waste that's left. The cleaned water is disinfected and released into the East River.

The sludge goes to the eggs. Inside, with no oxygen, kept warm, close to body temperature, other bacteria digest it for weeks, turning much of it into a gas that's mostly methane, the same gas that burns on a kitchen stove. The plant uses some of that gas for heat. What's left of the sludge is dried out and trucked away.

So the shining eggs are, in a sense, the city's stomach, and a reminder that what we flush doesn't simply disappear.
