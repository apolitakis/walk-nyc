=== STOP Columbus Circle
=== DEEPER Remember the Maine | history
Turn toward the park. At the corner entrance stands a tall stone pylon crowned by a gilded group: a woman riding in a chariot shaped like a seashell, pulled by three hippocamps, the half horse, half fish creatures of Greek myth. This is the USS Maine National Monument, and its story is as political as the statue in the middle of the circle.

On the night of February 15th, 1898, the American battleship Maine exploded in the harbor of Havana, in Spanish-ruled Cuba. More than two hundred and fifty men died. Nobody knew what had caused the blast, but William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal blamed Spain at once, and its rival, Joseph Pulitzer's World, joined in. Remember the Maine became a slogan, and two months later the United States was at war with Spain. It's the war whose most famous battle may have given San Juan Hill, your next stop, its name.

Hearst's paper also started the collection for a memorial, and it took fifteen years to finish. The monument was dedicated in 1913. The architect was Harold Van Buren Magonigle, and the sculptor was Attilio Piccirilli, of the Bronx family of stone carvers who carved the lions at the Public Library on the Midtown tour. The gilded figure at the top is Columbia Triumphant, and her bronze is said to come partly from metal salvaged from the Maine itself. If you took the Civic Center tour, you may remember Audrey Munson, the model who is said to have posed for her.

So what did sink the Maine? A navy inquiry in 1898 blamed a mine. In 1976, a study led by Admiral Hyman Rickover concluded that the likeliest cause was a fire smoldering in a coal bunker, which set off the ship's own ammunition. Historians are still arguing about it.
=== DEEPER The Lynching Behind the Holiday | politics
You heard that Italian immigrants faced intense discrimination when the Columbus statue went up. One event, the year before it was unveiled, shows just how intense.

In October 1890, the police chief of New Orleans, David Hennessy, was shot near his home and died the next day. He's said to have blamed Italians, and the police rounded up more than a hundred Italian immigrants. Nineteen men were indicted. In March 1891, a jury acquitted some of them and could not reach a verdict on others. The next morning, a crowd of thousands, led by some of the city's most prominent men, broke into the parish prison and killed eleven Italian prisoners, shooting most of them and hanging two. It's often called the largest single lynching in American history.

Italy recalled its ambassador, and for a time there was talk of war. In the end, the United States paid Italy an indemnity of twenty five thousand dollars. And in 1892, President Benjamin Harrison proclaimed a national celebration of the four hundredth anniversary of Columbus's voyage, which many historians see as a gesture toward Italian Americans. New York held days of parades that October, and this statue was unveiled in the middle of them.

Columbus Day became a national holiday under Franklin Roosevelt in the 1930s. And in 2019, the mayor of New Orleans formally apologized to Italian Americans for the lynching. So when you hear the arguments about this statue, remember that it carries two histories at once: the Indigenous people Columbus harmed, and the immigrants who put him up here to say that they belonged.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Columbus in a Living Room | look
Look up the column. Bronze ships' prows jut from its sides, for the Niña, the Pinta and the Santa María, and near its foot, a winged marble figure leans over a globe, as if working out the route. Columbus himself stands at the very top, far above the traffic, too high for anyone on the ground to see his face.

Except for a few weeks in 2012. That fall, the Japanese artist Tatzu Nishi, working with the Public Art Fund, wrapped the top of the monument in scaffolding and built a living room around the statue. Visitors with free timed tickets climbed a long staircase up the scaffolding and stepped into a furnished apartment, with sofas, lamps, a television, and wallpaper Nishi designed, patterned with images from American popular culture. In the middle of the room, standing on the coffee table, was Columbus. The piece was called Discovering Columbus, and for the first time since 1892, people could look him in the eye.

Nishi has built rooms like this around monuments in other cities, and the idea is always the same: something distant and official becomes, for a moment, a very large houseguest.

The circle around you was remade too, in the 2000s, with fountains, benches and plantings. For most of the twentieth century, it was a roaring traffic circle that people on foot crossed at their peril.
=== STOP Lincoln Center & the Lost San Juan Hill
=== DEEPER Opening Night, 1966 | culture
You heard that the Metropolitan Opera House anchors this plaza. The company is much older than its home. It was founded in 1883 by new money, families like the Vanderbilts, who couldn't get boxes at the old Academy of Music on 14th Street, where old New York held the best seats. So they built their own opera house, on Broadway at 39th Street, with a ring of private boxes that came to be called the Diamond Horseshoe. Critics called the building the yellow brick brewery, but for more than eighty years it was the center of opera in America.

The new house here opened on September 16th, 1966, with an opera written for the night: Samuel Barber's Antony and Cleopatra, starring the soprano Leontyne Price. Price had grown up in Laurel, Mississippi, and when she made her Met debut in 1961, the audience reportedly applauded for more than forty minutes. Now she was opening the company's new home.

The production, staged by the Italian director Franco Zeffirelli, was colossal, with a huge cast and a giant revolving stage that broke down in rehearsal. On the night, the critics praised Price and panned almost everything else. Barber was devastated, and years later he revised the opera into a leaner version. The old house on 39th Street was torn down in 1967, despite protests.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Crystal and Color | look
Look through the Met's five arched windows. The two huge paintings inside are by Marc Chagall, each about three stories tall: The Triumph of Music, mostly in reds, and The Sources of Music, mostly in yellows. Look for the floating musicians, the dancers, the angels and the birds. Chagall was nearly eighty when he painted them. In 2009, in the depths of the recession, the Met pledged them as collateral for a loan.

Then look up at the chandeliers, spiky crystal globes like bursting stars. They were a gift from the government of Austria, in thanks for American help in rebuilding the country after the Second World War, and they were made in Vienna by the glassmakers Lobmeyr. If you go to a performance, watch the ones in the auditorium. Just before the music starts, they rise slowly to the ceiling as the lights dim, so that everyone in the upper tiers can see the stage. It's one of the loveliest little rituals in New York.
=== DEEPER The Hall That Couldn't Hear Itself | architecture
You heard that the New York Philharmonic plays in David Geffen Hall, to your right. It's the hall's third name, and its third interior.

It opened in 1962 as Philharmonic Hall, the first building at Lincoln Center to be finished, designed by Max Abramovitz. Leonard Bernstein conducted the opening concert, broadcast live on television, with the First Lady, Jacqueline Kennedy, in the audience. Almost at once, musicians and critics complained about the sound. The players couldn't hear one another, and the bass seemed to vanish. For years, engineers fiddled with panels and ceilings.

In 1973, the audio pioneer Avery Fisher, who had made a fortune selling high fidelity equipment, gave the hall about ten million dollars, and it was renamed for him. In 1976 the whole interior was torn out and rebuilt, with the architects Philip Johnson and John Burgee and the acoustician Cyril Harris. It was better, but it never won many hearts.

In 2015, the entertainment mogul David Geffen gave a hundred million dollars, and Fisher's family was paid fifteen million to give up the name. The hall was rebuilt once more and reopened in October 2022, with the stage pushed forward and the seats wrapping around the orchestra. Among the music written for the reopening was a piece by the trumpeter Etienne Charles called San Juan Hill: A New York Story, a portrait of the neighborhood that was cleared to build this place.
=== DEEPER Where San Juan Hill Went | history
You heard that thousands of families were displaced. The estimates are startling. The Lincoln Square project cleared about eighteen blocks, and historians put the number of families who lost their homes at about seven thousand, most of them Black, Puerto Rican or from the Caribbean, along with some eight hundred businesses. The Housing Authority moved many of the families into public housing elsewhere, and a large share ended up in Brownsville, in Brooklyn, far from the jobs and churches they had known.

The neighborhood didn't go quietly. Residents and shopkeepers took the city to court, arguing, among other things, that public power was being used to clear land for private institutions, including a Catholic university, Fordham, which got a campus just north of here. The courts let the project go ahead.

One piece of the old neighborhood's housing survived. The Amsterdam Houses, public housing finished in the late 1940s, still stand just west of Amsterdam Avenue, between 61st and 64th Streets.

And the memory is coming back. In 2024, the filmmaker Stanley Nelson's documentary San Juan Hill: Manhattan's Lost Neighborhood had its premiere here, at the institution that replaced it.
=== STOP The Dakota
=== DEEPER The Lawyer Behind the Sewing Machine | companies
You heard that the Dakota was built by Edward Clark, the head of the Singer company. If you took the SoHo and Tribeca tour, you met Isaac Singer, the inventor with several families at once. Clark was his opposite, a careful lawyer from upstate New York who took a share of the business in return for his legal work, and ended up running it.

Clark's great idea was about money, not machinery. A sewing machine cost more than most families could pay at once, so in the 1850s, Clark began letting customers buy one with a small payment down and the rest in installments. It's often called one of the first installment plans in American selling. Sales soared, and Singer became one of the first great American multinational companies, with factories in Scotland and agents around the world.

Clark died in 1882, two years before the Dakota opened, so he never saw it finished. But the family's money went on to shape American culture in unexpected places. One grandson, Stephen Clark, founded the National Baseball Hall of Fame, which opened in 1939 in Cooperstown, the upstate village where the family had long kept a home. Another, Sterling Clark, collected French paintings, especially Renoirs, and founded the Clark Art Institute in Williamstown, Massachusetts. So there's a little sewing machine money behind Cooperstown's plaques and the Clark's galleries.

Before you move on, look near the top of the arched entrance on 72nd Street for a carved stone head of a Native American. Whatever the true origin of the building's name, Clark clearly enjoyed it.
=== DEEPER Goodbye, Lenny | people
You heard that Leonard Bernstein lived here. He spent the last years of his life in the Dakota, and of all its famous residents, none was more closely tied to the music of the West Side. If you took the Fifth Avenue tour, you heard that as a young man he lived in a studio above Carnegie Hall.

Bernstein became famous overnight in November 1943, when he was a twenty five year old assistant conductor of the New York Philharmonic. The guest conductor, Bruno Walter, fell ill, and Bernstein stepped in on a few hours' notice, with no rehearsal, for a concert broadcast across the country. The next day, he was on the front page of the New York Times.

In 1957 came West Side Story. The idea had begun years earlier as East Side Story, about a Jewish girl and a Catholic boy on the Lower East Side. By the time it reached Broadway, the story had moved to the West Side, with Puerto Rican and white gangs, in streets like the ones you heard about at Lincoln Center. The next year, Bernstein became music director of the Philharmonic, the first American-born conductor to hold the job, and on television, his Young People's Concerts taught millions of children how to listen.

Bernstein died in his apartment here in October 1990. As his funeral procession left the Dakota and made its way through the streets, it's said that construction workers along the route took off their hard hats, waved, and called out, Goodbye, Lenny.
=== DEEPER Twin Towers on the Park | architecture
You heard that the twin towers of Central Park West make one of the city's best known skylines. They're there because of a law.

For decades, the rules had capped how tall an apartment house could be. Then in 1929, New York State passed the Multiple Dwelling Law, which let apartment buildings on very large lots rise much higher, as long as the upper floors took the form of towers set back from the street. On Central Park West, where whole blockfronts faced the park, several architects chose to build two towers instead of one, giving more apartments views, light and air.

The San Remo, at 74th Street, finished in 1930, was designed by Emery Roth. Each of its towers is crowned with a small round temple of columns, modeled on an ancient monument in Athens, the Choragic Monument of Lysicrates, the same model as the Soldiers' and Sailors' Monument at the end of this walk. Roth also designed the Eldorado, up at 90th Street. The Majestic, at 71st Street, and the Century, at 62nd, were designed by Irwin Chanin, in sleek Art Deco, and both were finished in 1931. The Century took its name from the Century Theatre, a grand and money-losing playhouse that stood on its site until 1930.

The Majestic had a famous tenant: the mob boss Frank Costello. On the night of May 2nd, 1957, as Costello walked into the lobby, a gunman stepped out, said, This is for you, Frank, and fired at his head. The bullet only grazed him. A young enforcer named Vincent Gigante was tried for the shooting, but Costello told the court he couldn't identify the man, and Gigante was acquitted. Costello soon stepped back from the rackets.
=== STOP The Ansonia & Verdi Square
=== DEEPER The Man Behind the Ansonia | people
You heard that William Earl Dodge Stokes kept a farm on the Ansonia's roof. That was among the milder things about him.

Stokes was a restless, combative millionaire who poured much of his inheritance into West Side real estate. He also bred trotting horses on a stock farm in Kentucky, and he believed that what worked for horses would work for people. In 1917 he published a book called The Right to Be Well Born, subtitled Horse Breeding in Its Relation to Eugenics, arguing for the now discredited idea of breeding better human beings.

His private life filled the newspapers. Both of his marriages ended in bitter, public divorces. And in June 1911, he went to an apartment a few blocks north of here, at Broadway and 80th Street, where two young women, Lillian Graham and Ethel Conrad, lived. He said they had lured him there with an offer to return letters he had written to Graham. They said they had acted in self-defense. Either way, they fired at him, and he was hit in the legs. The papers called them showgirls, and the case was a sensation for months. That December, a jury acquitted them both, and by then they had already gone on the vaudeville stage as a double act.

Stokes recovered, and went on building, suing and quarreling until his death in 1926. The Ansonia, which he meant as his monument, has outlasted the scandals by a century.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Verdi and His Characters | look
Step into the little park and look at the monument. Verdi stands on top in white marble, and around the base sit four of his characters, each facing a different way. There's Aida, the Ethiopian princess enslaved in Egypt. There's Otello, the general destroyed by jealousy. There's Leonora, the doomed heroine of La forza del destino, The Force of Destiny. And there's Falstaff, the fat, boastful knight of his last opera, a comedy he wrote when he was nearly eighty.

The sculptor was Pasquale Civiletti, from Sicily, and the monument was unveiled in October 1906, five years after Verdi's death. The money came from Italian New Yorkers, through a campaign led by the same man behind the Columbus statue at the start of this walk, Carlo Barsotti, the founder of the newspaper Il Progresso Italo-Americano. Barsotti also championed statues of Giovanni da Verrazzano, in Battery Park, and of Dante, in the small park across the street from Lincoln Center. Each one was a claim, in stone and bronze, that Italian culture belonged in New York.

Verdi was the obvious choice for a monument. In Italy he was a national hero. A famous story says that in the years before Italy was united, patriots scrawled Viva Verdi on walls as a code, the letters standing for Vittorio Emanuele, Re d'Italia, Victor Emmanuel, King of Italy. Historians suspect the slogan owes more to later legend than to the time itself, but the story tells you what Verdi meant to the immigrants who paid for this statue.

The little park was the northern end of Sherman Square until 1921, when it was named for Verdi. It's now one of the city's scenic landmarks, a rare honor for a patch of ground this small.
=== DEEPER Needle Park | politics
You heard that this square was once called Needle Park. The name came from the street, and so did the film.

In 1965, Life magazine ran a photo essay by Bill Eppridge about a young couple addicted to heroin, who spent their days around these few blocks of Broadway. The writer James Mills, who reported the story, turned it into a book, The Panic in Needle Park. The nickname covered both little triangles at this intersection, Verdi Square and Sherman Square, just to the south.

The 1971 film was directed by Jerry Schatzberg and written by Joan Didion and her husband, John Gregory Dunne, and much of it was shot on these streets. Kitty Winn won the best actress prize at the Cannes Film Festival for playing the young woman. She played Helen, a young woman who drifts into the life, and her costar, as Bobby, a small-time dealer, was a little-known actor named Al Pacino, in his first leading role. A year later, he was Michael Corleone in The Godfather.

The film came out as the city's heroin crisis was at its worst. In those years, upper Broadway was lined with cheap single room occupancy hotels, and many of the grand old buildings around you had fallen on hard times. The neighborhood's turnaround came slowly, through the 1980s and 1990s, and today the old days are hard to picture. But West Siders who lived through them still remember the park by its old name.
=== STOP The American Museum of Natural History
=== DEEPER The Boy Who Asked for His Father | politics
You heard about the Roosevelt statue that was removed from the front steps. The museum's own history holds a harder story, about a boy named Minik.

In 1897, the explorer Robert Peary came back from northern Greenland with an enormous iron meteorite, which you can still see in the museum, and with six Inuit people, brought to New York partly so the museum's scientists could study them. Among them were a man named Qisuk and his son, Minik, who was about seven. They were lodged at the museum, and within months most of them were sick. By the spring of 1898, four had died, including Qisuk.

The museum staged a funeral for the boy's sake. Minik watched a coffin being buried on the grounds, not knowing it held no body. His father's remains had been kept for study, and the skeleton went into the museum's collection. Minik was taken in by the museum's building superintendent, William Wallace, and grew up in New York as Minik Wallace. Years later, newspapers revealed that Qisuk's skeleton was at the museum, and Minik begged for his father's body. The museum refused.

Minik went home to Greenland, came back to the United States, and died in the flu pandemic of 1918, while working at a lumber camp in New Hampshire. Only in 1993, after years of pressure, did the museum send the remains of Qisuk and the three others back to Greenland, where they were finally buried.
=== DEEPER Akeley's Africa | nature
The dioramas you heard about owe the most to one man, Carl Akeley, a farm boy from upstate New York who reinvented taxidermy. Older taxidermists stuffed skins with straw and rags. Akeley sculpted the animal's body, muscle by muscle, made a light form from the sculpture, and stretched the skin over it, so the animals looked alive. Early in his career, he mounted P. T. Barnum's famous elephant, Jumbo, after it was killed by a train in 1885.

He was no armchair naturalist. In Somaliland in 1896, a wounded leopard sprang at him, and he killed it with his bare hands.

In 1921, Akeley went to the volcanoes of central Africa to collect mountain gorillas for the museum, and watching them changed him. He came home convinced they had to be protected, and he persuaded King Albert of Belgium to set aside their mountains. Albert National Park, created in 1925, was the first national park in Africa. Akeley died there in 1926, on his next expedition, and he's buried on the slopes of Mount Mikeno, in what's now the Democratic Republic of the Congo. His wife, Mary Jobe Akeley, carried on his work. The great hall that bears his name, with its herd of elephants in the center, opened in 1936.
=== DEEPER The Dinosaur Hunters | history
Two of the museum's greatest finds came from its own fossil hunters, and one came with a surprise ending.

Barnum Brown, reportedly named for the showman P. T. Barnum, was the museum's star collector. In 1902, in the Hell Creek badlands of Montana, he found the first partial skeleton of a giant meat eater that the museum's leading paleontologist, Henry Fairfield Osborn, named Tyrannosaurus rex. In 1908, Brown found a far more complete one, and that's the skeleton on display today. In 1941, the museum sold the first one to the Carnegie Museum in Pittsburgh, reportedly in part from fear that New York might be bombed in the war.

In the 1920s, the explorer Roy Chapman Andrews led the museum's expeditions into the Gobi Desert of Mongolia, in a convoy of cars supplied by camel caravans. In 1923, at a place called the Flaming Cliffs, his team found nests of fossil eggs, the first dinosaur eggs recognized by science. They were thought to belong to a plant eater called Protoceratops. Nearby lay a toothless skeleton, which Osborn named Oviraptor, the egg seizer, assuming it had died raiding the nest.

Seventy years later, in 1993, a museum expedition found a tiny embryo inside one of the same kind of eggs. It was an oviraptor. Later finds showed oviraptor relatives sitting on their nests with their arms spread, like brooding birds. The egg thief had been a parent all along.
=== DEEPER The Night of the Star of India | history
On the night of October 29th, 1964, three young men from Florida climbed into the museum through a window that had been left open, and made their way into the hall of gems. The alarm on the case holding the most famous stone, the Star of India, had a dead battery.

The ringleader was Jack Murphy, a surfer known as Murph the Surf. They took about two dozen gems, among them the Star of India, a blue star sapphire of more than five hundred and sixty carats, about the size of a golf ball, along with the DeLong Star Ruby and the Eagle Diamond. The police caught up with them within days. In January 1965, the Star of India and most of the other stones turned up in a locker at a Miami bus station. The DeLong ruby came back later, after a ransom of twenty five thousand dollars was paid. The Eagle Diamond was never found.

The Star of India, a gift to the museum from J. P. Morgan, is back on display, under better security.
=== STOP Zabar's & Upper Broadway
=== DEEPER The Caviar War | companies
You heard that the Zabar family still runs the store. Louis Zabar died in 1950, at only forty nine, and his son Saul, in his early twenties, took over, and his brother Stanley later joined him. Their youngest brother, Eli, would go his own way and build a food business of his own on the East Side.

The man who made Zabar's famous for its prices was Murray Klein, a former employee who came back as a partner around 1960 and stayed for more than thirty years. Klein loved a price war. In the 1970s, he picked a fight with Cuisinart by selling its new food processors at a deep discount.

His most famous battle came in the holiday season of 1983, against Macy's. It started with Pommery mustard, moved on to Lindt chocolate, and ended with Beluga caviar. Macy's put fourteen ounces of Beluga on sale for one hundred and forty nine dollars. Klein cut Zabar's price to one hundred and nineteen, selling at a loss rather than lose. The papers called it the caviar war, and the Zabars always said they won.

Klein retired in 1994. Saul Zabar became a legend in his own right as the store's master of smoked fish, and the bright orange bags still carry the family name.
=== DEEPER The Writer Who Fed the Pigeons | culture
As you may have heard on the Lower East Side tour, Isaac Bashevis Singer's stories first appeared in Yiddish, in the Forward. For most of his American life, though, he was an Upper West Sider.

Singer was born in Poland and came to New York in 1935, following his older brother, the novelist Israel Joshua Singer. He kept writing in Yiddish all his life, even as the number of its readers shrank, and he found a far larger audience in English translation, one of the first by the novelist Saul Bellow. In 1965 he moved into the Belnord, the huge courtyard building that fills the block between Broadway and Amsterdam Avenue at 86th Street, six blocks north of here, and he lived there for decades.

He was a vegetarian, and he ate in the neighborhood's dairy restaurants and cafeterias, which turn up in his fiction. His story The Cafeteria is set in one on Broadway. And he fed the pigeons on the Broadway median, a habit that found its way into his stories too.

In 1978 he won the Nobel Prize in Literature, the only writer in Yiddish ever to receive it. In his Nobel lecture, he joked that he liked to write ghost stories, and nothing suits a ghost better than a dying language. A few years later, his story Yentl became a film with Barbra Streisand, which Singer made no secret of disliking. In 1990, the year before he died, the city named the stretch of West 86th Street between Broadway and Amsterdam Avenue Isaac Bashevis Singer Boulevard. Look for the sign if you walk that way.
=== DEEPER The Loyalist's Elms | names
You heard that William Waldorf Astor built the Apthorp. He borrowed its name from the neighborhood's colonial past.

Charles Ward Apthorp was a rich merchant who, in 1764, built a country house about a dozen blocks north of here, near today's Columbus Avenue and 91st Street. It was known as Elmwood, after its trees, and a lane connected it to the Bloomingdale Road. In the Revolution, Apthorp stayed loyal to the King.

In September 1776, after the defeat on Long Island, George Washington used the house briefly as his headquarters while the American army fell back. According to one account, the British moved in within hours of his leaving. A few years after Apthorp's death, in 1799, the house was sold to a wealthy Englishman named William Jauncey, and the lanes leading to it took his name.

Later in the 1800s, the old grounds became Elm Park, a picnic ground and beer garden. On July 12th, 1870, Irish Protestants of the Orange Order held a picnic there to celebrate an old Protestant victory in Ireland, and it ended in a battle with Irish Catholic laborers. Several people were killed, in the first of the city's two Orange Riots. The house came down in the 1890s.

Astor's apartment house revived the name in 1908, when a whiff of old New York was a selling point.
=== STOP Riverside Park & the Soldiers' and Sailors' Monument
=== DEEPER The War on Noise | politics
Look at the freestanding mansion on the corner of 89th Street, facing the park. This is the Isaac L. Rice Mansion, finished in 1903, one of only two freestanding mansions left on Riverside Drive. Isaac Rice was a lawyer and entrepreneur who founded the Electric Boat Company, which built the Navy's first submarines, and he was such a devoted chess patron that an opening, the Rice Gambit, carries his name.

The more famous Rice, though, was his wife, Julia Barnett Rice. She had earned a medical degree, though she never practiced, and she was convinced that noise was a danger to health. All night, the tugboats on the Hudson blew their whistles outside her windows, many of them, she was sure, for no reason at all. So she recruited students from Columbia to sit up and count. Their tallies ran to thousands of blasts in a single night.

In 1906 she founded the Society for the Suppression of Unnecessary Noise. In 1907, Congress passed a law against needless whistling by boats in the harbor, and the society went on to win quiet zones around the city's hospitals, and later its schools, with a children's branch in the public schools. It was one of the first organized campaigns against noise in America.

Since 1954, the house has been a yeshiva, a Jewish school.
=== DEEPER The Freedom Tunnel | underfoot
You heard that trains still run in a tunnel beneath this park. After the last freight trains stopped using it, around 1980, the tunnel went dark, and homeless New Yorkers moved in. They built shacks and even small houses along the tracks, lit by the shafts of daylight that fall through the grates in the park above you.

A graffiti artist named Chris Pape, who signed his work Freedom, used the long concrete walls as his canvas, painting enormous murals in those shafts of light, and the tunnel came to be known by his name.

In 1991, Amtrak began running passenger trains through again, bringing its trains from Albany straight into Penn Station, and the people living inside faced eviction. A young Englishman named Marc Singer, who had lived among them, made a documentary about the community, Dark Days, shot in black and white with a crew drawn from the tunnel's own residents. It came out in 2000 and won awards at the Sundance Film Festival. By the time the last residents left, many had been helped into apartments with housing vouchers.

Amtrak trains still rumble beneath the park. Stand near a grate long enough and you may hear one.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Joan at 93rd Street | look
Four blocks north, at 93rd Street, Joan of Arc rides into battle in bronze, in full armor, raising her sword. The sculptor, Anna Hyatt Huntington, was one of the finest animal sculptors in America. She worked on her model in Paris, where it won an honorable mention at the Salon in 1910, and the statue was dedicated in December 1915, while France was fighting for its life in the First World War.

Look closely at the pedestal. The committee behind the statue, which included Tiffany's famous gem expert, George Frederick Kunz, learned in 1912 that the tower in Rouen where Joan was imprisoned before she was burned at the stake was being cleared away. They bought more than two hundred of its stone blocks, which crossed the Atlantic in 1914. A fragment of the cathedral at Reims, where Joan saw her king crowned, is set into the base too.

It's often called the first statue in New York of a real woman, rather than a goddess or an allegory.
=== DEEPER The Cliffs Across the River | nature
Look across the Hudson at the Palisades, the wall of cliffs running north along the New Jersey shore. They were born underground about two hundred million years ago, when molten rock forced its way between layers of sandstone and shale and cooled slowly into a thick sheet of hard, dark stone called diabase. As it cooled, it shrank and cracked into tall columns. Over millions of years, the softer rock wore away, and the columns were left standing like a fence of logs. That's the name: a palisade is a wall of upright stakes, like the one that gave Wall Street its name on the Financial District tour.

By the 1890s, quarries were blasting the cliffs apart for crushed stone. The campaign to stop them was led by the women's clubs of New Jersey, and in 1900, New York and New Jersey created the Palisades Interstate Park Commission. J. P. Morgan put up money to buy out the worst of the quarries. Later, John D. Rockefeller Junior bought land along the clifftops and gave it to the park, partly to protect the view from the Cloisters, the museum he paid for in northern Manhattan.

So the view from this terrace is no accident. It was saved.
