=== DEEPER The Man Who Had Never Designed a Park | people
You heard that Frederick Law Olmsted had never designed a park when he and Calvert Vaux won the competition for this one. That's true, and the life around it is one of the great American careers.

Olmsted was born in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1822. A poisoning that damaged his eyes cut short his schooling, and for years he drifted. He sailed to China as a young seaman, and ran a farm on Staten Island. He walked through England and wrote a book about its farms and countryside, and near Liverpool, he saw a new public park, at Birkenhead, that he never forgot.

Then a newspaper sent him south. In the 1850s, Olmsted traveled through the slave states for the paper that became the New York Times, and his dispatches, later collected in a book called The Cotton Kingdom, were a detailed and damning portrait of slavery and the society built on it. In 1857, the commissioners of this park hired him as its superintendent, a writer and farmer who knew more about land and people than about design. Vaux, the trained architect, invited him to enter the competition as his partner.

After Central Park, Olmsted kept going. During the Civil War, he ran the Sanitary Commission, which cared for sick and wounded Union soldiers. He managed a gold mine in California and helped protect Yosemite Valley. He and Vaux designed Prospect Park, and Olmsted went on to shape the grounds of the United States Capitol, Boston's chain of parks called the Emerald Necklace, and the Biltmore estate in North Carolina.

Dementia clouded his last years, which he spent at McLean, a hospital outside Boston whose grounds he had helped to plan. He died in 1903. But you're standing in the work he's remembered for.
=== DEEPER The Gates Without Gates | names
Look at the entrance at Fifth Avenue and 60th Street. There's no gate, just an opening in the low stone wall. That was a choice, and it took a fight.

In 1862, the park's commissioners decided to give the entrances names, and they turned down the obvious ideas: no war victories, no states or cities, no famous men. Instead, the gates would honor the people of New York by their work. Along the southern edge, this one became the Scholars' Gate, and to the west come the Artists', the Artisans' and the Merchants' Gates. Farther up, there are gates for Engineers and Inventors, Mariners and Miners, Woodmen and Hunters, and for Women, Children, Boys and Girls. There's even one for Strangers. The park, the names said, belonged to everyone who worked in the city.

Then came Richard Morris Hunt, the Paris-trained architect you may have heard about on Fifth Avenue, who later designed Alva Vanderbilt's château. Hunt drew grand, monumental gateways for the southern entrances, in the manner of imperial Paris, with sculpture, and the commissioners liked them. Olmsted and Vaux were appalled. To them, triumphal gates belonged to an emperor's capital, not a republic's park. In 1863, partly over the gates, they resigned. Hunt's gates were never built, and two years later, Olmsted and Vaux were back.

The names appeared on maps, but for well over a century, you couldn't see them anywhere at the entrances themselves. Only around the year 2000 were they finally carved into the stone of the walls. Look for this one as you go.
=== DEEPER Where Do the Ducks Go? | culture
In J. D. Salinger's novel The Catcher in the Rye, published in 1951, the teenage narrator, Holden Caulfield, can't stop wondering about this pond. He calls it the lagoon near Central Park South, and what bothers him is the ducks. Where do they go in winter, when the water freezes over? Does somebody come in a truck and take them away to a zoo, or do they just fly off?

Lonely and adrift in the city after being thrown out of his school, Holden asks two different taxi drivers. The first looks at him as if he's crazy. And the novel doesn't leave the park. It ends a few minutes' walk from here, as you'll hear at the carousel.

=== DEEPER The Day the Animals Escaped | history
On the morning of November 9th, 1874, New Yorkers opened the New York Herald to a horrifying story. The animals of the Central Park menagerie, it said, had broken loose. A rhinoceros had smashed its cage and set others free, and big cats and bears were roaming the streets, mauling people as they went. The article ran on for columns, with lists of the dead and injured.

None of it was true. As you may have heard on Park Row, New York's papers had a weakness for hoaxes, and this was one of the biggest. The story's last paragraph admitted that the whole thing was invented. But many readers never got that far. Men took guns into the streets to hunt for escaped beasts.

The idea reportedly came from the Herald's managing editor, Thomas Connery, after he saw a leopard nearly get loose while it was being moved at the menagerie. The paper claimed it wanted to warn the city that the cages were unsafe. The Herald, then run by James Gordon Bennett Junior, whose owls you may have heard about at Herald Square, loved a stunt.

Rival papers were furious, and a group of citizens went to the district attorney to ask whether the Herald could be prosecuted. It never was, and its sales didn't suffer. The menagerie stayed beside the Arsenal for another sixty years, until Robert Moses replaced it with a proper zoo.
=== DEEPER Balto | legends
Walk a little north of the zoo, toward the East Drive at about 67th Street, and look for a bronze sled dog standing on a rock. His back and ears have been rubbed shiny by generations of children climbing on him. This is Balto, and in 1925, he was the most famous dog in America.

That January, diphtheria broke out in Nome, a remote town on the coast of Alaska. Children were falling ill, and the town's supply of antitoxin was nearly gone. The nearest serum was about a thousand miles away, in Anchorage. So the serum went north by train to the end of the line, and from there, relay teams of mushers and sled dogs carried it nearly seven hundred miles, through blizzards and temperatures far below zero. It reached Nome in less than six days.

Balto led the team that ran the last stretch into Nome, so he got the headlines, and in December 1925, this statue was unveiled with Balto himself in attendance. The inscription honors all the dogs of the relay, for their endurance, fidelity and intelligence.

Here's where the legend and the record part ways. The longest and most dangerous leg was run by a musher named Leonhard Seppala and his lead dog, Togo, who covered about twice the distance of any other team, including a crossing of the treacherous sea ice of Norton Sound. Seppala felt that Togo had been robbed of the credit. Lately, Togo has won some of it back, with books, a film, and a small statue in Seward Park, on the Lower East Side.

Balto himself ended up in a dime museum in Los Angeles, until the people of Cleveland raised money to bring him and his teammates to their zoo. When you pat this bronze dog, spare a thought for Togo, too.
=== DEEPER Gus, the Bear Who Swam in Circles | nature
In the 1990s, the most famous animal in this zoo was a polar bear named Gus.

Gus was born at the Toledo Zoo in 1985 and came here in 1988, just as the rebuilt zoo opened. Visitors could watch him swim through a window into his pool. But in 1994, people began to notice something odd. Gus swam the same pattern over and over, for hours a day, pushing off the same wall and turning at the same spot, around and around.

Behavior like this is common in wide-ranging animals kept in small spaces, and polar bears in the wild roam across vast stretches of sea ice. Newspapers called Gus neurotic, and he became a symbol of a hard question: can a zoo ever be a good home for an animal like that?

The zoo took it seriously. It brought in an animal behaviorist from California, at a cost of about twenty-five thousand dollars, and changed Gus's days. Keepers froze his food into blocks of ice and hid treats in puzzles, and gave him new toys, so that he had to search and work and think. The swimming eased. Ideas like these, called enrichment, are now standard in good zoos everywhere, and Gus helped make them famous.

He lived here for twenty-five years, and more than twenty million people came to see him. In 2013, at twenty-seven, he was found to have an inoperable tumor and was put to sleep. New Yorkers mourned him like a neighbor.

=== DEEPER The Swill Milk Scandal | food
You heard that swill milk, from cows fed on distillery waste, was blamed for the deaths of thousands of children. How New York finally faced it is a story about a newspaper, and about a politician who tried to make it go away.

In May 1858, Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper began a crusade. Its artists went into the distillery dairies of Manhattan and Brooklyn and drew what they found: hundreds of cows crammed into dark, filthy sheds, sick and covered in sores, fed on hot mash piped in from the distillery next door. One engraving showed a dead cow being dragged out just after milking. The thin, bluish milk was whitened with chalk and sold as pure country milk.

The city ordered an investigation and put an alderman named Michael Tuomey in charge. Tuomey defended the distillers, and his committee cleared them. Leslie's answered with a cartoon of politicians painting a sickly cow white, while a distiller slipped a bag of money into Tuomey's pocket. From then on, he was known as Swill Milk Tuomey.

But the public's anger didn't fade, and in 1862, the state legislature passed a law against swill milk. Enforcement stayed weak for decades, but the fight for safe food had begun. And it explains why this little Gothic building, with its promise of clean milk from country cows, felt like a gift to the children of the city.
=== DEEPER The Rink That Took Six Years, and Then Four Months | politics
You heard that the Wollman Rink, down the hill to the south, opened in 1950. In the 1980s, it became the setting for a very New York political story.

In 1980, the city closed the rink to rebuild it. The work went badly wrong, with leaking pipes and endless delays, and six years and about twelve million dollars later, the rink was still shut.

In May 1986, a developer named Donald Trump wrote to Mayor Ed Koch offering to take over the job. After a very public exchange of letters, Koch accepted. Trump's team brought in experienced rink builders, and the rink reopened that November, about four months after work began and well under its budget of three million dollars.

The episode made Trump a symbol of private speed against public failure, and he told the story for years afterward. Critics pointed out that the city paid for the work, and that Trump's company went on to run the rink as a profitable concession, so the deal was good business as well as good publicity.

His company managed the rink for decades, and later the carousel too. In 2021, after the attack on the United States Capitol, the city moved to end its contracts with the Trump Organization, and his name came off the rink.

Whatever you make of the politics, on a winter night, the rink is one of the loveliest sights in the city.
=== DEEPER The Carvers from Williamsburg | companies
Take a closer look at the carousel's horses. They're big, muscular animals with flaring nostrils, and manes that whip back as if they're galloping into a gale. That's the signature of their carvers, Solomon Stein and Harry Goldstein.

Stein and Goldstein worked as carvers in Brooklyn, in the years when Coney Island was the carousel capital of America. In 1908, they built this one at their own shop in Williamsburg, which they called the Artistic Carousel Manufacturing Company. Their horses are prime examples of what collectors call the Coney Island style: flashy, theatrical animals decked with jewels and gold.

This carousel spent its first decades in a trolley terminal at Coney Island, and it had been left there, abandoned, by the time the park needed a replacement for its own burned carousel. Restored and brought here in 1951, it's officially named for Michael Friedsam, a president of the B. Altman department store, whose foundation helped pay for it.

If you've read The Catcher in the Rye, you may remember that the novel ends at the carousel on this spot. On a rainy Sunday, Holden Caulfield walks his little sister, Phoebe, past the sea lions at the zoo and brings her here. She rides around and around on a big brown horse, reaching for the gold ring, while he sits on a bench getting soaked, and feels, all of a sudden, so happy he could almost cry.

=== DEEPER Draft Cards in the Meadow | politics
You heard that Sheep Meadow became a gathering place for protests against the Vietnam War. The biggest began here on April 15th, 1967.

That Saturday, a coalition called the Spring Mobilization to End the War in Vietnam filled this lawn with an enormous crowd, ready to march to the United Nations. Estimates of its size ran from about a hundred thousand to several hundred thousand. At the front of the march were Martin Luther King Junior, who had come out publicly against the war just eleven days before, in a speech at Riverside Church, along with the singer Harry Belafonte and the baby doctor Benjamin Spock.

Before the march set off, something happened here that the organizers hadn't planned, and many of them didn't want. A group of young men, many of them students from Cornell University in Ithaca, gathered in the meadow, and one after another, they set fire to their draft cards. Burning a draft card was a federal crime, punishable by up to five years in prison, and they did it in front of the cameras. They claimed that nearly two hundred cards were burned, though nobody could check. The next morning's New York Times headline read: Many Draft Cards Burned. Eggs Tossed at Parade.

Then the crowd poured out toward the United Nations, where King spoke. It was one of the largest antiwar protests the country had yet seen.
=== DEEPER A Happening in Central Park | culture
On June 17th, 1967, Barbra Streisand gave a free concert on this meadow. She was twenty-five, and already a star of records, Broadway and television. The crowd was enormous, by her own count about a hundred and thirty-five thousand people, spread across the grass as far as she could see.

The concert was filmed for a television special called A Happening in Central Park, and on film, it looks like a triumph. But during the evening, she forgot the words to a song, and by some of her own accounts, to more than one. The night of her biggest audience was also the night that kept Barbra Streisand off the concert stage for a generation.
=== DEEPER Tavern on the Green | food
Look toward the western edge of the meadow for the restaurant you heard about, Tavern on the Green. Its story runs from sheep to glitter.

The building began in 1870 as the Sheepfold, designed by Calvert Vaux and Jacob Wrey Mould in a Victorian Gothic style, a home for the flock and its shepherd. In 1934, Robert Moses sent the sheep away and turned the building into a restaurant, which opened that October.

Then, in the 1970s, came Warner LeRoy. He was the son of the Hollywood director Mervyn LeRoy, who had produced The Wizard of Oz, and he had a showman's taste for spectacle. He took over the lease and spent about ten million dollars turning the Tavern into a fantasy, with chandeliers, stained glass, topiary animals on the lawn, and a glass-walled Crystal Room, unveiled in 1976. Critics often sniffed at the food, but nobody came for the food alone. Tourists, wedding parties, celebrities and birthday crowds packed it, and it became one of the highest-grossing restaurants in the United States, taking in about thirty-eight million dollars as late as 2007.

LeRoy died in 2001, and his family ran it until the city put the lease up for bid. The family's company lost the lease and went bankrupt, and on the last night of 2009, the Tavern served its last dinners. Even the chandeliers were auctioned off. It has since reopened under new owners, and the old Sheepfold still stands: a building made for sheep that became a palace for parties.

=== DEEPER The Most Famous Poet You've Never Heard Of | people
On the Literary Walk, three of the writers need no introduction: Shakespeare, the statue the Booth brothers raised money for, as you may have heard at Gramercy Park, and the Scottish poets Robert Burns and Walter Scott. The fourth, seated in bronze, is Fitz-Greene Halleck. He's the only American on the walk, and in his day, he was one of the most popular poets in the country.

Halleck came to New York from Connecticut as a young man. In 1819, he and his close friend Joseph Rodman Drake published a series of witty, satirical poems about the city's notables in the Evening Post, signed The Croakers, and all New York laughed along. When Drake died the next year, at twenty-five, Halleck wrote an elegy for him with lines that people quoted for a century: Green be the turf above thee, friend of my better days. None knew thee but to love thee, nor named thee but to praise. Historians of gay New York have pointed to the depth of that friendship.

Halleck's poem about a Greek hero, Marco Bozzaris, was recited by schoolchildren across America. And for years, he earned his living as the private secretary of John Jacob Astor, the richest man in the country.

When Halleck died in 1867, his admirers, led by the poet and editor William Cullen Bryant, raised money for this statue. On May 15th, 1877, President Rutherford B. Hayes came to unveil it, with members of his cabinet, and the crowd was so big that it overwhelmed the stands.

Then the world forgot him. Tastes changed, and his poems began to seem stiff and old-fashioned. Halleck sits here as a lesson in the fickleness of fame: a president came to honor him, and today, hardly anyone stops to read his name.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Concert Ground | look
Walk to the north end of the Mall, to the open plaza in front of the Naumburg Bandshell. This was designed as the Concert Ground, and music was played here for sixty years before the bandshell was built.

It started with Jacob Wrey Mould, the architect behind so much of the park's decoration, who was also an amateur musician. In the park's first years, Mould persuaded wealthy friends to pay for free band concerts in the Ramble. The crowds were so big that the park's board took them over, and in 1862, it built Mould's Music Pavilion here, a Moorish fantasy in wood and cast iron, brightly painted and gilded. Bands played there on fine Wednesday and Saturday afternoons.

By the 1920s, the old pavilion was worn out, and its sound was poor. A retired banker named Elkan Naumburg, who loved music, had been paying for free orchestral concerts here since 1905, and in 1921, he offered the city a hundred thousand dollars for a new bandshell. His nephew, William Tachau, designed the neoclassical shell you see, which opened in 1923. The Naumburg concerts still go on every summer, one of the oldest series of free outdoor classical concerts in the country.

Now look around the edges of the plaza for the busts. Beethoven is here, a gift from a German singing society in 1884, and so is Victor Herbert, the composer of operettas. Nearby is the poet Friedrich Schiller, whose bust, by most accounts, was the very first sculpture placed in Central Park, in 1859.
=== DEEPER The Skaters of the Dead Road | culture
On a summer weekend, walk west from the bandshell toward a short stretch of old roadway that no longer carries traffic, and listen for the music. You'll probably find a circle of roller skaters, young and old, some of them gray-haired veterans, dancing, spinning and gliding to disco, funk and house music from a DJ's speakers.

The spot is called the Dead Road, and skaters have been gathering here since the late 1970s, when roller skating and disco were taking off together across the city. A skate rental shop opened nearby, and soon this stretch of pavement became an open-air roller disco, where skaters of every background invented moves and showed them off.

The good times didn't last without a fight. In the 1990s, after the area was repaved during the restoration of the Bandshell and Bethesda Terrace, the skaters faced the threat of being banned from the park. They organized, forming the Central Park Dance Skaters Association, to keep the circle alive and to work things out with the city.

It worked. Decades later, the circle still turns, with veterans who have skated here since the disco days teaching newcomers the moves. It's one of the most joyful sights in the park, and a living reminder that a great public space is shaped not only by its designers, but by the people who make it their own.

=== DEEPER The Sculptor and the Actress | people
You heard that Emma Stebbins, the sculptor of the Angel of the Waters, shared her life with the actress Charlotte Cushman. Their story begins in Rome.

Stebbins came from a wealthy New York family, and in the 1850s, in middle age, she went to Rome to study sculpture, encouraged by her brother Henry. There she met Cushman, the most celebrated American actress of the day, a commanding figure famous for playing Lady Macbeth, and for playing men, above all Romeo. Cushman's household in Rome became the center of a circle of independent American women sculptors, working in marble, whom the novelist Henry James later described as a white, marmorean flock. Stebbins and Cushman stayed together for the rest of Cushman's life.

The fountain was a remarkable commission for a woman in the 1860s, and not everyone thought Stebbins had won it fairly. Her brother Henry had become president of the board that ran Central Park, and Cushman used her own connections on her behalf. Stebbins modeled the angel in Rome over several years, and the casting in bronze was held up by the Franco-Prussian War.

When the fountain was finally unveiled in 1873, the reviews were mixed, and some critics were scornful. Time has been kinder. The angel, wings spread, blessing the water with one hand and holding lilies in the other, has become one of the best-loved figures in New York.

By then, Cushman was fighting breast cancer, and Stebbins nursed her until her death in 1876, then wrote a book about her life. They lie far apart, Cushman near Boston and Stebbins in Brooklyn. But here, at the heart of the park, the angel stands as their monument too.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Witch on the Stairs | look
Before you go down to the fountain, go back to the top of the terrace, along the road, and look closely at the carved stone posts and panels beside the staircases.

The theme was chosen by Calvert Vaux, and the carving was designed by Jacob Wrey Mould: the turning of the seasons and the hours of the day, told through plants, birds and animals. Look for the seasons in flowers, fruit, leaves and birds. Look for a rooster crowing in the morning, and an owl for the night.

Now find the most famous carving of all. On the western face of one of the posts at the top of the terrace, a witch flies across the stone on her broomstick, and beside her is a jack-o'-lantern. Either way, she's a wonderful surprise to find on a Victorian terrace.

Mould and his carvers covered the terrace in scene after scene like these, and most visitors walk right past them. Over the decades, soot and weather wore many of them down, and restorers have since cleaned and repaired them with great care. Take a few minutes to hunt for your favorite.
=== DEEPER Vaux, the Partner in the Shadow | people
Bethesda Terrace is the architectural heart of the park, and above all, it's the work of Calvert Vaux. Yet for more than a century, most of the park's fame went to his partner.

Vaux was born in London in 1824 and trained as an architect there. In 1850, the American landscape designer Andrew Jackson Downing, one of the first to call for a great park in New York, came to London looking for an architect, and hired him. Two years later, Downing was killed when a Hudson River steamboat caught fire. Vaux was left without his mentor in a new country, but he stayed, and he carried on Downing's dream of a park. He named one of his sons Downing.

It was Vaux who saw the 1857 competition as a chance, and Vaux who asked Olmsted, then the park's superintendent, to join him. Olmsted knew the ground, and Vaux knew design. Their Greensward Plan won. But Olmsted held the official title in charge of the work, and the newspapers and the public came to think of the park as Olmsted's. Vaux fought, sometimes bitterly, to have his share recognized.

He went on to design Prospect Park and Morningside Park with Olmsted, and, with Mould, the first buildings of the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the American Museum of Natural History.

In November 1895, at seventy, Vaux went out for a walk near Gravesend Bay in Brooklyn, and didn't come home. His body was found in the water two days later. Whether he slipped or meant to go has never been settled. Stand on the terrace and look out at what he made. This is his monument.

=== DEEPER The Foundry Behind the Bridge | companies
You heard that Bow Bridge is one of the oldest cast-iron bridges in America. Its ironwork came from a foundry that was working on something much bigger at the same time.

The firm was Janes, Kirtland and Company, an ironworks that turned out all kinds of ornamental iron. Bow Bridge, finished in 1862, is its little cousin in the park.

That's where a favorite park legend comes in. For years, people said that the bridge rests on cannonballs, set under one end so that the span could roll back and forth as it expands and contracts. It's a lovely image: a bridge riding on ammunition. But when the bridge was restored, workers went looking for the cannonballs, and found none at all. The bridge still breathes with the seasons, just without the cannonballs.
=== DEEPER The Ladies' Pond | history
You heard that in the nineteenth century, the Lake was the park's great skating pond. Not all of it was open to everyone.

On winter days in the park's early years, tens of thousands of skaters crowded onto the ice. For women in long skirts, a jostling crowd of strangers on skates could be daunting, so a small pond off the Lake's western arm was set aside as the Ladies' Pond, for women skaters. On the rocky point called Hernshead, a little shelter called the Ladies' Cottage gave them a place to warm up and put on their skates. In 1860, a young illustrator named Winslow Homer drew a scene for Harper's Weekly of women skating on the Ladies' Pond, in bonnets and wide skirts, gliding past the bare trees.

The Ladies' Pond was filled in long ago. But look west across the water toward Hernshead for a delicate cast-iron pavilion with a lacy roof. That's the Ladies' Pavilion, designed by Jacob Wrey Mould in 1871, and it didn't start out here. It was built as a shelter for people waiting for streetcars at the park's entrance at Columbus Circle. Around 1912, it was moved to Hernshead to make way for the Maine Monument, which still stands at that entrance.

By the 1970s, the pavilion was battered and broken. Its restoration, celebrated in 1973, was one of the first projects in which private donors teamed up with the city to repair the park, years before the Conservancy was born. So a former streetcar shelter helped point the way to the park's rescue.
=== DEEPER The Park's Ornamentalist | architecture
You've heard Jacob Wrey Mould's name again and again on this walk: the carvings of Bethesda Terrace, the Music Pavilion, the Sheepfold, the Ladies' Pavilion, and this bridge. Who was he?

Mould was born in England in 1825 and trained under Owen Jones, a designer famous for his studies of color and ornament, who spent years documenting the Alhambra, the great Moorish palace in Spain. Mould is said to have helped him with that work, and the Moorish love of pattern and bright color stayed with him all his life. You can see it in the tiles of the Bethesda arcade, and you once could see it in the gilded pavilion on the Mall.

He came to New York in the early 1850s to design a church, All Souls Unitarian, on Fourth Avenue at 20th Street. Its bold stripes of brick and pale stone startled the city, and New Yorkers nicknamed it the Church of the Holy Zebra. It was torn down long ago, but it made Mould's name. Soon he was working beside Vaux in the park, designing much of its ornament, from carved birds to iron railings.

Mould was a gifted and unusual character, a lover of music who got the park's first concerts going. In the 1870s, he spent several years working in Lima, Peru, before coming back to design for the city's parks. He died in 1886.

Olmsted and Vaux gave the park its shape, and Mould gave it its sparkle. Look for his touch in the curves of this railing.

=== DEEPER The Real Strawberry Field | names
This garden takes its name from a song, and the song takes its name from a real place in Liverpool.

As a boy, John Lennon lived with his Aunt Mimi in the Liverpool suburb of Woolton. Nearby stood a Victorian mansion called Strawberry Field, which had become a children's home run by the Salvation Army. The children's home closed in 2005, and its famous red gates were later replaced with copies. In 2019, the Salvation Army reopened the site with an exhibition about Lennon and the song.

So two gardens, an ocean apart, share a name: the one where a lonely boy played, and the one where the world comes to remember him.
=== DEEPER Lennon Versus the Government | politics
You heard that John Lennon lived across the street, at the Dakota. For a few years in the 1970s, the United States government tried to throw him out of the country.

Lennon and Yoko Ono moved to New York in 1971, and Lennon threw himself into radical politics. He sang at rallies, befriended antiwar activists, and talked of a concert tour that would register young voters and campaign against President Nixon in the 1972 election. The government moved against him. Lennon fought back, and the case dragged on for years. In October 1975, a federal appeals court ruled in his favor. The judge, Irving Kaufman, wrote that the courts would not condone selective deportation based upon secret political grounds. Two days later, on Lennon's thirty-fifth birthday, Yoko Ono gave birth to their son, Sean.

In July 1976, Lennon finally received his green card, which let him stay in the United States for good. He called New York home for the rest of his life. Years later, the FBI's files on him were released, showing just how closely he had been watched.
=== DEEPER The Househusband of the Dakota | culture
After Sean was born in 1975, John Lennon did something almost unheard of for a rock star at the height of his fame. He stopped.

For five years, Lennon released no new music. He stayed home at the Dakota and raised his son, while Yoko Ono ran the family's business affairs. He baked bread, and was proud of it, and later joked that after all that work, he felt he deserved a gold record. He called himself a househusband, an unusual word at the time, and he meant it. He spent his days with Sean, read, drew and traveled, and, by many accounts, walked in the park across the street.

In 1980, he and Ono recorded a new album, Double Fantasy, released that November, with songs about family and this quiet life. In one, Watching the Wheels, he sings about the people who told him he was crazy to give it all up, while he was happy just sitting there, watching the wheels go round and round. In another, Beautiful Boy, a lullaby for Sean, he sings a line that has become one of his most quoted: life is what happens to you while you're busy making other plans.

Three weeks after the album came out, he was killed outside his home.

Strawberry Fields is a memorial to a Beatle, but it's also a memorial to a father who chose, for a while, to step away from the world and stay home. Stand by the mosaic and imagine him crossing the street on an ordinary afternoon, with a little boy by the hand.
