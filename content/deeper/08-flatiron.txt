=== STOP Union Square
=== DEEPER The First Labor Day | history
You heard that the first Labor Day parade marched to Union Square on September fifth, 1882. Notice the day of the week: it was a Tuesday, an ordinary working day. The ten thousand or so workers who marched up Broadway from City Hall had to take the day off on their own, and many of them gave up a day's pay to do it.

The parade was organized by the Central Labor Union, an alliance of the city's unions. Who first thought of it is still argued about, and the argument has a comic twist: the two leading candidates had almost the same name. One was Matthew Maguire, a machinist who was the union's secretary. The other was Peter McGuire, a carpenter who helped found the national carpenters' union and later the American Federation of Labor. The federal Department of Labor lays out both claims, and notes that more recent research seems to favor the machinist.

The idea spread. Other cities held parades, states made the day a legal holiday, and in 1894 Congress made the first Monday in September a national one. Most of the rest of the world honors workers on the first of May instead, a date tied to the strikes for an eight-hour day, and the Haymarket affair, in Chicago in 1886.

Union Square kept its role. On March sixth, 1930, in the first winter of the Great Depression, tens of thousands of people filled the square for a Communist-led rally of the unemployed. When part of the crowd set off to march on City Hall without a permit, police on foot and on horseback charged into it with nightsticks. When you see a protest here today, you are watching one of the oldest traditions in the city.
=== DEEPER Tiffany's Blue Box | companies
You heard that Tiffany had its flagship on the west side of the square from 1870. Charles Lewis Tiffany had a gift for publicity as sharp as his eye for jewels.

In 1858, when the first telegraph cable was laid across the Atlantic, New York went wild with celebration. Tiffany bought up leftover cable, cut it into short lengths, bound the pieces with brass bands, and sold them as souvenirs, each with a certificate. The cable itself stopped working within weeks, but the souvenirs helped make Tiffany a household name. During the Civil War, the store supplied the Union Army, including handsome presentation swords for its officers.

In 1878, Tiffany bought one of the largest yellow diamonds ever found, from the new mines at Kimberley in South Africa. It weighed two hundred and eighty-seven carats in the rough, and was cut down to about a hundred and twenty-eight. The Tiffany Diamond has been the company's showpiece ever since, and Audrey Hepburn wore it in publicity photographs for Breakfast at Tiffany's.

The famous color goes back even further. Since 1845, Tiffany has published a catalog called the Blue Book, and its robin's egg blue, on covers, boxes and bags, became one of the most recognizable colors in commerce. A New York newspaper reported in 1906 that there was one thing Tiffany would not sell you at any price: an empty box. You could only get one with something inside.
=== DEEPER The Tammany Tiger | politics
You heard that Tammany Hall ended its days in the building on the east side of the square. It began in the 1780s as a social and patriotic club, the Society of Saint Tammany, named for Tamanend, a Lenape chief remembered for his wisdom and for making peace with William Penn. Its members borrowed Native American titles: their leaders were sachems, and their meeting hall was the Wigwam.

The tiger came later, and it came from an enemy. William Tweed got his start as foreman of a volunteer fire company, Americus Engine Company Number Six, known as Big Six, whose emblem was a snarling tiger. In 1871, the cartoonist Thomas Nast turned that tiger against him, drawing Tammany as a beast loose in a Roman arena, mauling the Republic, while Boss Tweed watched from the emperor's box. Tammany, unashamed, made the tiger its symbol.

No one explained the system better than George Washington Plunkitt, a Tammany district leader who held court at a shoeshine stand in the county courthouse. A reporter, William Riordon, wrote down his talks and published them in 1905. Plunkitt drew a line between dishonest graft, like shaking down gamblers and saloon keepers, and what he called honest graft: learning ahead of time where the city planned a new park or bridge, buying the land cheap, and selling it to the city at a profit. He summed up his career in a line that has never been forgotten: I seen my opportunities and I took 'em.

By the time Tammany moved here in 1929, its days were numbered. Fiorello La Guardia, mayor from 1934 to 1945, cut the machine off from city jobs, and in 1963 a young Greenwich Village lawyer named Ed Koch beat the last great Tammany boss, Carmine DeSapio, in a race for district leader.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Square's Statues | look
You heard about George Washington, on horseback at the south end. His sculptor, Henry Kirke Brown, also made the Abraham Lincoln at the north end, put up a few years after Lincoln's assassination. Lincoln didn't start there. He first stood at the southwest corner of the square, and was moved when the park was rebuilt.

His old corner now belongs to Mahatma Gandhi, striding along with his staff. The statue was dedicated on October second, 1986, Gandhi's birthday, and its sponsors chose Union Square for its long history of nonviolent protest.

Look for the Marquis de Lafayette, the young French nobleman who crossed the Atlantic to fight beside Washington, shown offering his heart and his sword to the American cause. He is the work of Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, who designed the Statue of Liberty. France gave him to New York to thank the city for its help to Paris during the siege of 1870 and 1871, and he was unveiled in 1876, in the very years Bartholdi was at work on Liberty.

Then find the tall flagpole rising from a massive bronze base. It was made for the hundred and fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, in 1926, and its base carries the Declaration's entire text. The Tammany Society paid for it, as a memorial to Charles Francis Murphy, the boss who had led the machine for more than twenty years.

Washington is the oldest of them all. He has watched nearly every rally, march and protest this square has seen.

=== DEEPER The Woman Who Ran Macy's | people
You heard that Macy's started out on Sixth Avenue at Fourteenth Street, at the southern edge of Ladies' Mile. You can hear about Rowland Macy himself, the Nantucket whaler, on the Midtown walk. This is the story of the person who ran his store.

Margaret Getchell was a distant cousin of Macy's, also from Nantucket. Around 1860, not yet twenty, she came to work at his new store in New York, keeping the books and handling the cash. She was quick with figures and even quicker at running things, and in 1866 Macy made her superintendent of the whole business, in charge of nearly two hundred employees and the store's daily operations. That freed him to sail to Europe on buying trips. Macy's has called her the first woman promoted to an executive position in American retailing.

She is credited with starting an early mail-order business, so customers could shop from home. In 1869 she married a Macy's colleague, Abiel LaForge, and the next year she stepped down to raise a family. She died in 1880, at only thirty-eight.

The Fourteenth Street store kept growing until it filled much of its block. Macy's says it was the first department store to decorate its windows for Christmas, in 1874, with porcelain dolls posed in scenes from Uncle Tom's Cabin. Macy died in 1877, and the store later passed to the Straus family, who carried the red star north to Herald Square in 1902.
=== DEEPER Cash Girls and the White List | politics
Behind the counters of Ladies' Mile was a world the shoppers rarely saw. Saleswomen stood on their feet for long days, often in hot, stuffy rooms. And running between the counters were the cash girls and cash boys, children as young as ten, who carried each customer's money to the cashier's desk and brought back the change, whenever a clerk called out, Cash!

In 1890, the reformer Jacob Riis wrote that cash girls in New York stores earned about a dollar seventy-five a week, for days that in the busy season stretched to sixteen hours, and that girls fainted on the job.

A group of New York women decided to use the one power they had over the stores: their own shopping. At the start of the 1890s, with the reformer Josephine Shaw Lowell as president, they founded the Consumers' League of New York, and drew up a White List, a published list of stores that paid fair wages, kept reasonable hours, and provided decent, sanitary working conditions. Shoppers were urged to buy only from stores on the list, and in time about forty stores made it.

It was an early version of an idea that is still with us: that where you shop is a political act. Wire carriers and pneumatic tubes eventually replaced the cash children, and state laws began to limit how long women and children could work in stores. In 1910, the league hired a young social worker named Frances Perkins to run its New York office. She went on to become secretary of labor under Franklin Roosevelt, the first woman in a president's cabinet.
=== DEEPER Birds on the Hats | nature
In February 1886, a young ornithologist named Frank Chapman went bird watching in the uptown shopping district, which in those days meant streets like these. He wasn't looking at the trees. He was looking at women's hats.

On two late afternoon walks, Chapman counted about seven hundred hats, and five hundred and forty-two of them were decorated with feathers, wings or whole stuffed birds. Among them he identified about forty native species, many of them common songbirds. He published his tally in the magazine Forest and Stream.

The milliners of Ladies' Mile were at the heart of the fashion. Plume hunters were wiping out colonies of egrets and herons in Florida, shooting the adults in breeding season, when their plumes were finest, and leaving the chicks to starve.

Chapman's count became ammunition for a new movement. In 1896, two Boston women, Harriet Hemenway and her cousin Minna Hall, held teas to persuade their friends to give up feathered hats, and founded the Massachusetts Audubon Society. Other states followed. In 1900 Congress passed the Lacey Act, which banned shipping illegally killed birds across state lines, and in 1903 Theodore Roosevelt, born just around the corner from here, created the first federal bird reservation, at Pelican Island in Florida.

Chapman spent more than half a century at the American Museum of Natural History, and in 1900 he proposed a new holiday tradition. Instead of competing to shoot as many birds as possible on Christmas Day, as hunters then did, people should go out and count them. The Christmas Bird Count still takes place every winter.

=== DEEPER The Boy Who Brought Home a Seal | nature
When Theodore was seven or eight, walking up Broadway, he passed a market and saw a dead seal laid out on a slab. He was spellbound. As long as the seal was there, he came back day after day to study it, and he measured it as best he could with a folding pocket ruler. He never got the seal, but he did get its skull, and with two of his cousins he made it the first specimen of what the boys grandly called the Roosevelt Museum of Natural History.

The museum grew, specimen by specimen, with whatever a determined boy could collect and preserve. For his fourteenth birthday, his father gave him lessons in taxidermy from John G. Bell, a taxidermist who had once traveled with John James Audubon. That same year, Theodore got his first pair of glasses, and discovered how much of the world a nearsighted boy had been missing. He later wrote that he had no idea how beautiful the world was until he got those spectacles.

That winter, on a family trip up the Nile, he spent the voyage shooting and preparing birds for his collection.

The boy naturalist became the president who protected more American land than any before him, in national forests, bird reservations and national monuments. Some of the birds he collected as a teenager survive to this day in museum collections.
=== DEEPER Roosevelt's Midnight Rambles | politics
In 1895, at thirty-six, Roosevelt became president of the city's board of police commissioners, in charge of a department that a state investigation had just exposed as deeply corrupt. He decided to see for himself what his patrolmen did at night.

With the journalist Jacob Riis as his guide, the commissioner walked the streets in the small hours, checking on the men on their beats. They found officers asleep, chatting in saloons, or simply gone, and some were summoned to headquarters the next morning to face the commissioner himself. The newspapers loved it, and cartoonists drew Roosevelt as little more than a pair of glasses and a mouthful of teeth.

Riis had his own reasons. As a penniless young immigrant, he had once spent a night in one of the city's police station lodging houses, filthy rooms where the homeless were allowed to sleep, and there, he later wrote, a policeman killed the little stray dog that had been his only companion. He showed Roosevelt the lodging houses, and soon afterward Roosevelt shut them down.

Not every crusade went so well. Roosevelt insisted on enforcing the state law that closed saloons on Sundays, the one day most working men had free, and it made him enemies across the city, especially among German New Yorkers who loved their Sunday beer gardens. He held the job for about two years before leaving for Washington as assistant secretary of the Navy.
=== DEEPER Teddy's Bear | legends
In November 1902, President Roosevelt went bear hunting in Mississippi, at the invitation of the state's governor. The other hunters found bears. He didn't. So his guide, Holt Collier, a renowned bear hunter who had been born into slavery, cornered a big black bear with his dogs, lassoed it, and tied it to a tree for the president to shoot. Roosevelt refused. It wasn't sporting. The cuddly version stops there. In most accounts, the bear had already been badly hurt, and Roosevelt had it put out of its misery.

A cartoonist at the Washington Post, Clifford Berryman, drew the scene on November sixteenth, under the title Drawing the Line in Mississippi: the president turning away from a frightened bear. In later cartoons the bear got smaller and cuter, and became a regular companion of the cartoon president.

In Brooklyn, a shopkeeper named Morris Michtom saw the cartoon. He and his wife, Rose, made stuffed toy bears and put them in their window with a sign that read Teddy's bears. The family always said he wrote to the White House and got the president's permission to use his name. The bears sold so well that the Michtoms went on to found the Ideal Novelty and Toy Company, one of the biggest toy makers in the country.

At almost the same moment, in Germany, Richard Steiff of the Steiff toy company designed a jointed stuffed bear of his own, and collectors still argue over who came first. One more irony: Roosevelt is said to have disliked the nickname Teddy. The bears made sure it stuck.

=== DEEPER A Booth Saves a Lincoln | people
You heard that Edwin Booth was the brother of Lincoln's assassin. There is a stranger story about him, and the witness was a Lincoln.

During the Civil War, the president's son Robert was traveling from New York to Washington. On the crowded platform at the Jersey City station, passengers pressed toward the sleeping car as the train began to move, and Robert was twisted off his feet and slipped into the gap between the platform and the car. A hand seized his coat collar and pulled him back to safety. He turned to thank his rescuer, and knew him at once. It was Edwin Booth, whose face was famous.

Robert told the story himself, many years later, in a letter published in The Century Magazine. Booth learned whose life he had saved from his friend Adam Badeau, an officer on General Grant's staff, who wrote to tell him.

There is one more strange scene. On November twenty-fifth, 1864, Edwin and his brothers Junius and John Wilkes appeared on stage together for the only time, in Julius Caesar at the Winter Garden Theatre on Broadway, a benefit to raise money for a statue of Shakespeare in Central Park, the one that stands on the Mall today. Edwin played Brutus, the noble assassin. John Wilkes played Mark Antony. That same night, Confederate agents tried to set New York on fire, starting blazes in a string of hotels, one of them right next door to the theater. When the fire bells rang, Edwin stepped to the footlights and calmed the audience, and the play went on.

Less than five months later, John Wilkes Booth shot Robert's father at Ford's Theatre.
=== DEEPER Tilden's Library | history
You heard that Samuel Tilden won the popular vote for president in 1876 and lost the White House anyway. He lost it by a single electoral vote. He spent his last years among his books, here and at Greystone, his estate in Yonkers, and when he died in 1886, unmarried and childless, he left the bulk of his fortune to found a free library and reading room for the people of New York.

His relatives had other plans. A nephew sued, other relatives joined in, and they attacked the clause of the will that created the library trust. The case dragged on for years, and the courts largely sided with the family. In the end, only part of the fortune, roughly two million dollars by most accounts, reached the library trust.

That wasn't enough to build a great library. But New York had two other libraries that were struggling: the Astor Library, in the building on Lafayette Street that is now the Public Theater, and the Lenox Library on Fifth Avenue. In 1895 the three joined forces as the New York Public Library, whose great marble building on Fifth Avenue you can visit on the Midtown walk.

So a disappointed candidate's last wish, half broken by his own family, helped create one of the great libraries of the world.
=== DEEPER The Four Million | culture
From 1903 to 1907, an apartment at 55 Irving Place, a short walk south of the park, was home to one of the most popular writers in America. He signed his stories O. Henry. His real name was William Sydney Porter, and he arrived in New York with a past: he had served three years in an Ohio prison for embezzling from a bank in Austin, Texas, where he had worked as a teller. It was in prison that he began publishing stories under his new name.

In New York he wrote at a furious pace, for a time a story a week for the Sunday edition of the New York World, about shopgirls, clerks, waiters, swindlers and lonely people in rented rooms.

In 1906 he collected some of them in a book called The Four Million. The title was a jab at Ward McAllister, the society arbiter who had declared that only about four hundred people in New York really mattered, the number, it was said, who could fit in Caroline Astor's ballroom. O. Henry's answer was that all four million New Yorkers were worth a story.

The book included The Gift of the Magi, the tale of a poor young couple, Jim and Della, who each sell their most precious possession to buy the other a Christmas present. She sells her long hair to buy a chain for his watch, and he sells his watch to buy combs for her hair. As you heard, Pete's Tavern claims he wrote it there, and it points to its second booth.

O. Henry drank too much and spent faster than he earned, and he died in 1910, at forty-seven.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Lamps, Knights and a Witch | look
Walk around the park and look at the houses. Several have stories on their doorsteps.

On the west side, numbers 3 and 4 share a lacy cast iron porch, usually credited to the architect Alexander Jackson Davis. In front of number 4, look for a pair of tall lamps. This was the home of James Harper, one of the brothers who founded the publishing house Harper and Brothers, and mayor of New York in the 1840s. By an old custom, said to go back to Dutch New Amsterdam, a pair of lamps marked the mayor's home. Harper's lamps are still here, though the custom faded after Gracie Mansion became the mayor's official residence in 1942.

On the east side, look at number 36, a white terra cotta apartment house in the Gothic style, finished around 1910 to a design by James Riely Gordon. Two knights in armor stand guard at its door.

Next door, number 34, finished in 1883, is often described as the first cooperative apartment building in New York, where residents owned shares in the building instead of renting. Its residents have included the actor James Cagney, and Margaret Hamilton, who played the Wicked Witch of the West in The Wizard of Oz.

Like the other buildings around the park, these come with the most coveted perk in the neighborhood: a key to the gate.
=== STOP The Flatiron Building
=== DEEPER The Cowcatcher | architecture
Walk to the north tip of the Flatiron and look at the bottom of the prow. The building's sharp edge doesn't come all the way down to the sidewalk. A low, one-story shop pokes out in front of it, like the cowcatcher on the front of a locomotive, and that is exactly what New Yorkers called it.

It wasn't part of the original design. Harry Black, the head of the Fuller Company, wanted every square foot of rentable space he could get on his expensive lot, and he insisted on a storefront at the tip. Burnham objected. Black won, and the space was leased to United Cigar Stores.

You heard that the Flatiron is a steel-frame skyscraper. On this lot, that frame was everything. The building rises twenty-two stories, about two hundred and eighty-five feet, and the steel skeleton carries the weight, so the stone and terra cotta walls are a covering rather than a support. A traditional masonry building this tall would have needed enormously thick walls at the bottom, and on a triangle that narrows to about six and a half feet, the tip would have been nearly solid. Steel let the prow stay hollow, and full of offices.

Those offices at the prow are the most famous rooms in the building, narrow wedges with windows on both sides, looking out over Madison Square and up two of the most famous avenues in the world. Once you notice the cowcatcher, though, you will never see the Flatiron quite the same way again.
=== DEEPER The Flatiron in Snow and Mist | culture
You heard that Alfred Stieglitz and Edward Steichen made famous photographs of the Flatiron. Here is how.

In the winter of 1903, on a day of heavy snow, Stieglitz set up his camera in Madison Square, across the street, and framed the new building behind the forked trunk of a snow-covered tree. Years later he recalled that the building had seemed to move toward him like the bow of a monster ocean steamer. He published the photograph in his magazine, Camera Work.

Steichen's version came a year or so later, at twilight, with a horse-drawn cab and the dark branches of a tree in front of the building. He printed the negative in layers of tinted gum over platinum, so each print came out a different color. Three of them, each in a different tone, are now at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, a gift from Stieglitz in 1933.

The two men led a movement called the Photo-Secession, which argued that a photograph could be a work of art, as personal as a painting. In 1905 they opened a small gallery a short walk up Fifth Avenue, at number 291, known simply as 291. Besides photographs, it gave many Americans their first look at modern art from Europe: drawings by Rodin, work by Matisse, and in 1911, Picasso. In 1916, Stieglitz showed the drawings of a young artist named Georgia O'Keeffe, whom he later married.
=== DEEPER Toyland on Fifth Avenue | companies
From the Flatiron's tip, look north across Twenty-third Street at the big building on the west side of Fifth Avenue, number 200. For most of the twentieth century, this was the capital of the American toy business.

The site had been the Fifth Avenue Hotel, until it was torn down in 1908. The office building that replaced it filled with toy companies, and it became the heart of the annual Toy Fair, which had begun in 1903 with fewer than ten companies showing their wares, Lionel electric trains among them. Every winter, buyers from stores all over the country came to see what children would be asking for the following Christmas, in showrooms that spread into a second building on Broadway, joined to this one in 1967 by a bridge high above the street.

Some of the most famous toys in the world made their debuts at Toy Fair. In 1959, a California toy company introduced a teenage fashion doll there. Her name was Barbie.

In the 2000s the toy companies moved out, the building was sold and remade, and the fair moved to the Javits Center on the far West Side. Today the old Toy Center holds Eataly, the Italian food hall, and the corporate offices of Tiffany and Company. When you pass its doors, picture the buyers who once crowded in each winter to see what American children would want next.
=== STOP Madison Square Park & the Met Life Tower
=== DEEPER The Arm in the Park | history
You heard that the Statue of Liberty's arm and torch stood in this park. They had already been on tour. Bartholdi's workshop in Paris finished the arm first, and shipped it to Philadelphia for the Centennial Exhibition of 1876, the great fair for a hundred years of American independence. It arrived months after the fair opened, but visitors still paid to climb up inside it to the balcony around the torch. The arm alone is about forty-two feet long, and the index finger is eight feet.

After the fair, the arm came here, to the northwest corner of Madison Square, and stayed until 1882, to drum up money for the pedestal. France was paying for the statue, but Americans were expected to pay for its base, and they were in no hurry. In 1882 the arm went back to France, to be joined to the rest of the statue. It took Joseph Pulitzer's newspaper campaign of 1885 to pay for the pedestal, and the statue was dedicated in October 1886.

The torch had a hard life after that. In 1916, German saboteurs blew up a munitions depot on Black Tom Island, across the harbor in New Jersey, and flying debris damaged the arm. The torch has been closed to visitors ever since. That same year, the sculptor Gutzon Borglum, who later carved Mount Rushmore, cut windows into the torch's copper flame and fitted them with amber glass, so it could glow from within. The windows leaked every time it rained, and for decades the water damaged the arm.

During the restoration for the statue's hundredth birthday, the old torch came down, and in 1986 a new one went up, its flame covered in gold leaf. The original, the one that once rose out of this park, is now on display in the Statue of Liberty Museum on Liberty Island.
=== DEEPER The Trial of the Century | people
You heard that Harry Thaw shot Stanford White on the roof of Madison Square Garden in June 1906. What followed filled the newspapers for years.

At the center of it was Evelyn Nesbit. She had come to New York as a teenage model and chorus girl, and White, more than thirty years older, pursued her when she was sixteen. In his apartment on Twenty-fourth Street there was a red velvet swing, and the girl on the red velvet swing became the most famous phrase of the scandal.

Thaw, the unstable heir to a Pittsburgh railroad fortune, married Nesbit in 1905, and his jealousy of White became an obsession. At his first trial, in 1907, Nesbit testified that White had drugged and assaulted her when she was sixteen, and the whole country hung on her words. The jury deadlocked. At the second trial, in 1908, Thaw's lawyers argued that he was insane, and the jury agreed: not guilty by reason of insanity. He was sent to the state hospital for the criminally insane at Matteawan.

His family's money kept working for him. In 1913 he escaped from Matteawan and fled to Canada, and was brought back. In 1915 a jury declared him sane, and he walked free.

Nesbit spent the rest of her long life trying to escape one night in 1906. She danced in vaudeville, acted in silent films, and struggled with poverty and addiction. In 1955 she served as an adviser on a Hollywood film about her own story, The Girl in the Red Velvet Swing, with Joan Collins playing her. She died in 1967.
=== DEEPER The Light That Never Fails | companies
You heard that Met Life sold small policies to working-class families. It was called industrial insurance. For a few cents a week, collected door to door by an agent, a family could make sure there would be money for a funeral, and a little to live on, if the breadwinner died. Millions of families signed up, and their nickels and dimes paid for the tower on the east side of the park. For decades, the company used the tower as its symbol.

Its most remarkable idea came in 1909. At the suggestion of Lillian Wald, the founder of the Henry Street Settlement on the Lower East Side, Met Life began sending visiting nurses into the homes of sick policyholders, free of charge. It began with Henry Street nurses in Manhattan and grew into a service across the country that lasted more than forty years. The company argued that it was good business, too: healthier policyholders lived longer and kept paying their premiums.

Now look across Twenty-fourth Street, at the massive limestone building just north of the tower. It was meant to be the base of a skyscraper of up to a hundred stories. The Great Depression stopped it at about thirty. What you see is the stump of a tower that never was, and on its ground floor is the restaurant Eleven Madison Park.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Madison Square's Bronze Men | look
Madison Square Park is full of statues, and three of them repay a closer look.

At the north end, find Admiral David Farragut, standing as if on the deck of his ship. Farragut was the Union admiral who, in 1864, led his fleet through the mines, then called torpedoes, at the mouth of Mobile Bay, with an order remembered as: damn the torpedoes, full speed ahead. The statue, unveiled in 1881, was the first major work of the young sculptor Augustus Saint-Gaudens, and its curving stone base, with figures and lettering that seem to flow like water, was a collaboration with his friend Stanford White, then a young architect. It made Saint-Gaudens famous, and began a partnership with White that lasted until White's death across the street.

At the southwest corner sits William Seward, Lincoln's secretary of state and the man who later bought Alaska. For generations, New Yorkers have told a story that the sculptor saved money by putting Seward's head on a body made for a statue of Lincoln. It is a good story, but no one has found evidence that it is true.

And at the southeast corner stands Roscoe Conkling, one of the most powerful senators of the Gilded Age. In March 1888, during the great blizzard that buried New York, Conkling set out on foot from his law office on Wall Street, heading uptown. The walk took about three hours, through deep drifts and a howling wind, and by most accounts he nearly collapsed in the snow around Union Square before he reached shelter. He died a few weeks later. His friends wanted his statue in Union Square, near where he fell. The park commissioners said no, and he ended up here.
=== STOP Tin Pan Alley
=== DEEPER Irving Berlin's Black Keys | people
You heard that Irving Berlin came out of the Tin Pan Alley world. He never learned to read music. Israel Beilin was born in the Russian Empire in 1888 and came to New York with his family when he was five. His father, a cantor, died when Israel was thirteen, and before long the boy left home to make his own way, singing for pennies on street corners and in saloons. He found work as a singing waiter at the Pelham Café on Pell Street in Chinatown, making up parodies and new songs for the customers.

In 1907 he wrote the words for his first published song, Marie from Sunny Italy. As the story is usually told, the printer got his name wrong, and the sheet music credited I. Berlin. He kept it.

Berlin taught himself the piano, but he played almost entirely in one key, F sharp, which lies mostly on the black keys. Once he was successful, he had special pianos built with a lever that shifted the whole keyboard, so he could keep playing on the black keys while the music came out in whatever key he chose. Musical secretaries wrote down what he played and sang.

In 1911, Alexander's Ragtime Band made him a star. He went on to write around fifteen hundred songs, among them Blue Skies, Puttin' On the Ritz, Cheek to Cheek, Easter Parade and There's No Business Like Show Business. The composer Jerome Kern said that Irving Berlin had no place in American music. He was American music. Berlin lived to be a hundred and one.
=== DEEPER Who Named the Alley? | legends
You heard the legend that a journalist gave Tin Pan Alley its name. The usual suspect is Monroe Rosenfeld, a songwriter and newspaperman who knew this block well. As the story goes, around the turn of the twentieth century, Rosenfeld visited the office of the composer and publisher Harry Von Tilzer, who liked to weave strips of paper through his piano strings to give them a tinny, banjo-like sound. Rosenfeld said it sounded like a tin pan, and when he wrote about the street, the name stuck.

That's the legend. The article in which Rosenfeld supposedly coined the phrase has never been found, and others say tin pan was simply slang for a battered, tinny piano, a name that may have been around before anyone wrote it down.

Harry Von Tilzer deserves a story of his own. He was born Aaron Gumbinsky, and as a teenager he took his mother's maiden name, Tilzer, and added a Von to make it sound grander. He wrote hundreds of songs, including A Bird in a Gilded Cage and Wait Till the Sun Shines, Nellie. His brother Albert wrote the music for Take Me Out to the Ball Game, though by most accounts neither Albert nor the lyricist, Jack Norworth, had ever been to a baseball game when they wrote it.

Whatever its origin, the name outlived the block. By the 1920s, Tin Pan Alley meant the whole American popular song business, wherever it happened to be.
=== DEEPER The Song Pluggers | companies
You heard that publishers hired song pluggers to demonstrate their songs. The job went far beyond the office piano. Pluggers sang at the sheet music counters of department stores and five-and-dime stores, and wherever a crowd gathered. The young George Gershwin left high school at fifteen to plug songs for the publisher Jerome Remick, for fifteen dollars a week, pounding a piano for hours a day. Publishers also paid popular singers, in cash, gifts or a share of the royalties, to put their songs into their acts, an early form of what radio would later call payola.

The song that showed how much money there was in the business was After the Ball, a sentimental waltz by Charles K. Harris of Milwaukee. Harris published it himself, a popular singer put it into a hit musical comedy, and it went on to sell millions of copies, an unheard-of number at the time. After that, everyone wanted a piece of the trade, and publishers crowded onto this street, close to the theaters and the vaudeville booking offices.

For years, though, songwriters were paid only for printed music. When a restaurant orchestra played their song, they got nothing. In 1914, the composer Victor Herbert joined other songwriters and publishers to found ASCAP, the American Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers, to collect fees for public performance. Herbert sued Shanley's, a Times Square restaurant whose musicians had performed his song Sweethearts. The restaurant argued that diners paid for the food, not the music. In 1917, the Supreme Court sided with Herbert. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that if music did not pay, it would be given up. The fees that restaurants, stores and radio stations pay for the music they play trace back to that decision.
