=== STOP The Brooklyn Heights Promenade
=== DEEPER A Highway on the Promenade? | politics
You heard that the triple cantilever under your feet is wearing out, and that its future has been fiercely debated. Here is how heated that debate got.

In 2018, the city's Department of Transportation presented its options for rebuilding the structure. The slower one would have repaired it lane by lane over many years, snarling traffic for most of a decade. The faster one shocked the neighborhood. It would have closed the Promenade for about six years and run a temporary six-lane highway right here, on top of the structure, at the level where you're standing, beside the back gardens of some of the most historic houses in New York.

The Heights reacted the way it had in the 1940s. Residents packed meetings, hired their own engineers, and proposed alternatives. The mayor appointed a panel of experts to study the problem. In 2020, that panel delivered a warning. Too many heavy trucks were pounding the old structure, and without action, parts of it could become unsafe for traffic within a few years. It recommended taking away a lane in each direction, to lighten the load, while a permanent fix was worked out.

In 2021, the city did just that, cutting the highway beneath you from three lanes in each direction to two. Later, it began using sensors in the roadway to catch and fine overweight trucks. Plans for a long-term rebuild have been argued over ever since.

So the next time you stroll here, remember that this calm walkway was very nearly a highway itself, and that the argument over what lies beneath it isn't finished.
=== DEEPER Saving the Heights, House by House | history
You heard that in 1965, Brooklyn Heights became the first neighborhood protected as a historic district under the city's new landmarks law. That didn't happen by accident. The neighborhood campaigned for it for years.

After the fight over the expressway, residents had reason to worry. In the 1950s, urban renewal cleared blocks at the north end of the Heights, and developers eyed the old houses as sites for apartment towers. At the same time, a new generation was moving in, many of them young couples buying run-down rooming houses and restoring them, stripping old paint off woodwork and putting back the stoops and ironwork.

In the late 1950s, a group of these residents began pressing the city for legal protection. One of their leaders was a young lawyer named Otis Pratt Pearsall, who lived in the Heights. They surveyed the neighborhood house by house, documenting its architecture, and they lobbied city officials for a law that would let a whole district, not just a single building, be protected.

The law finally came in 1965, after the demolition of the old Pennsylvania Station shocked the city, as you may have heard on the East Village tour. Months later, the Heights became its first historic district. Since then, no house here can be torn down or altered on the outside without approval.

Look along the streets behind the Promenade. Almost everything you see from the nineteenth century survives because neighbors decided, sixty years ago, that it was worth saving, and then did the slow, unglamorous work to save it.
=== DEEPER The Novelist in the Crow's Nest | people
Look up at the top floors of the houses along Columbia Heights, just behind the Promenade. In one of them, at 142 Columbia Heights, Norman Mailer lived for more than four decades, from the early 1960s until his death in 2007.

You heard that Mailer wrote much of The Naked and the Dead in the Heights. He came back as a famous and famously combative writer, and he made his top-floor apartment into something like a ship. Reportedly, it had ropes, ladders, and a little crow's nest high up under the roof, which visitors and Mailer's children could climb to, with a sweeping view of the harbor.

From here, he wrote some of his most celebrated books, including The Armies of the Night, about a march on the Pentagon to protest the Vietnam War, and The Executioner's Song, about a murderer in Utah. Each won a Pulitzer Prize.

In 1969, Mailer ran for mayor of New York, with the newspaper columnist Jimmy Breslin as his running mate for City Council president. Their central idea was that New York City should break away from New York State and become the fifty first state. They also proposed Sweet Sunday, one day a month when cars would be banned from the city. Their slogan was Vote the Rascals In. They finished far back in the Democratic primary, but nobody forgot the campaign.

Mailer spent his last decades between this apartment and Provincetown, on Cape Cod. Picture him up in his crow's nest, looking out across the water at the city he once tried to run.
=== STOP Columbia Heights & Willow Street: Writers' Row
=== DEEPER February House | culture
You heard that W. H. Auden, Carson McCullers, Gypsy Rose Lee and Benjamin Britten shared a house on Middagh Street. It may have been the most remarkable household in American literary history.

In 1940, George Davis, an editor at the fashion magazine Harper's Bazaar, rented an old house at 7 Middagh Street and invited his friends to move in. Auden came, and so did McCullers, a young Southern novelist who had just published The Heart Is a Lonely Hunter. Britten arrived with his partner, the tenor Peter Pears, and the composer and writer Paul Bowles came with his wife, the writer Jane Bowles. Gypsy Rose Lee, the most famous striptease artist in America, moved in because Davis was helping her write a mystery novel. She finished it there. It was called The G-String Murders.

The house was chaotic, and reportedly, Auden tried to run it like a strict schoolmaster, collecting the rent and laying down rules about meals and noise. The writer Anaïs Nin is said to have called it February House, because so many of its residents had birthdays in February.

The work done there was real. Auden and Britten worked on an operetta together, Paul Bunyan. McCullers conceived two of her finest books, The Member of the Wedding and The Ballad of the Sad Café, during her time in Brooklyn.

Don't look for the house. It was demolished in the 1940s to make way for the approaches to the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway. Only the stories survive, and a book about the house, called February House, which brought them back to life in 2005.
=== DEEPER Printed on Cranberry Street | history
A few blocks north of here, where Cranberry Street meets Cadman Plaza West, which used to be part of Fulton Street, stood a small print shop run by two brothers, Andrew and James Rome. In the summer of 1855, they printed a thin book of poems for a Brooklyn journalist and carpenter named Walt Whitman.

You may have heard about Whitman at Fulton Ferry Landing. This was his first great book, Leaves of Grass. Whitman paid for it himself, and he's said to have set some of the type with his own hands. The first edition ran to fewer than a thousand copies.

It was a strange-looking book. The author's name didn't appear on the title page. Instead, facing it, was an engraving of Whitman himself, in workman's clothes, his hat cocked, his shirt open at the neck, one hand on his hip. You had to read into the poems to find his name, where he announces himself as Walt Whitman, an American, one of the roughs.

Most reviewers ignored it or were appalled. But one reader recognized it at once. Ralph Waldo Emerson, the most respected writer in America, sent Whitman a letter that began, I greet you at the beginning of a great career. Whitman, never shy, printed that line in gold on the spine of the next edition, without asking Emerson's permission.

The print shop is long gone. But the book born a few blocks from here changed American poetry, and Whitman kept revising and expanding it for the rest of his life.
=== DEEPER The Basement Tenant | people
You heard that Truman Capote rented a basement apartment at 70 Willow Street. The house belonged to his friend Oliver Smith, a stage designer who created the sets for Broadway hits including West Side Story and My Fair Lady.

Capote's apartment was on the lower floor, but reportedly, when Smith was away, he liked to show visitors through the whole yellow house as if it were his own. He loved the neighborhood, and in 1959, he described it in his essay A House on the Heights, which opens with one of the most quoted lines ever written about the borough: I live in Brooklyn. By choice.

That same year, Capote read a short newspaper item about the murder of a farm family in Holcomb, Kansas. He traveled there with his childhood friend Harper Lee, whose novel To Kill a Mockingbird was about to be published, and he spent the next six years on the story, much of that time writing in this basement. The result, In Cold Blood, published in 1966, was a true crime story told with the techniques of a novel. Capote called it a nonfiction novel, and it made him one of the most famous writers in the world.

Success pulled him away from Brooklyn, into Manhattan society, and he never completed another book on that scale. But for about a decade, one of the most influential books of the century was taking shape below this sidewalk, in a basement apartment in a friend's yellow house.
=== STOP Plymouth Church
=== DEEPER Pinky's Ring | people
You heard that in 1860, Henry Ward Beecher brought a young enslaved girl called Pinky before the congregation, and that the collection bought her freedom. Her name was Sally Maria Diggs, and she was about nine years old.

As the church tells the story, the collection plates came back overflowing, and among the bills and coins was a gold ring, dropped in by a woman in the congregation. Beecher took the ring, slipped it onto the girl's finger, and told her to remember that with that ring, she was wedded to freedom.

The girl grew up free, as Rose Ward, and later married and became Rose Ward Hunt. For decades, she lived quietly, far from the spotlight of that Sunday.

Then, in 1927, Plymouth Church got a new pastor, Stanley Durkee, who had just come from serving as president of Howard University, in Washington. One of his first acts was to invite Rose Ward Hunt back to Brooklyn. In May 1927, sixty seven years after she had stood beside Beecher as a frightened child, she returned to Plymouth Church as an honored guest. Her visit became a national sensation, reported in newspapers and magazines across the country. As the church tells it, she brought the ring with her and gave it back to the congregation that had given it to her.

Beecher's mock auctions were theater, and some people at the time found them troubling. But for one child, the theater meant freedom, and she came back, a lifetime later, to say so.
=== DEEPER The Woman Who Lit the Fuse | politics
You heard that Beecher's career was tarnished by the Tilton scandal. The person who made it public was one of the most extraordinary women of the century.

Victoria Woodhull had already shocked the country. With her sister, Tennessee Claflin, she had opened a stock brokerage on Wall Street, backed by Cornelius Vanderbilt. They published their own newspaper. And in 1872, Woodhull became the first woman to run for president of the United States.

Woodhull was an outspoken advocate of free love, and Beecher had criticized ideas like hers. She had heard, reportedly from fellow activists, that the great preacher was having an affair with Elizabeth Tilton, the wife of his friend. In November 1872, she printed the whole story in her newspaper. Her point, she said, wasn't to condemn the affair. It was to expose Beecher's hypocrisy.

The response came fast. Anthony Comstock, the crusader against vice, had Woodhull and her sister arrested for sending obscene material through the mail, and they spent weeks in jail before they were eventually acquitted. But the story was out. Plymouth Church held its own inquiry and cleared its pastor. Theodore Tilton sued. The trial in 1875 lasted about six months, and when the jury deadlocked, most of the jurors had sided with Beecher.

Years later, Elizabeth Tilton publicly confessed that the affair had happened. By then, Beecher was still in his pulpit here, and he stayed there until his death in 1887.
=== DEEPER Mark Twain and the Pilgrims | culture
In 1867, members of this congregation organized one of the first great pleasure cruises in American history. A steamship called the Quaker City would carry passengers on a five-month voyage to Europe and the Holy Land. The advertisements promised that Henry Ward Beecher himself would sail along. In the end, he didn't go.

A young Western humorist named Mark Twain did, as a traveling correspondent for a San Francisco newspaper. Most of his fellow passengers were pious, prosperous and middle-aged, and Twain found them hilarious. In his dispatches, he divided the ship into the pilgrims and the sinners, and made it clear which group he belonged to.

He turned those dispatches into a book, The Innocents Abroad, published in 1869. It poked fun at tourists who gushed over every old master painting and holy relic, and at the Old World too. It became the best-selling of all Twain's books during his lifetime, and it made him famous.

The voyage changed his private life as well. On board, he became friends with a young man named Charles Langdon, who showed him a small portrait of his sister, Olivia. Twain later said he fell in love with the picture. He met her soon after the trip, and they married in 1870.

So when you look at this plain brick church, remember that it helped launch, by accident, the career of America's greatest humorist, along with his marriage.
=== STOP Montague Street & Jackie Robinson
=== DEEPER The Long Audition | people
You heard that Branch Rickey met Jackie Robinson at 215 Montague Street in 1945. Getting him there took careful planning, and even some deception.

Rickey had decided to integrate the Dodgers, but he knew that word would leak if his scouts were seen watching Black players. So he announced that he was starting a new team in a Black league, to be called the Brooklyn Brown Dodgers, and sent his scouts out under that cover. One of them, Clyde Sukeforth, found Robinson in Chicago, playing for the Kansas City Monarchs, and brought him to Brooklyn for the meeting.

After he signed, Robinson spent 1946 with the Dodgers' top minor league team, the Montreal Royals. It was a brutal season on the road, but Montreal embraced him. He led the International League in batting, and the Royals won the Little World Series. After the final game, a crowd of joyful fans chased him through the streets to celebrate.

Then came 1947 and Ebbets Field. Robinson won the first Rookie of the Year award, which today is named in his honor. In 1949, he was the National League's Most Valuable Player, and in 1955, he helped the Dodgers win their only World Series in Brooklyn. In 1962, he was elected to the Hall of Fame.

In 1997, fifty years after his first game, every team in the major leagues retired his number, forty two. Today, every April 15th, every major league player wears forty two for a day. It all began with a three-hour conversation in an office on this street.
=== DEEPER Lady Mary's Street | names
Montague Street wasn't always called that. Its first name was Constable Street, for the family of Hezekiah Pierrepont's wife. Then it was renamed for a far more famous name in the family tree: Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, who was born a Pierrepont, in England.

Lady Mary was one of the most remarkable women of the eighteenth century. In 1715, she survived smallpox, which left her face scarred, and the disease had already killed her brother. The next year, she traveled with her husband, the British ambassador, to Constantinople, the capital of the Ottoman Empire.

There she saw something astonishing. Local women practiced inoculation. They scratched a little material from a mild smallpox case into a healthy person's skin, giving them a light case that protected them for life. Lady Mary had her young son inoculated there. Back in London in 1721, during an epidemic, she had her daughter inoculated too, in front of the king's doctors, and she campaigned to spread the practice in England, against fierce opposition from physicians and clergy.

Inoculation was risky, and it was later replaced by Edward Jenner's much safer vaccine, at the end of the century. But Lady Mary helped open the way, and her letters from Turkey, published after her death, are still read today.

So this busy shopping street, with its bank buildings and cafés, carries the name of a pioneer of public health. Notice, by the way, that the street added a final e that she never used.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Doors from an Ocean Liner | look
Walk one block south to the corner of Remsen and Henry Streets, and look at the stone church with the round-arched doorways. It was built in the 1840s as the Church of the Pilgrims, designed by Richard Upjohn, the architect of Trinity Church in Manhattan. It was one of the first churches in America built in the round-arched Romanesque style. Reportedly, a fragment of Plymouth Rock was set into its walls, to honor the Pilgrims.

Since the 1940s, it has been home to Our Lady of Lebanon, now a Maronite Catholic cathedral, serving the Lebanese community whose shops you'll see on Atlantic Avenue.

Now look closely at its bronze doors. They came from the Normandie, the great French ocean liner of the 1930s, one of the most luxurious ships ever built. In February 1942, while the Normandie was being converted into a troopship at a pier on the West Side of Manhattan, a fire broke out. So much water was poured onto her that she rolled over and sank at the pier. The wreck was eventually scrapped, and some of its fittings were sold.

The cathedral acquired a set of doors from the ship's grand dining room. Look for the medallions on them, which reportedly show towns and scenes of Normandy, in northern France.

So a door that once opened onto one of the most glamorous rooms on the Atlantic now opens onto a cathedral in Brooklyn Heights.
=== STOP Borough Hall
=== DEEPER The Sandhog Who Flew | underfoot
Beneath the plaza in front of you is Borough Hall station, the end of the line for the first subway to reach Brooklyn. It opened on January 9th, 1908, through the Joralemon Street Tunnel, the first subway tunnel under the East River. The 4 and 5 trains still use it.

Building it was dangerous. Sandhogs worked in compressed air, pushing a steel shield through the mud under the river, with air pressure holding back the water above them. If the riverbed above was thin, the air could burst through it, in what was called a blowout.

On a day in late March 1905, that's exactly what happened. A worker named Richard Creedon was standing near the leak when the riverbed gave way. According to the accounts of the time, the rushing air sucked him into the hole and shot him up through many feet of mud and then through the river itself, until he burst out of the water and into the air, on top of a geyser. He was fished out alive. By one account, his body had briefly plugged the hole, giving his fellow workers time to escape.

Creedon became famous as the sandhog who was blown through the East River and lived. Newspapers loved the story, and it has been retold ever since.

So as you hear the trains rumble below Joralemon Street, remember the men who dug the tunnel, and the one who left it by the shortest possible route.
=== DEEPER A City Hall Built Twice | architecture
You heard that this building was completed in 1848 as the City Hall of the independent City of Brooklyn. It was nearly not built at all.

In 1835, soon after Brooklyn became a city, it held a competition for the design of a grand new city hall. The winner was an architect named Calvin Pollard. Workers dug the foundations, and in 1836, the cornerstone was laid. Then came the Panic of 1837, a financial collapse that stopped construction across the country. Brooklyn ran out of money, and for years, the foundations sat empty.

When the city was ready to try again, in the mid-1840s, it turned to Gamaliel King, who had finished second in the original competition, and who reportedly started out as a grocer before becoming a builder and architect. King produced a simpler, more affordable Greek Revival design. He used the white marble that had already been cut for the first plan, along with stone from the old foundations. The marble came from Tuckahoe, in Westchester County, quarries that supplied marble for many New York buildings of the time.

The land itself was a gift from the Pierrepont and Remsen families, two of the great landowning families of the Heights, whose names are on the streets nearby.

Look at the building's columns and portico, and the cupola on top, which you heard was rebuilt after a fire. For fifty years, this was the seat of government of one of the largest cities in America, and when Brooklyn joined Greater New York, as you may have heard at the Municipal Building on the Civic Center tour, it became the borough's hall instead.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The House Where No One Lives | look
If you walk west along Joralemon Street, toward the river, through some of the prettiest blocks in the Heights, keep an eye out for number 58, a modest Greek Revival townhouse from the 1840s.

It looks like its neighbors, with brick walls, a stoop and a front door. But nobody lives there. When the subway tunnel was being built, the transit company took over the house and converted it into a ventilation building and an emergency exit for the tunnel below. Look at the windows, and you'll see that they're dark. Behind the facade is a shaft leading down to the tracks, with fans to move air through the tunnel, and a way out for passengers if a train were ever stranded under the river.

The house was kept looking like a house so that it would blend into the street, and it has done so for more than a century. Most people walk right past it.

It's not the only disguised building in New York. The city hides all sorts of infrastructure behind ordinary facades, from substations to ventilation shafts. But this may be the most convincing one, a working part of the subway dressed up as a Brooklyn Heights townhouse.

Stand quietly in front of it, and if you're lucky, you may hear the faint rumble of a train passing deep below, or feel a breath of air from the tunnel.
=== STOP Atlantic Avenue & the Secret Tunnel
=== DEEPER A Railroad to Boston | companies
You heard that the Long Island Rail Road built the tunnel under your feet in 1844. Here's what the railroad was for. It wasn't meant to serve Long Island at all.

In the 1830s, there was no railroad along the Connecticut shore, because the many rivers along the way were too hard to bridge. So the Long Island Rail Road was planned as a shortcut to Boston. Passengers would take a ferry from Manhattan to the foot of Atlantic Avenue, ride a train through the tunnel and straight out across Long Island, all the way to Greenport, near its eastern tip, then board a steamboat across Long Island Sound to Stonington, Connecticut, and catch another train to Boston.

The line to Greenport opened in 1844, and for a few years, it worked. But in 1848, railroads finally bridged their way along the Connecticut shore, and travelers could ride all the way from New York to Boston by land. The Long Island route lost its reason to exist, and the railroad soon fell into financial trouble.

It survived by turning to Long Island itself, carrying farmers' produce, commuters and, later, beachgoers. The tunnel under Atlantic Avenue had a shorter life. Brooklyn's residents complained about steam locomotives in their streets, a state law banned them within the city, and in 1861, the tunnel was closed and sealed.

The railroad never left Atlantic Avenue entirely. Its trains still run beneath the avenue further east, to the Atlantic Terminal, next to the Barclays Center, which you can visit on the Fort Greene tour.
=== DEEPER What's on the Shelves | food
You heard about Sahadi's and the Middle Eastern shops of Atlantic Avenue. Step inside one of them, and here's what to look for.

Start with the spices. Look for za'atar, a green-gray blend of wild thyme, sumac and sesame seeds. Mixed with olive oil and spread on flatbread, it makes one of the great breakfasts of the Levant. Sumac on its own is a dark red powder, tart and lemony, sprinkled over salads and grilled meat. Aleppo pepper is a mild, fruity red pepper flake named for the city in Syria.

Then look for the bins and barrels. There are olives in every shade, dried apricots and figs, pistachios and almonds, and roasted chickpeas. Look for pomegranate molasses, a thick, sweet and sour syrup used in stews and dressings, and for tahini, the sesame paste that goes into hummus.

The sweets are the best part. Baklava is layered with paper-thin dough, nuts and syrup. Ma'amoul are small shortbread cookies filled with dates, pistachios or walnuts, pressed into shape in carved wooden molds, and traditionally baked for Easter and other holidays. Halvah is a dense, crumbly sweet made of sesame paste and sugar.

Many of these foods came to New York with the immigrants of Little Syria, in lower Manhattan, as you may have heard on the Financial District tour, and followed them here when they moved across the river. So a bag of za'atar from Atlantic Avenue is a taste of more than a century of New York history, as well as a very good breakfast.
=== DEEPER South Ferry, Cobble Hill and Carroll Gardens | names
The names around you have stories of their own.

Start with the avenue. At its foot, by the river, a ferry began running in 1836 to Whitehall Street in Manhattan. Because it landed south of the older Fulton Ferry, it was called the South Ferry. The ferry is long gone, but its name survives on the Manhattan side, where the subway station and the ferry terminal at the tip of the island are still called South Ferry.

Now the neighborhoods. The blocks just south of Atlantic Avenue, west of Court Street, are Cobble Hill. The name comes from a steep, cone-shaped hill that once stood near the corner of Atlantic Avenue and Court Street, close to where you are. In 1776, American troops fortified it, and the earthwork was known as Cobble Hill Fort. The hill was later leveled, and the name was revived in the twentieth century for the neighborhood.

Further south is Carroll Gardens, named for Carroll Street, which honors Charles Carroll of Carrollton, a Maryland signer of the Declaration of Independence. The name is often said to honor the Maryland soldiers, too, who fought heroically nearby in the Battle of Brooklyn, as you can hear on the Prospect Park tour. The Gardens part comes from the unusually deep front yards on many of its blocks, laid out in the 1840s.

East of here is Boerum Hill, named for the Boerum family, who farmed this area in colonial times. Like several neighborhood names in this part of Brooklyn, it was adopted in the 1960s, as new residents restoring old row houses looked for a name with history.
