=== STOP The Brooklyn Navy Yard
=== DEEPER The Week of Fire | history
On the morning of December 19th, 1960, the aircraft carrier Constellation was in the last stages of construction at a pier here, with crews of workers swarming through its decks.

On the hangar deck, a forklift accidentally pushed its load into a steel plate and knocked it over. Within minutes, a fire had broken out, and it spread with terrifying speed through the ship. Thick smoke poured through the passageways and compartments below decks, where many workers were trapped. Firefighters and yard workers fought the blaze for hours.

About fifty workers were killed, and hundreds more were injured. It was the worst disaster in the history of the yard.

It came just three days after another catastrophe. On December 16th, two airliners had collided in the sky over the city, and one of them fell onto the streets of Park Slope, as you can hear on the Prospect Park tour. For Brooklyn, it was a terrible week.

The Constellation was repaired, at a cost of tens of millions of dollars, and it went on to serve the Navy for more than forty years. But the yard itself was running out of time. Less than four years after the fire, the Defense Department announced that the Brooklyn Navy Yard would close.

When you walk among the old buildings and cranes here today, remember the workers who built the Navy's ships, and especially the ones who didn't come home that December.
=== DEEPER On the Town | culture
In April 1944, two young New Yorkers, the composer Leonard Bernstein, whom you may have heard about on the Upper West Side tour, and the choreographer Jerome Robbins, premiered a ballet called Fancy Free, about three sailors on shore leave in the city. It was such a hit that by the end of the year, they had turned it into a Broadway musical, On the Town, with words by Betty Comden and Adolph Green. The sailors have just twenty four hours to see New York and fall in love.

In 1949, MGM made it into a movie, starring Gene Kelly and Frank Sinatra. It was one of the first Hollywood musicals to film on location in New York, and it opens right here, at the Brooklyn Navy Yard, at six in the morning, as the sailors burst off their ship and into the city, singing New York, New York.

You probably know the line: The Bronx is up, and the Battery's down. In the stage version, the song called New York a helluva town. For the movie, the censors made it a wonderful town.

The story was rooted in real life. During the war, thousands of sailors passed through the yard's gates every day, and the street just outside the main gate, Sands Street, was lined with bars, tattoo parlors, cheap restaurants and boarding houses, catering to sailors with money in their pockets and a few hours to spend it.

So stand at the gate and picture it: three sailors in white, running out into the morning, with a whole city in front of them and a day to see it.
=== DEEPER The Walloons' Bay | names
The yard sits on Wallabout Bay. The name comes from the Dutch, and it's usually translated as the bay of the Walloons.

The Walloons were French-speaking Protestants from what is now southern Belgium. Many had fled religious persecution, and some of the very first families to settle New Netherland, in the 1620s, were Walloons. Among them were Joris Rapalje and his wife, Catalina Trico. Their daughter Sarah, born in 1625, is often called the first European child born in the colony. In the 1630s, the Rapaljes acquired land along this bay and farmed it.

Catalina Trico lived into her eighties, and in old age, she gave sworn testimony about the colony's earliest days. It's one of the very few eyewitness accounts of the founding of what became New York.

Over the following centuries, the bay changed beyond recognition. The British anchored their prison ships here during the Revolution, as you heard. The Navy built its yard along the shore, and its dry docks, piers and buildings spread over filled-in marshes and mudflats. Next door, from the 1880s, the Wallabout Market grew into one of the largest produce markets in the city, until the Navy took over its land during the Second World War to expand the yard.

Today, most of the bay is gone, buried under docks and buildings. But its name still remembers a handful of Walloon farmers who arrived here four centuries ago.
=== STOP Fort Greene Park & the Prison Ship Martyrs Monument
=== DEEPER Life and Death on the Jersey | history
You heard that more than eleven thousand prisoners are believed to have died on the British prison ships in Wallabout Bay. The most notorious of them was the Jersey, an old warship stripped of its guns and anchored in the bay for the last years of the war. Prisoners called it Hell.

As many as a thousand men at a time were crammed below its decks, in darkness and stifling heat in summer and bitter cold in winter. Most were sailors captured aboard American privateers, privately owned ships licensed to raid British shipping. They were fed moldy, worm-eaten bread and spoiled meat, and the drinking water was foul. Smallpox, dysentery and fever swept through the ship.

According to survivors' accounts, each morning the guards called down through the hatches: Rebels, turn out your dead! The bodies were carried up, rowed ashore, and buried in shallow trenches in the sand, where the tides soon uncovered them.

There was one way out. Any prisoner who agreed to serve the British could leave the ship. Remarkably few accepted. Some tried to escape by slipping overboard at night and swimming for shore, and a few made it.

After the war, some survivors wrote memoirs of their captivity, and those memoirs, along with the bones that kept washing up along the shore for decades, made sure that Brooklyn never forgot. The prisoners are the reason this monument stands on the hill, looking out toward the bay where they died.
=== DEEPER The General Who Missed the Battle | people
The park and the neighborhood are named for Nathanael Greene, one of the most unlikely generals of the Revolution. He was a Quaker from Rhode Island, from a family that ran an iron forge. He taught himself military science from books, and he walked with a limp. Reportedly, his Quaker meeting expelled him for his interest in military affairs.

In the summer of 1776, Washington put Greene in charge of the defenses of Brooklyn. He studied the ground, the hills, the roads and the passes, and he built a line of forts, including one on this hill. Then, in mid-August, days before the British landed, he came down with a raging fever and had to be taken across the river to recover. The generals who replaced him didn't know the ground as he did, and in the Battle of Brooklyn, the British marched around the American flank through a pass that had been left nearly unguarded, as you can hear on the Prospect Park tour.

He recovered, and became one of Washington's most trusted generals. He made a costly mistake at Fort Washington, as you may have heard on the Washington Heights tour, but later he led the campaign in the South that wore the British down before Yorktown.

After the war, Georgia gave him a plantation near Savannah, called Mulberry Grove. He died there in 1786, at forty three, probably of sunstroke. A few years later, a young man staying at Mulberry Grove as a guest of his widow, Eli Whitney, built the first cotton gin there, a machine that helped spread cotton farming, and slavery, across the South.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Urn and the Eagles | look
Look up at the bronze urn on top of the column, about a hundred and fifty feet above you. Like the four bronze eagles on the granite posts at the corners of the plaza, it was made by Adolph Weinman, the sculptor of Civic Fame, the gilded figure on top of the Municipal Building in Manhattan, whom you may have heard about on the Civic Center tour. The monument's architect was Stanford White.

The urn was meant to hold a flame burning in memory of the prisoners. But the light went dark long ago, by most accounts in the early 1920s, and for most of the century, the top of the monument stayed dark.

The eagles had a rough century too. After years of vandalism, they were taken down in the 1960s and put away in storage.

In 2008, for the monument's hundredth birthday, a restoration cleaned the column and the plaza, brought the eagles back to their posts, and lit the top of the column again, now with electric light.

Look at the eagles, wings half spread, keeping watch over the stairway and the crypt beneath it. It's a fitting story for a memorial to men who were forgotten for so long: neglected, rescued, and finally lit up again.
=== STOP Fort Greene Brownstones
=== DEEPER Native Son's Brooklyn | people
You heard that Richard Wright wrote much of Native Son while living on Carlton Avenue, nearby. He had come to New York from Chicago in 1937, and in Fort Greene he lived for a time with friends, in a house a short walk from here. It's said that he often wrote outdoors, on a bench in Fort Greene Park.

Native Son, published in 1940, tells the story of Bigger Thomas, a poor young Black man on Chicago's South Side, who accidentally kills a young white woman, and of the poverty, fear and racism that shaped his life. It was the first novel by a Black American writer chosen by the Book-of-the-Month Club, and it became a bestseller almost overnight. The next year, Orson Welles directed a stage version on Broadway.

Wright followed it in 1945 with Black Boy, a memoir of his childhood in the South. Then, in 1946, tired of American racism, he moved to Paris, where he lived for the rest of his life. He died there in 1960, at fifty two.

Native Son changed American literature. It opened doors for a generation of Black writers, including James Baldwin, who began as Wright's protégé, and then famously criticized the novel in an essay, which strained their friendship.

So when you look along these quiet, leafy blocks, remember that one of the angriest, most powerful American novels of the century was written right here, among the brownstones.
=== DEEPER Reading a Brownstone | architecture
Stand across the street from one of these houses and read it from the bottom up.

At street level, under the stoop, is the ground floor, a few steps down, with its own small entrance. This was the service floor, where the kitchen was, and where servants worked in houses that had them.

The stoop leads up to the parlor floor, the most formal floor, with the tallest windows and the grandest rooms, raised above the dirt and noise of the street. The word stoop comes from the Dutch, a reminder of New York's Dutch roots.

Look at the doorway. Many of these houses have heavy carved hoods or brackets over the door, typical of the Italianate style, and tall double doors with arched panels.

At the roofline, look for the cornice, the projecting band along the top, often held up by carved brackets. Many cornices here are wood or pressed metal, painted to look like stone.

Now look closely at the brownstone itself. In many places, it's flaking, or peeling off in sheets. That's because builders often set the stone with its natural layers standing on edge, facing the street, instead of lying flat, the way they formed in the ground. Water seeps between the layers, freezes, and pushes the face off. Restorers patch it with tinted cement, carefully matched to the stone.

Finally, notice the iron railings and fences, many of them original.
=== DEEPER Where Brownstone Comes From | nature
Brownstone is sandstone, and its story begins about two hundred million years ago, in a great valley that stretched across what is now Connecticut and Massachusetts.

Rivers carried sand and mud into that valley, and over millions of years, the layers piled up and hardened into rock. Iron minerals in the sediment rusted, giving the stone its chocolate brown and reddish color. Dinosaurs walked across those muddy flats, and the same rock layers in the Connecticut River Valley are famous today for their dinosaur footprints.

Much of New York's brownstone came from quarries in Portland, Connecticut, on the banks of the Connecticut River. Quarrymen cut the stone out in huge blocks, and boats carried it down the river and along Long Island Sound to New York, where it was sawn into the slabs that face these houses. More came from quarries in New Jersey.

At the height of the brownstone craze, in the decades after the Civil War, it seemed that every street in Brooklyn wanted it. But fashions changed. By the early twentieth century, builders preferred lighter stone, brick and, increasingly, concrete, and the industry declined. Then the river finished it off. A great flood in 1936, and the hurricane of 1938, filled the Portland quarries with water, and they closed. Today the flooded pits are a park.

So run your hand over a stoop here. You're touching sand that settled in a valley in Connecticut when dinosaurs were still walking past.
=== STOP BAM: The Brooklyn Academy of Music
=== DEEPER Caruso's Last Brooklyn Night | history
You heard that the great tenor Enrico Caruso sang at BAM in its early years. This stage also saw the beginning of the end of his career.

On December 11th, 1920, Caruso was performing here in Donizetti's comic opera L'elisir d'amore when he began bleeding from his throat. As the story is usually told, he kept singing through the first act, dabbing at his mouth between phrases while his colleagues handed him towels in the wings, until the rest of the performance was called off.

He was seriously ill. He managed a few more performances that month, and his last appearance at the Metropolitan Opera came on Christmas Eve, 1920. Soon after, he collapsed with a severe infection, and he never sang in public again. He went home to Italy to recover, and he died in Naples in August 1921, at forty eight.

So the voice that had made Caruso the most famous singer in the world, and one of the first great recording stars, began to fail on a Brooklyn stage, in front of a Brooklyn audience who didn't yet know what they were witnessing.
=== DEEPER Einstein on the Beach | culture
You heard that BAM's Next Wave Festival showcased the composer Philip Glass and the director Robert Wilson. Their most famous collaboration is an opera called Einstein on the Beach.

It premiered in France in 1976 and played two sold-out nights at the Metropolitan Opera House that fall, in a theater its creators had rented with their own money. In 1984, BAM brought it back as part of the Next Wave, and it returned here again in 1992 and in 2012.

It's unlike any other opera. It runs about four and a half hours, without an intermission, and the audience is invited to come and go as they please. There's no plot. Instead there are images: a train, a trial, a bed, a spaceship, and a violinist dressed as Einstein. The chorus sings numbers, one, two, three, four, and the syllables do, re, mi, over Glass's rippling, repeating music, while performers recite texts, some written by a young poet named Christopher Knowles. Short scenes called Knee Plays connect the acts.

Some people walk out. Others say it changed their lives. Either way, it's the kind of work that made BAM famous, and that made people cross the river to see it.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Theater Built to Look Ruined | look
A block west, on Fulton Street, BAM has a second, very different theater. It opened in 1904 as the Majestic, a playhouse that later showed movies and then sat empty for years.

In 1987, the British director Peter Brook needed a New York home for his nine-hour stage epic The Mahabharata, based on the ancient Sanskrit poem. Brook loved worn old theaters. So instead of restoring the Majestic to look new, BAM restored it as a ruin. Workers made the building safe but kept its peeling, faded paint, its patched plaster and exposed brick, so that it feels like an ancient amphitheater that has seen centuries of performances.

In 1999, it was renamed the Harvey Theater, in honor of Harvey Lichtenstein, who you heard turned BAM around.

If you can, step inside and look at the walls. That beautiful decay is carefully maintained. It's one of the few buildings in New York that is kept looking old on purpose.
=== STOP The Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower
=== DEEPER The Missing H | names
Look at the name on the tower: Williamsburgh, with an h at the end. It isn't a typo. It's how the name was spelled when the bank was founded, in 1851.

Williamsburg was laid out around 1800 and named for Jonathan Williams, the Army engineer who surveyed it. Williams was a grandnephew of Benjamin Franklin, and he became the first superintendent of the military academy at West Point. The settlement grew into a village, and then, in 1852, into a city of its own, the City of Williamsburgh, with its own government, just upriver from the City of Brooklyn.

Its independence lasted three years. In 1855, Williamsburgh merged into Brooklyn, along with the town of Bushwick, and in the process, it lost its h. The bank, like a few other institutions with long memories, kept the old spelling.

The bank's first great building still stands in Williamsburg, at Broadway and Driggs Avenue, near the foot of the Williamsburg Bridge. It's a domed palace from 1875, designed by George B. Post, the architect of the Center for Brooklyn History, which you may have heard about on the Brooklyn Heights tour. If you take the Williamsburg tour, look for it near Peter Luger.

By the 1920s, Brooklyn's center of gravity had shifted toward downtown, and the bank built this tower beside the new transit hub at Atlantic and Flatbush, where trains from all over the borough came together. So the tallest building in Brooklyn for eighty years carried, right on its front, the name of a lost city, spelled the way that city spelled it.
=== DEEPER Banks for Working People | companies
The Williamsburgh was a mutual savings bank, a kind of bank that has almost disappeared. A mutual savings bank had no stockholders. In principle, it belonged to its depositors, and it was run by trustees, often prominent local citizens, who were supposed to act as stewards rather than owners.

The idea came from Britain. New York's first savings bank, the Bank for Savings, opened in 1819, to encourage thrift among working people, at a time when commercial banks had little interest in small accounts. Savings banks took deposits of a few dollars, or even a few cents, paid steady interest, and lent the money out, mostly as home mortgages. Many New Yorkers opened their first account as schoolchildren, depositing coins every week through school savings programs run by banks like this one. The Dime Savings Bank of Brooklyn, whose domed building stands near the Fulton Mall, reportedly took its name from the idea that a dime was enough to open an account.

Their buildings were part of the pitch. A savings bank had to convince working families that their money was safe, so it built like a temple or a fortress, with columns, domes, marble halls, and sometimes a tower you could see from anywhere in the borough.

In the late twentieth century, deregulation, the savings and loan crisis and a wave of mergers swept most of these banks away. Many converted into ordinary banks owned by stockholders, and the Williamsburgh was eventually absorbed into larger banks. The temples remain, turned into apartments, event spaces and shops, monuments to an age when saving a dime a week was a civic virtue.
=== STOP Barclays Center & the Dodgers' Lost Stadium
=== DEEPER The Dome That Never Was | architecture
You heard that Walter O'Malley wanted a new stadium at this corner, possibly a domed one. In 1955, he asked Buckminster Fuller, the inventor of the geodesic dome, whom you may have heard about on the East Village tour, to imagine one.

Fuller and his students worked up designs for a huge, light dome over the entire ballpark, so that games would never be rained out and fans would be sheltered from the weather. It would have been the first domed stadium in the world. The Houston Astrodome, which usually gets that title, didn't open until 1965.

It never got past models and drawings. Without the land, there was no stadium, and without the stadium, O'Malley went west.

More than half a century later, a very different arena rose here. Frank Gehry, the architect of the Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao, had designed the original plan for the project, but his design was dropped as too expensive. The Barclays Center that was built instead was designed by SHoP Architects, wrapped in thousands of panels of steel that were rusted on purpose, a nod to Brooklyn's industrial past. In 2015, a green roof of low, hardy plants was added on top, partly to soften the noise for the neighbors.

Look up at the oculus over the entrance plaza, the great opening in the canopy. Then imagine, in its place, a dome of shining triangles, with the Dodgers playing underneath.
=== DEEPER Hilda's Cowbell | culture
You heard about Ebbets Field. It opened in 1913, built by the team's owner, Charles Ebbets, on a site in Flatbush known as Pigtown, a scruffy area of shacks and garbage dumps where pigs once rooted. It was small, and that was part of its magic. Fans were close enough to talk to the players, and many did.

Along the right field wall was an advertisement for a clothing store owned by a merchant named Abe Stark: Hit sign, win suit. Hardly anyone ever won, because the right fielder stood right in front of it. Stark became so well known that he was later elected Brooklyn borough president.

The fans were part of the show. Hilda Chester, a woman with a booming voice, rang a cowbell from the bleachers for decades and became the most famous fan in baseball. A band of amateur musicians called the Dodgers Sym-Phony roamed the stands, playing gleefully off-key, and Gladys Gooding played the organ.

Year after year, the Dodgers reached the World Series, and year after year, they lost to the Yankees. Fans learned to say, Wait till next year. Next year finally came in 1955, when they beat the Yankees at last.

Two years later, they were gone. In 1960, a wrecking ball painted to look like a baseball knocked Ebbets Field down. Apartment buildings stand there now, called the Ebbets Field Apartments. And older Brooklynites will still tell you exactly where they were sitting.
=== DEEPER Why Dodgers? | names
The team had many names before it settled on Dodgers. In the 1880s, it was the Grays, and then the Bridegrooms, after several players married in the same season. In those years, it played at a ballpark next to the Old Stone House in Park Slope, as you can hear on the Prospect Park tour.

Around 1899, the team became the Superbas, after a popular vaudeville troupe called Hanlon's Superbas, a play on the name of the team's new manager, Ned Hanlon. From 1914 to 1931, under a beloved, portly manager named Wilbert Robinson, the team was often called the Robins.

Through it all, many people called them the Trolley Dodgers. Brooklyn in the 1890s was laced with streetcar lines, and pedestrians had to dodge the trolleys to cross the street. New Yorkers across the river used the name to tease Brooklynites. The Brooklynites adopted it, and in the early 1930s, Dodgers became the team's official name.

Then there was Dem Bums, the affectionate nickname for a team that always seemed to lose in the end, drawn by the newspaper cartoonist Willard Mullin as a ragged, unshaven tramp with a cigar stub.

When the team moved to Los Angeles, it took the name along, although there were no trolleys left to dodge there, only freeways.
=== STOP Fulton Mall & Junior's
=== DEEPER The Cheesecake Question | food
You heard that Junior's has been serving cheesecake since 1950. Before that, reportedly, the Rosen family ran a sandwich shop on this corner. When Harry Rosen reopened it as Junior's, he set out to make the best cheesecake in New York. As the story goes, he worked for months with a baker named Eigel Peterson, trying recipe after recipe.

The result is classic New York cheesecake: tall, dense, rich and creamy, made with cream cheese, eggs, sugar and heavy cream. Junior's version sits on a thin layer of sponge cake, instead of the graham cracker crust you'll find in many other places.

Cream cheese itself is a New York invention. In 1872, a dairyman named William Lawrence, in Chester, in upstate New York, began making it. A few years later, a New York cheese distributor branded it Philadelphia, because Philadelphia was then famous for fine dairy products. The name stuck, and so did the confusion.

Cheesecake made with cream cheese became a specialty of the delis and restaurants of New York in the early twentieth century, at places like Lindy's on Broadway, and Junior's carried the style into Brooklyn and made it famous across the country. Today Junior's has branches in Manhattan and ships its cheesecakes nationwide, but this corner is the original.

If you go in, the plain one is the classic. Purists say you don't need anything else.
=== DEEPER The Albee Square Mall | culture
Near the corner of Fulton Street and DeKalb Avenue stood the Albee Theatre, a grand vaudeville and movie palace that opened in 1925. It was named for Edward F. Albee, the vaudeville impresario, whose adopted grandson was the playwright Edward Albee.

In the late 1970s, the theater was torn down and replaced by the Albee Square Mall, an indoor mall that became a hangout for Brooklyn's young Black and Latino shoppers, full of sneaker stores, gold jewelry counters, and stalls selling records and mixtapes.

In 1988, the rapper Biz Markie, the clown prince of hip-hop, recorded a song called Albee Square Mall, celebrating it as the place to hang out, see and be seen, and buy gold. Fulton Street's gold chains, rings and teeth became part of hip-hop style. Biggie Smalls, the Notorious B.I.G., one of the greatest rappers of all time, grew up on Saint James Place in nearby Clinton Hill, and came of age a short walk from here.

The mall was demolished in the late 2000s, and the City Point complex, with apartment towers, shops, and an underground food hall, rose in its place. The old name lives on in the little plaza at the corner, still called Albee Square.

So if you hear old-timers talk about the Albee Square Mall, they're not talking about a building so much as a time and a scene, when this stretch of Fulton Street was one of the capitals of hip-hop.
=== DEEPER The Most Filmed Station | underfoot
A few blocks from here, at Hoyt and Schermerhorn Streets, is Hoyt-Schermerhorn station, one of the oddest in the subway. It has more tracks and platforms than it needs. Two of its platforms have been out of regular service for decades, left over from a shuttle line to the Court Street station, which closed in 1946 and later became the New York Transit Museum.

Because those empty platforms can be closed off without disturbing riders, film and television crews love them, and the station has appeared in countless productions. Its long platforms and tiled walls can pass for New York in almost any decade, which is exactly what filmmakers want.

The most famous is The Warriors, the 1979 cult film about a Coney Island gang fighting its way home across a hostile city. The station reportedly stood in for Union Square in the film's final subway showdown.

In 1987, the director Martin Scorsese filmed Michael Jackson's music video for Bad here, with Jackson and his dancers facing off against a rival gang in the station.

So when you change trains here, look around. You may be standing on one of the most filmed subway platforms in New York.
