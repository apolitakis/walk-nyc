=== STOP St. John's Terminal & Hudson Square
=== DEEPER The Chapel, the Commodore and the Bronze | history
You heard how Trinity Church sold St. John's Park to Cornelius Vanderbilt in 1867. Here's what became of the pieces.

Start with the chapel. St. John's Chapel was finished in 1807, designed by the brothers John and Isaac McComb, with a columned portico and a tall steeple rising over the park. It stayed even after the park became a freight yard, though its congregation dwindled. In 1892, Trinity's leaders announced plans to tear it down, and the remaining parishioners rebelled, joined by New Yorkers who simply loved the building. Their pastor, Philip Brown, led the holdouts until his death in 1909. It was one of the city's earliest fights to save a historic building. In the end, the street won. When the city widened Varick Street for the new subway, the chapel's portico stood in the way, and the whole building came down in 1918. If you ride the 1 train, look for the mosaics in the Canal Street station that remember it.

Then the depot. Vanderbilt's freight depot went up in 1868, and the next year he unveiled a monument New Yorkers never forgot: an enormous bronze sculpture across the front of the building, crowded with steamships, locomotives and scenes from his career, with a statue of the Commodore himself standing in the middle. It was designed by Albert De Groot, who had once captained one of Vanderbilt's steamships, and sculpted by Ernst Plassmann. Critics found it ridiculous: a monument a man had raised to himself, on top of a freight shed.

The statue outlived the depot. In 1929, it was moved to Grand Central Terminal, the family's grandest monument, where it still stands on the south side of the building, above Park Avenue. The depot was later torn down, and its land became the exit plaza of the Holland Tunnel. When you see cars pouring out of the tunnel and around the traffic circle a few blocks south of here, you're looking at the ground where the city's most exclusive park once stood.
=== DEEPER Trinity Church, Landlord | companies
Much of the ground around you belongs to a church, and that church is one of the great landlords of New York.

Trinity's fortune began in 1705, when Queen Anne granted the parish a large farm on Manhattan's west side, land that had passed from the Dutch to the English crown. Over the next two centuries, the city grew over it. Trinity sold some of the land, gave some away for other churches and for King's College, which became Columbia University, and leased much of the rest on long leases, collecting ground rent while others built.

In the early twentieth century, the city cut through the neighborhood. Seventh Avenue was pushed south, Varick Street was widened, and the subway was dug underneath. Trinity and its neighbors put up big, heavy loft buildings, and printers filled them. For decades, the presses here turned out magazines, books, catalogs, stock certificates and phone books, and typesetters, binders and paper merchants clustered around them.

When printing moved away, the area was rebranded in the 1990s as Hudson Square, after the old park. Trinity pushed for a rezoning, approved by the city in 2013, that allowed apartments alongside offices. Then, in 2015, the church went into partnership with an unexpected investor: Norway's sovereign wealth fund, which invests that country's oil money. The fund paid about one and a half billion dollars for a stake of nearly half in eleven of Trinity's office buildings here.

So as you walk south, you're crossing land granted by an English queen to a church, now shared with a Norwegian oil fund and filled with tech companies. Look at the buildings as you go. Many still have their freight elevators, loading docks and huge steel-framed windows, built to light the press rooms. The rents pay for Trinity's churches, programs and charitable grants, as the land has done for more than three hundred years.
=== DEEPER Clifford Holland's Tunnel | underfoot
The Holland Tunnel was one of the great engineering feats of its time, and it cost the lives of the two men who first led it.

When construction began, in 1920, no one had ever built a long underwater tunnel for cars. The hard part wasn't the digging. It was the air. Car exhaust carries carbon monoxide, and in a tube more than a mile and a half long, with thousands of cars passing every hour, drivers could be poisoned. The engineers worked with the United States Bureau of Mines and with scientists from Yale University, who tested the gas on volunteers to learn how much people could safely breathe. Their answer was a new kind of ventilation: fresh air blown in through ducts beneath the roadway, and foul air drawn out through the ceiling, around the clock.

The digging itself was done by sandhogs, who pushed huge steel shields through the mud beneath the river, working in compressed air to hold back the water, and lined the tunnel behind them with rings of cast iron.

The chief engineer, Clifford Holland, was thirty six when he took the job, and he drove himself without rest. In 1924, worn out, he went to a sanatorium in Michigan to recover, and died there of a heart attack at forty one, just before the two halves of the tunnel were due to meet under the river. His successor, Milton Freeman, died of pneumonia a few months later. A third engineer, Ole Singstad, finished the job, and went on to build the Queens Midtown and Brooklyn Battery Tunnels.

The tunnel opened in November 1927. Before any cars were let in, about twenty thousand people walked through it, for the thrill of strolling under the Hudson. It was named for the man who didn't live to see it, and the ventilation system his team designed became a model for road tunnels around the world.
=== STOP Hook & Ladder 8 & the Heart of Tribeca
=== DEEPER The Firehouse That Was Cut in Half | architecture
This firehouse looks perfectly proportioned, but you're looking at only half of the building that opened here in 1903.

When Ladder 8 moved in, the firehouse was twice as wide, with two big doors, and the rigs were pulled by horses. In the firehouses of that era, the horses lived in stalls on the ground floor, behind the apparatus, and the firefighters slept upstairs, ready to slide down a brass pole. When the alarm bell rang, the horses were trained to step out of their stalls and take their places in front of the rig, where harnesses hanging from the ceiling dropped onto their backs. A good company could be rolling in moments.

Then came the subway. Around 1913, the city widened Varick Street for the new line being dug beneath it, and the firehouse was in the way. Rather than move the company, the fire department cut the building down to fit. The half nearest Varick Street was taken down, a new wall went up along the new sidewalk, and the front you see on North Moore Street, with its single big door, is the half that survived. The same street widening doomed St. John's Chapel, a few blocks north.

The horses didn't last much longer. The department replaced them with motor rigs through the 1910s, and in 1922, the last horse-drawn engine in the city made its final run, in Brooklyn, with a ceremony to mark the end of an era.

The firehouse itself has been well looked after. In the late 2010s it was renovated for about six million dollars, while Ladder 8 spent about three years in a firehouse in SoHo. Then the company moved back home, and its rig still squeezes out of that single door onto North Moore Street, to answer calls all over lower Manhattan.
=== DEEPER Ghostbusters and the Movie Firehouse | culture
In 1984, a comedy about three scientists who lose their university jobs and go into business catching ghosts made this firehouse one of the most famous buildings in the world.

In the film, the three buy a run-down old firehouse for their headquarters. Dan Aykroyd's character, Ray, falls in love with it and slides down the pole, and the team parks their converted 1959 Cadillac ambulance, the Ecto One, in the bay.

Here's a movie secret. Only the outside was filmed here. Most of the interior scenes were shot in Los Angeles, in an old downtown firehouse that had been taken out of service, Fire Station 23, which fans still visit on the West Coast.

The movie also caught a real moment in New York. In the 1970s, during the city's fiscal crisis, the fire department shut down companies across the city, and lower Manhattan was full of empty industrial lofts that a team of oddballs could rent for very little. Under the jokes, Ghostbusters is a movie about downtown real estate.

The firefighters here have made peace with their fame. Inside, they display half of an actual sign used in the sequel, Ghostbusters Two, from 1989. When Harold Ramis, who played Egon, died in 2014, they hung a Ghostbusters sign outside in his memory. And in 2021, a fan group from Buffalo raised money for a new, weatherproof copy of the famous logo, a ghost inside a red circle with a slash through it, and gave it to the firehouse.

If you'd like more of the movie's New York, it opens at the main library on Fifth Avenue at Forty Second Street, with its stone lions, and Sigourney Weaver's character lives in the grand apartment building at 55 Central Park West, where the film's final battle takes place on the roof.
=== DEEPER Triangles, Lofts and Landmarks | names
You heard that Tribeca is short for Triangle Below Canal. The story behind the name is odder than it sounds.

In the early 1970s, during a fight over zoning, the residents of one small, triangle-shaped block just south of Canal Street, between Church Street and Broadway, formed a group they called the Triangle Below Canal Block Association. They soon shortened that to the Tribeca Block Association. Other accounts credit city planners with the name. Either way, by the mid 1970s it was in print, and it had spread to the whole district, west to the river and south to Chambers Street. That area isn't a triangle at all. It's a lopsided four-sided shape. The name stuck anyway.

The artists who moved into Tribeca's empty warehouses mostly lived there illegally, in buildings zoned for manufacturing. In 1982, the state passed the Loft Law, which protected tenants who had moved into commercial lofts and required landlords to bring the buildings up to the code for homes. It let many of the pioneers stay, but it couldn't stop the neighborhood from changing around them.

Preservationists, meanwhile, went to work on the buildings themselves. In 1991 and 1992, the city created four historic districts here, Tribeca West, East, North and South, protecting hundreds of the warehouses and stores you're walking past. That's why these streets still look much as they did a century ago, even as lofts with eighteen foot ceilings sell for tens of millions of dollars.

Real estate agents have been trying to repeat the trick ever since, inventing names for every corner of the city. A few, like Nolita, for North of Little Italy, have caught on. Most fade away. Tribeca lasted, partly because it sounds good, and partly because the people who lived here embraced it.
=== STOP Harrison Street & the Lost Washington Market
=== DEEPER What They Sold at Washington Market | food
Picture this neighborhood at four in the morning around 1900. You'd hardly be able to move, and you'd smell the market before you saw it.

Wagons, and later trucks, jammed every street. Dealers sold by the crate and the barrel to grocers, hotel chefs, restaurant owners and pushcart peddlers, who loaded up before dawn and fanned out across the city. There were mountains of oysters and clams, barrels of apples, crates of oranges and bananas off the ships, butter and cheese from upstate farms, and live chickens by the thousands.

In the nineteenth century there was also an enormous trade in wild game. A butcher named Thomas De Voe, who kept a stall at the Jefferson Market in Greenwich Village, published a book in 1867 called The Market Assistant, describing everything sold in the markets of New York and other eastern cities. It lists venison and bear meat, wild turkeys, ducks and geese, and a long list of small birds, robins among them, sold by the dozen. It's a picture of a time before game laws, when almost anything that ran, flew or swam could end up on a New York table.

Some dealers specialized in a single thing: eggs, or poultry, or turtles and terrapin for the rich soups that fancy restaurants served. Many of these businesses were family firms, handed down for generations.

The market was also a mess. Food spoiled in the gutters, carts and trucks blocked the streets for hours, and each middleman along the way added to the price. For decades, reformers argued that New Yorkers paid more for their food because of it, which is part of why the city finally moved the trade to the Bronx.

Look around for the clues that remain: metal canopies over the sidewalks, where crates were stacked out of the rain, loading platforms at the height of a wagon bed, and the names of old food companies, still carved or painted on the buildings.
=== DEEPER Butter, Eggs and the Mercantile Exchange | companies
Walk to the corner of Harrison and Hudson Streets and look for the red brick building with the tower over its entrance. It was the home of a market that grew from butter and eggs into oil.

In 1872, dairy merchants in this district founded the Butter and Cheese Exchange of New York, so they could trade under agreed rules, grades and prices. Eggs were soon added, and in 1882 the exchange took a grander name: the New York Mercantile Exchange. In the mid 1880s it moved into this building, designed by Thomas Jackson in a style called Queen Anne, and traders shouted their bids on the floor inside for about ninety years.

For much of the twentieth century, the exchange's biggest business was potato futures: contracts to buy or sell Maine potatoes at a set price on a set date. Then, in 1976, traders who had sold contracts for May potatoes failed to deliver them, the largest default the futures markets had ever seen. The scandal nearly ruined the exchange.

It survived by reinventing itself. It left this building for the new World Trade Center, and began trading contracts on heating oil, and then, in 1983, on crude oil. That crude oil contract became one of the most closely watched prices on earth. When you hear on the news that the price of oil rose or fell, it's often the price set by a market that began with butter, a few steps from where you're standing.

In 2008, the exchange was bought by its great rival, the Chicago Mercantile Exchange. Its old home here has been converted to new uses, but look up at the tower over the Harrison Street entrance, recently restored with a new copper spire. It's a monument to the butter and egg men.
=== DEEPER Annetje Jans and the Endless Lawsuit | legends
You heard that Duane Park was once part of a farm owned by a Dutch woman named Annetje Jans. For nearly two centuries, people claiming to be her heirs tried to take that farm back.

Annetje Jans came to New Netherland around 1630 with her first husband, Roelof Jansen. In 1636, the Dutch governor granted them a farm of about sixty two acres, just outside the little town of New Amsterdam, along the shore of the Hudson, beginning near today's Warren Street and running north toward Canal Street. After Roelof died, Annetje married the minister of the Dutch church, Everardus Bogardus, and the land became known as the Domine's farm, the minister's farm.

She died in 1663. In 1671, her heirs sold the farm to the English governor, Francis Lovelace. All of them signed the deed, except the family of one son, Cornelius Bogardus, who had died a few years before. That missing signature was the seed of the legend.

The farm passed to the English crown, and in 1705 Queen Anne granted it to Trinity Church. As the city grew over it, the land became some of the most valuable on earth, and Annetje's descendants multiplied into thousands of families across America. Many grew up hearing that they were the rightful owners of lower Manhattan, cheated out of their inheritance by a church.

They went to court again and again. In 1749, one descendant simply moved onto part of the old farm, and lost when Trinity took him to court. Another case, begun in 1830, dragged on until 1847 before it was dismissed. Trinity won every time, in part because the church had held the land openly for so long. Still the legend lived on, and through the nineteenth century, promoters collected money from hopeful descendants to pay for new lawsuits.

So the legend is checked: no court ever accepted the heirs' claim. But the story is true in another way. Annetje Jans's farm really is under your feet, and Trinity's fortune really did grow from it.
=== STOP Telecom Fortresses: 33 Thomas Street & 60 Hudson
=== DEEPER Western Union and the Telephone It Turned Down | legends
Western Union was the most powerful communications company in America for half a century, and the most famous story about it is almost certainly made up.

The company grew in the 1850s by buying up and merging small telegraph lines. In 1861 it completed the first telegraph line across the continent, and two days later, the Pony Express, which had carried mail on horseback across the West, announced it was shutting down. Western Union carried news for the papers, orders for the railroads, and stock prices for brokers. A young Thomas Edison worked as a telegraph operator, and later invented machines for the company.

Now the story. In 1876, Alexander Graham Bell's backers are said to have offered his telephone patent to Western Union for a hundred thousand dollars, and to have been turned down. A memo is often quoted, supposedly from a Western Union committee, saying that the telephone had too many shortcomings to be seriously considered as a means of communication. That memo is almost certainly a fake. It surfaced decades later, and no one has ever found an original. There may have been an informal offer, and Western Union certainly didn't buy the patent. But the company wasn't blind to telephones. It quickly hired Edison and others to build telephones of its own and went into the business, until 1879, when it settled a patent lawsuit with Bell's company and agreed to leave telephones to Bell, in return for a share of the rental fees.

Western Union carried on with telegrams for another century. It introduced the singing telegram in the 1930s, and grew into a business that sends money around the world. It sent its last telegram in 2006.

Its old headquarters at 60 Hudson Street still hums with wires, but the messages passing through them now belong to the internet.
=== DEEPER The Wires Under Your Feet | underfoot
Look up as you walk around here and notice what you don't see: telephone poles and overhead wires. Their absence has a story.

By the 1880s, the streets of lower Manhattan were a tangle of wires. Telegraph companies, telephone companies, stock ticker services, fire alarm systems and the new electric light companies each strung their own lines on their own poles, some of them as tall as ninety feet and carrying dozens of crossbars. On some streets the wires were so thick they darkened the sky. The most dangerous were the high voltage lines for the new electric arc lights.

The state ordered the wires buried in the 1880s, but the companies dragged their feet. Then the Blizzard of 1888 brought down poles and wires all over the city. And in October 1889, a Western Union lineman named John Feeks was electrocuted while working high on a pole at Centre and Chambers Streets, near City Hall. His body hung in the tangle of wires above a horrified crowd.

That was the end of the poles. Mayor Hugh Grant sent crews with axes to chop down the poles of companies that wouldn't comply. The wires went into underground conduits, which New Yorkers called subways years before the trains took that name. One of the companies formed to build and run them, the Empire City Subway Company, founded in 1891, still manages the conduits for telephone and internet cables under Manhattan's streets.

When fiber optic cables arrived a century later, many of them were pulled through those same old ducts. That's part of why buildings like 60 Hudson Street became internet hubs. The paths under these streets lead to them, as they once led to the telegraph headquarters.

So every manhole cover you pass is a door into another city below, full of copper, glass fiber and history.
=== DEEPER Ralph Walker's Palaces for Wires | architecture
Three of the finest Art Deco buildings in lower Manhattan were built for the companies that carried words and voices, and one architect designed all three.

Ralph Walker was a young designer at the firm of McKenzie, Voorhees and Gmelin when the New York Telephone Company asked for a new headquarters in the early 1920s. His Barclay Vesey Building, finished in 1926 beside today's World Trade Center, was the first skyscraper to treat the city's new zoning rules, which made tall buildings step back as they rose, as a chance for drama instead of a burden. It's often called one of the first Art Deco skyscrapers.

Then came 60 Hudson Street for Western Union, finished in 1930, with the brickwork you heard about, shading from dark to light as it rises. And a few blocks north of here, at 32 Avenue of the Americas, Walker enlarged an older building and gave it new fronts for AT&T's long distance service, finished in 1932.

If you have a few minutes, walk up and look into its lobby, which is an official city interior landmark. One wall holds a tiled map of the world, its continents separated by oceans but tied together by lines of wire, under the words: Telephone wires and radio unite to make neighbors of nations. Overhead is a glittering ceiling of glass mosaic by Hildreth Meière, the artist who covered the banking hall at One Wall Street in red and gold, with figures of the continents and messengers linked by golden lines. It's an office building, so you may only be able to look in from the door.

Walker was proud and opinionated. Frank Lloyd Wright, who admired very few architects, reportedly called him the only other honest architect in America. In 1957, when the American Institute of Architects turned one hundred, it created a special award for him, and The New York Times called him the Architect of the Century. Today he's half forgotten, but his telephone palaces are still here, still full of wires.
=== STOP Greene Street Cast Iron
=== DEEPER The Highway That Would Have Erased SoHo | politics
You heard that Robert Moses wanted to run an expressway along Broome Street, a few blocks south of here. Here's how close it came.

The Lower Manhattan Expressway was first proposed in the early 1940s, and for years it was official city policy. As planned in the 1960s, it would have been ten lanes wide, much of it raised above the streets, slicing across the island. Estimates at the time said it would displace about two thousand families and some eight hundred businesses, many of them small manufacturers employing thousands of workers. The buildings on this street would have stood in its shadow, if they survived at all.

The opposition was a patchwork: families in Little Italy and on the Lower East Side, small business owners, churches, preservationists, artists quietly living in lofts, and the writer Jane Jacobs, who had already fought Moses in Greenwich Village. In April 1968, at a state hearing on the plan, Jacobs and other protesters rushed the stage, and the stenographer's tape was torn up and scattered. Jacobs was arrested and charged with inciting a riot, among other things. The charges were later reduced.

In 1969, Mayor John Lindsay, who had once supported the road, withdrew his backing, and a couple of years later the expressway was finally wiped off the city's plans.

Without the highway hanging over it, the neighborhood could finally plan a future. The city legalized artists' lofts in 1971, and in 1973 the cast iron district was protected. If Moses had won, you might be standing beside a wall of concrete pillars, in the roar of trucks. Instead you're on one of the most beautiful streets in America.
=== DEEPER Hell's Hundred Acres | history
You heard that firefighters called this district Hell's Hundred Acres. Here's why, and how that reputation nearly doomed it.

The cast iron fronts made the buildings look solid, but behind them most had wooden floors and joists, and they were packed with bales of cloth, paper and rags. When a fire took hold, iron columns could crack when cold water from the hoses hit them, and whole fronts could come down. Firefighters learned to keep their distance.

The deadliest fire came on March 19th, 1958, at 623 Broadway, a few blocks east of here. Flames trapped workers at the Monarch Underwear Company on the upper floors. The building had no sprinklers, and twenty four people died, a short walk from the site of the Triangle Shirtwaist fire, nearly half a century later. Mayor Robert Wagner called for laws requiring sprinklers in loft buildings, and the city tightened its rules.

Fires like that gave the district's critics ammunition. In 1962, a civic group, the City Club of New York, published a report called The Wastelands of New York City, which described this area as a commercial slum, ready to be cleared. But when the city asked a planner named Chester Rapkin to study the district, he found something else: hundreds of small businesses employing thousands of workers, in jobs that depended on cheap space near the center of the city. His 1963 report helped make the case against tearing it all down. Rapkin is also credited with the district's nickname: SoHo, for South of Houston.

So the district survived its reputation. Today almost every building on this street has sprinklers and fire escapes, and the hundred acres are among the most valuable in the world.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Reading a Cast Iron Street | look
Stand on Greene Street and look for the details most shoppers walk right past.

Start at your feet. The street is paved with Belgian blocks, rectangular granite paving stones laid to stand up to iron wagon wheels and heavy loads. People call them cobblestones, but true cobblestones are rounded stones from riverbeds.

Look at the vault lights in the sidewalk, the small round glass lenses set in iron plates. Many have turned a pale purple. That's because the glass contained manganese, which slowly turns violet after decades in the sun.

Now look at the bottoms of the iron columns on the building fronts, just above the sidewalk. Many carry small raised plates with the names and addresses of the foundries that cast them, names like Cornell, or Badger's Architectural Iron Works, cast right into the building like a signature.

Then walk to the corner of Prince Street and look at the wall of 112 Prince Street that faces Greene Street. Count its windows, columns and cornices. Almost all of them are paint. In the mid 1970s, the artist Richard Haas painted that blank brick wall to match the building's real cast iron front around the corner, with more than fifty painted windows. Look for the painted cat in one of them. The architecture critic Ada Louise Huxtable called the mural a triumph. It was Haas's first outdoor mural, and after four decades of weather it was carefully repainted, and rededicated in 2024.

That wall sums up SoHo: artists making something beautiful out of a blank industrial wall, in tribute to the buildings they had come to love.
=== STOP The Haughwout Building & the First Passenger Elevator
=== DEEPER Elisha Otis, the Man Who Made Falling Safe | people
The man who made skyscrapers possible was a tinkerer who never lived to see one.

Elisha Graves Otis was born on a farm in Vermont in 1811 and drifted through many trades: wagon driver, mill builder, wagon and carriage maker, and inventor of small machines. In 1852 he was working as a master mechanic at a bedstead factory in Yonkers, just north of the city, when he had to work out how to move heavy equipment to the upper floors. Hoisting platforms were common, but workers feared them, because when the rope broke, the platform and everything on it fell.

His answer was simple and brilliant. He fixed a wagon spring to the top of the platform, attached to the hoisting rope. As long as the rope pulled taut, the spring stayed bent. If the rope snapped, the spring straightened and pushed two catches outward into toothed rails on either side of the shaft, locking the platform in place. He started a small business making safety hoists, and in 1854 he took one to the Crystal Palace exhibition, on the site of today's Bryant Park, for the famous demonstration you heard about.

Otis died in 1861, at forty nine, only four years after the Haughwout elevator went in. His sons, Charles and Norton, built up the company. Otis elevators went into hotels, stores and office towers across the country. In 1889 the company built elevators that climb the curving legs of the Eiffel Tower, and in the twentieth century, Otis elevators carried people up the Empire State Building and the original World Trade Center.

The safety elevator changed how buildings were valued, too. Before elevators, the best rooms were on the lower floors, and the top floors went to servants and the poor, who had to climb. After elevators, the top floors became the most desirable of all. The penthouse is, in a sense, an Otis invention.
=== DEEPER Broadway's Marble and Iron Palaces | companies
In the 1850s, this stretch of Broadway was the most fashionable shopping street in America, and the stores along it helped invent the way we still shop.

It began a little south of here, at Broadway and Chambers Street, where an Irish immigrant named Alexander Turney Stewart opened his Marble Palace in 1846: a huge store faced in white marble, often called the first department store in America. Stewart sold an enormous range of goods under one roof, at fixed prices, with no haggling, behind big plate glass windows. It was a place where respectable women could spend a day shopping on their own. Stewart became one of the richest men in the country.

Rivals soon lined Broadway north into SoHo. Lord and Taylor and Brooks Brothers both had stores at Broadway and Grand Street, a block south of here. Tiffany and Company was a couple of blocks north, at 550 Broadway, with a carved wooden figure of Atlas holding up a clock over its entrance. That Atlas clock moved uptown with the company, and it still stands over the door of its flagship on Fifth Avenue.

Just across Broadway and up the block stood the St. Nicholas Hotel, opened in 1853, one of the most luxurious hotels in the world, with hundreds of rooms and marble and gilding everywhere.

And Haughwout's sold fine china, silver and glass. In May 1861, the new first lady, Mary Todd Lincoln, came to New York on a shopping trip to refurnish the White House, and ordered a state dinner service of French porcelain, decorated by Haughwout's with a wide border in a fashionable purplish pink called solferino, around the arms of the United States. Pieces of it survive in the White House collection and in museums.

The shopping district kept moving north. By the 1870s, the big stores had moved up to Ladies' Mile, near Union Square, and SoHo became a district of wholesalers and warehouses. It took a century for the shoppers to come back.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: A Venetian Palace in Iron | look
Stand across Broadway from the Haughwout Building and give it a slow look. It repays the time.

The architect was John Gaynor, and most historians credit the iron to Daniel Badger's Architectural Iron Works. Notice how one unit, an arched window framed by small columns and set between larger columns, repeats across the Broadway front and down the long Broome Street side, dozens of times, floor after floor. That repetition was the whole point of cast iron. Once the molds were made, every additional window was cheap.

In Venice, the library it imitates was carved from stone. Here, the iron was painted to look like stone, so that shoppers would see a palace, not a factory product.

Now find the big clock on the Broadway side. Stores of the time liked to put clocks on their buildings, as a public service and an advertisement at once. Look closely at how it writes the number four. Most clocks with Roman numerals use four strokes, I I I I. This one uses the proper Roman form, I V.

Then look at the windows themselves. The iron could carry the building's weight on slender supports, so most of the front is glass, letting daylight deep inside. That mattered in a store whose customers were examining fine china and crystal, decades before electric light.

The building had a hard twentieth century. The store moved out, the iron rusted, and some of the ornament was lost. It was one of the first buildings the city protected under its new landmarks law, in 1965, and in the early 1990s, its iron fronts were carefully restored.

And remember what's inside: the place where the first safe passenger elevator went into service, in 1857, the first step toward the skyscrapers uptown.
=== STOP The Little Singer Building & SoHo's Galleries
=== DEEPER Isaac Singer's Complicated Life | people
The Singer sewing machine was a model of reliability. The life of the man behind it was anything but.

Isaac Merritt Singer was born in upstate New York in 1811, left home as a boy, and spent years as a touring actor and a part-time machinist. In 1850, in a Boston machine shop, he was asked to look at a sewing machine that didn't work well. In about eleven days, he redesigned it, with a needle that moved straight up and down, an arm that held the needle over the cloth, and a foot to hold the fabric in place. He patented it in 1851.

The sewing machine business quickly became a war of patents, with Singer, Elias Howe and others suing each other. In 1856, they settled it in a new way: they pooled their patents and shared the fees, so that all of them could keep making machines. It's often called the first patent pool in American business.

Singer's private life was a scandal even by the standards of the time. He kept several families at once, with different women, sometimes under different names, and fathered about two dozen children. When the scandals became public, his partner Edward Clark, a proper lawyer, insisted that Singer step back from the company, and Singer moved to Europe. He built a mansion in Paignton, on the English coast, and died there in 1875, very rich.

Now a legend. Singer's young widow, Isabella Boyer, a striking Frenchwoman, is often said to have been the model for the face of the Statue of Liberty. The sculptor, Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, never said who posed for it, and many historians think the face is closer to his own mother, Charlotte. So the Isabella story is a nice one, but unproven.
=== DEEPER The Galleries and the Earth Room | culture
SoHo's great gallery era is mostly over, but two works of art from it are still here, and they're among the strangest and most wonderful things in New York.

The first gallery in SoHo was Paula Cooper's, which opened on Prince Street in 1968 with an exhibition that raised money for the movement against the Vietnam War. Leo Castelli followed in 1971, as you heard, and through the 1970s and 1980s, SoHo was where artists showed new work, from minimalism and conceptual art to the big paintings of the 1980s boom. On Saturday afternoons, West Broadway was crowded with collectors.

Now the survivors. At 141 Wooster Street, a short walk from here, climb to the second floor and look through the doorway at the New York Earth Room, a work by the artist Walter De Maria, installed in 1977. It's a large loft filled with dirt, twenty two inches deep, about two hundred fifty cubic yards of earth, and nothing else. The room smells like a forest after rain. The Dia Art Foundation has kept it for decades, raking and watering it, and lets visitors see it for free for much of the year.

At 393 West Broadway, you can see another De Maria work, The Broken Kilometer, from 1979: five hundred polished brass rods, laid in five rows across a gallery floor, which together would stretch for one kilometer.

There's something perfect about these works surviving in SoHo. They take up a great deal of very expensive space and do nothing but sit there, being beautiful. In one of the most valuable neighborhoods in the world, a room full of dirt is the ultimate luxury.
=== DEEPER Ernest Flagg's Glass Front | architecture
The Little Singer Building looks decades ahead of its time, and its architect, Ernest Flagg, was a man of strong ideas.

Flagg trained in Paris at the École des Beaux-Arts, the great school of classical architecture, and designed grand buildings in that style, like the Corcoran Gallery of Art in Washington and much of the Naval Academy in Annapolis. His brother-in-law was the publisher Charles Scribner, and Flagg designed the Scribner company's buildings on Fifth Avenue. But he was an engineer at heart, interested in fireproofing, ventilation and how to make cities healthier.

The Little Singer Building shows the engineer. Its front is a light frame of iron and steel, painted green, filled with sheets of glass, with delicate balconies and panels of terracotta. Instead of hiding the frame behind stone, Flagg made it the design.

He also thought about the city as a whole. As skyscrapers rose, he worried they were turning streets into dark canyons. He proposed that tall buildings be allowed to rise without limit only if their towers covered a small part of their lots, leaving light and air around them. His slender Singer Tower downtown, finished in 1908, was meant to show how. The city's first zoning law, in 1916, adopted a version of the idea, letting towers rise without limit over a quarter of the lot, and that rule helped shape the tower-topped skyscrapers of the 1920s and 1930s.

Flagg also designed small, fireproof stone houses for ordinary families, and built himself an estate on Todt Hill, on Staten Island. He died in 1947. Twenty one years later, his Singer Tower was torn down, and he was spared the sight.
