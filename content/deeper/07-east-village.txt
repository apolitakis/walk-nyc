=== DEEPER Right Makes Might | history
You heard that Lincoln's speech in the Great Hall helped make him president. Here is how it happened. The invitation was to speak at Henry Ward Beecher's church in Brooklyn. Only when Lincoln reached New York did he learn that a club of young Republicans had taken charge of the evening and moved it here, to Peter Cooper's brand new hall. His fee was two hundred dollars.

Lincoln knew that eastern audiences thought of him as a western stump speaker, a rail splitter with funny stories. So he told no stories at all. He had spent weeks in the state library in Springfield reading old congressional records, and he built the speech on one question: what did the men who wrote the Constitution believe about slavery in the western territories? Of the thirty-nine who signed it, he showed, twenty-three had later acted on that very question, in Congress or elsewhere, and twenty-one of them, a majority of all thirty-nine, had acted as if Congress had every right to keep slavery out. The founders, he argued, had treated slavery as an evil to be contained, and Republicans stood exactly where the founders stood.

About fifteen hundred people came. Some remembered the tall, awkward man in a badly wrinkled new suit, and a high voice that put them off at first, until the argument took hold. He ended with a line people still quote: let us have faith that right makes might, and in that faith, let us, to the end, dare to do our duty as we understand it.

The next morning, New York papers printed the whole speech. Lincoln went on to speak across New England, partly so he could visit his son Robert at school in New Hampshire. Less than three months later, the Republican Party nominated him for president.
=== DEEPER Voices in the Great Hall | politics
Lincoln was only the beginning. Before they were elected, Ulysses Grant, Grover Cleveland, William Howard Taft, Theodore Roosevelt and Barack Obama all spoke from this stage. So did Frederick Douglass, and the suffrage leaders Susan B. Anthony and Victoria Woodhull.

In 1867, a young humorist from the West named Mark Twain gave his first New York lecture here, about the Sandwich Islands, as Hawaii was then called. He wrote afterward that the night was a failure and a success at once. In 1870, the hall filled for Red Cloud, the Lakota leader who had forced the army to abandon its forts along the Bozeman Trail. Through an interpreter, he told New Yorkers that his people had been handed papers to sign that no one honestly explained, and asked the East to help them get fair treatment.

And on the night of November twenty-second, 1909, the hall was packed with young garment workers, most of them immigrant women from the tenements to the south. For hours they listened to union leaders, Samuel Gompers among them, debate whether to call a general strike of the shirtwaist makers. Then a twenty-three-year-old worker named Clara Lemlich, who had recently been beaten on a picket line, asked for the floor. Speaking in Yiddish, she said she had no more patience for talk, and moved that a general strike be declared now. The hall erupted. The chairman asked the crowd to raise their right hands and swear an old oath: if I turn traitor to the cause I now pledge, may this hand wither from the arm I now raise.

Within days, some twenty thousand workers walked out, in what became known as the Uprising of the Twenty Thousand. By February, nearly every shop had signed a union contract. One that held out was the Triangle Waist Company, on Washington Place. A year later, it burned.
=== DEEPER The Shakespeare Riot | culture
You heard that the Astor Place Riot began as a feud between two actors. It had been simmering for years. When the American star Edwin Forrest toured Britain in the 1840s, he was hissed in London and blamed his English rival, William Charles Macready. Soon after, Forrest stood up in a theater in Edinburgh and loudly hissed Macready's Hamlet. To his fans back home, Forrest was the voice of a rough young democracy, and Macready was the darling of the snobs.

The Astor Opera House, just west of here, was built for those snobs. It even had a dress code. So when Macready came to play Macbeth there in May 1849, the Bowery's working men took it personally. On the first night, the gallery pelted him with eggs and even chairs, and the play had to stop. Macready meant to sail home. But forty-seven prominent New Yorkers, Washington Irving and the young Herman Melville among them, signed an open letter begging him to stay, and promising that order would be kept.

The letter only made things worse. Handbills went up around the city asking: working men, shall Americans or English rule in this city? On May tenth, while Macready finished Macbeth under police guard, thousands outside hurled paving stones at the building. The Seventh Regiment was called out, and when the stones kept coming, the soldiers fired into the crowd. At least twenty-two people died. Macready slipped out in disguise and left the city.

One of the men convicted of stirring up the mob was a writer named Edward Judson, better known as Ned Buntline. He served a year on Blackwell's Island. Twenty years later he found a better use for his gift for spectacle: he made a young army scout named William Cody the hero of his dime novels, and turned him into Buffalo Bill.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Columns and Beavers | look
Two details nearby are easy to walk past.

First, find the row of tall marble columns on the west side of Lafayette Street, facing the Public Theater. This is Colonnade Row, four survivors of a terrace of nine grand houses finished around 1833 and named La Grange Terrace, after the Marquis de Lafayette's country estate in France. Behind one continuous screen of Corinthian columns, each family had its own house, so the whole row read as a single palace. Members of the Astor family lived here, and so did Warren Delano, grandfather of Franklin Delano Roosevelt. The marble is said to have been cut by prisoners at Sing Sing. The other five houses were torn down long ago, and the survivors are grimy and patched, but squint and you can see one of the grandest addresses in the New York of Andrew Jackson's day.

Second, go down into the Astor Place subway station and look along the platform walls. Every so often you'll find a glazed terra cotta plaque of a beaver. The station opened in 1904 on the city's first subway line, and its designers chose the beaver as a nod to the fur trade that made John Jacob Astor rich. More than a century later, the beavers are still there.

When you come back up, look at the little cast iron and glass pavilion over the uptown entrance. It looks like 1904, but it's a replica, built in 1985 from old photographs of the kiosks that once stood over subway stairs all over the city.

=== DEEPER Sunday Nights on the Bowery | people
The full name on Hilly Kristal's awning was longer than CBGB. It read C B G B and O M F U G, for Country, Bluegrass, Blues and Other Music For Uplifting Gormandizers, a gormandizer being someone with a large appetite.

The other music arrived in the spring of 1974. A young band called Television, with the guitarists Tom Verlaine and Richard Lloyd, needed somewhere to play, and their manager, Terry Ork, kept pushing Kristal to let them in. Kristal finally gave them Sunday nights, his slowest of the week. By the band's own account, their first Sunday was March thirty-first, 1974. They brought their friends, the friends brought theirs, and the narrow room found its purpose. That August, four young men from Queens in leather jackets and torn jeans, all calling themselves Ramone, played there for the first time.

Kristal ran the club for more than thirty years, through punk and the hardcore years that followed, until a long fight with the landlord closed it. The last show, on October fifteenth, 2006, belonged to Patti Smith. She played for more than three hours and ended with her song Elegie, reading out the names of musicians and friends who had died. Kristal himself died the next summer.

Before you leave, walk a few steps north to the corner of East Second Street and look up, way up. The street sign there reads Joey Ramone Place, named in 2003 for the band's singer, who had died two years earlier. It became the most stolen street sign in New York, so in 2010 the city raised it to about twenty feet above the sidewalk. Marky Ramone, the band's drummer, joked that now you'd have to be a basketball player to see it.
=== DEEPER Monk at the Five Spot | culture
Before punk, the Bowery had jazz. Just to the north, at number 5 Cooper Square, stood a plain workingman's bar called the Five Spot Café, run by the Termini brothers, Joe and Iggy. In the mid-1950s they started booking jazz, and painters and poets from the cheap lofts nearby made it their hangout.

In the summer of 1957, Thelonious Monk began a run there that lasted about six months. Monk had spent years barely able to work in New York, after the police took away the cabaret card that nightclub performers then needed. Now he had a steady stage, and a new tenor saxophonist, John Coltrane, who had just kicked heroin. Night after night, Coltrane worked his way through Monk's strange, angular tunes, and other musicians came to listen. Coltrane said later that if you lost track of where Monk was going, you'd feel as if you'd stepped into an empty elevator shaft.

In November 1959, the Five Spot introduced New York to Ornette Coleman, whose quartet played without any set pattern of chords. Critics and musicians packed the room to argue over whether it was genius or noise. That same year, the poet Frank O'Hara wrote The Day Lady Died on the day Billie Holiday died. The poem ends here, with the memory of a night at the Five Spot when she whispered a song along the keyboard to her pianist, Mal Waldron, and everyone, O'Hara included, stopped breathing.

In the early 1960s the club moved a few blocks north to St. Mark's Place, and it closed later that decade. The original building is gone, but for a few years this was one of the most important rooms in jazz.
=== DEEPER Four Women on the Fourth Floor | history
The Tredwells were not alone in their house on Fourth Street. Climb to the top floor of the museum and you reach two small rooms under the roof where the family's servants slept. The state census of 1855 lists four of them, all women, all born in Ireland.

They had come in the years of the Great Famine, when about a million Irish people died and more than a million left. By the 1850s, roughly one New Yorker in four had been born in Ireland, and for young Irish women the most common work was domestic service: cooking, carrying coal and water up and down the stairs, scrubbing, washing and sewing. A bell at the top of the stairs could summon them at any hour. The museum considers these rooms the oldest intact place in New York City where Irish immigrants are known to have lived.

The house itself survived on a string of near misses. By the 1960s it was crumbling, and the blocks around it had filled with factories and warehouses. When New York passed its landmarks law in 1965, after the outcry over the demolition of the old Pennsylvania Station, this house was among the first buildings protected, and the very first in Manhattan. In 1981 its interior became a landmark too, one of a small number of interiors in the city with that protection. In recent years, its defenders have fought construction next door that they fear could crack its old walls.

=== DEEPER The Woman Who Owned a Men's Bar | people
McSorley's founder, John McSorley, was an Irish immigrant remembered as Old John. After he died, his son Bill kept the saloon almost exactly as his father had left it, which is one reason the walls look the way they do. The painter John Sloan drank here and painted the bar several times, starting in 1912. The poet E. E. Cummings wrote a poem that opens with him sitting in McSorley's while it snows outside. And in 1940, Joseph Mitchell wrote a long, loving portrait of the place for The New Yorker, called The Old House at Home, after the name the saloon carried in its early days.

In 1936, Bill McSorley sold the bar to one of his regulars, a retired policeman named Daniel O'Connell. Within a few years O'Connell died and left it to his daughter, Dorothy O'Connell Kirwan. So for decades, the most famous men-only bar in New York belonged to a woman.

She had promised her father that she would not disturb the customers, and as the story is told, she kept her word, coming in only on Sundays, when the bar was closed, or stopping at the door to raise a glass when a round was on the house. The bartenders ran the place, the old rules held, and the sawdust stayed on the floor.

When women were finally let in, in 1970, the walls did not change, and neither did the beer. But remember, as you look around, that for decades the keeper of New York's most famous men's bar was a woman, honoring a promise to stay outside it.
=== DEEPER Lincoln's Ale and Houdini's Handcuffs | legends
A bar this old collects legends the way its walls collect dust, and McSorley's is happy to share them.

The biggest is that Abraham Lincoln stopped in for an ale after his speech at Cooper Union, a few blocks away, in February 1860. It makes a good story, but there is no record that he ever set foot inside, and Lincoln barely drank at all.

Then there are the handcuffs. Look for a pair fastened to the bar, and another hanging higher up. Harry Houdini, the story goes, was a regular around the turn of the twentieth century, and left a pair of his cuffs behind. Which pair, if either, is a matter of barroom debate, since one has been found to carry a serial number from after Houdini's death. Some regulars add that if you see a cat sitting in the window, it is Houdini, back for a visit.

You heard that the wishbones over the bar were hung by young men leaving for the First World War. That too is the bar's own story, passed down rather than written down. What is certain is that each generation of owners has left them where they hung.

Legends like these do a job. They tie a working man's saloon to the great names of the city, and they give every regular something to tell a newcomer. Listen at the bar long enough and someone will tell you one of them as gospel.
=== DEEPER Taras Shevchenko Place | names
Look for the short lane beside St. George's church, running between Sixth and Seventh Streets. Its sign reads Taras Shevchenko Place. Until 1978 it was Hall Place, a forgettable alley. That spring, as the parish dedicated its new domed church, Mayor Ed Koch signed a law renaming the lane for the man Ukrainians consider their national poet.

Taras Shevchenko was born in 1814 a serf, the property of a landowner in central Ukraine. He had a gift for drawing, and his owner took him to Saint Petersburg, where artists noticed him. In 1838 they bought his freedom, raising the money by raffling off a portrait painted by Karl Bryullov. Two years later Shevchenko published a book of poems called Kobzar, named for the wandering minstrels of the Ukrainian countryside. Writing in the language of peasants, which the Russian Empire treated as a mere dialect, he helped turn it into a literary language.

His poems mourned his country's bondage, and the czar's government punished him for them. In 1847 he was sent into exile as a common soldier on the edge of the Kazakh steppe, and forbidden to write or paint. He kept writing anyway, in tiny notebooks he hid in the tops of his boots. He was freed after ten years, and died in 1861, just as the czar was proclaiming the end of serfdom.

Ukrainian exiles, including the ones who rebuilt this neighborhood after the Second World War, carried his portrait and his poems with them. Look for his face in Ukrainian shops and churches. You'll also find him on monuments from Kyiv to Washington.
=== STOP St. Mark's Place
=== DEEPER Number 77 | people
Walk east toward Avenue A and look for number 77. Two very different men passed through its doors.

In the winter of 1917, its basement held the offices of Novy Mir, The New World, a Russian-language socialist paper for the city's immigrant radicals. One of its editors was Nikolai Bukharin, later a leader of Soviet Russia. That January a new writer joined them, just deported from France and Spain: Leon Trotsky. Trotsky lived with his family in the Bronx, but he spent his days here, writing fierce articles against the First World War and speaking in halls around the neighborhood. In March came the news that the czar had fallen. Within weeks Trotsky sailed for Russia, and by the end of the year he and Bukharin were helping lead a revolution.

Thirty-six years later, the poet W. H. Auden moved into an apartment upstairs. A plaque by the door says he lived here from 1953 to 1972. By then Auden was one of the most celebrated poets in the English language, and friends remembered his apartment as cold, cluttered and run down, with books and papers everywhere and pipes that froze in winter. He kept a famously strict schedule, working through the morning and serving cocktails at a set hour. He stayed through the arrival of the hippies, and watched the street change around him for almost twenty years. He left New York in 1972, and died the following year.

So the same doorway saw a revolutionary planning to overturn an empire, and a poet who wrote some of the century's most famous lines about love, grief and the city. St. Mark's Place has always had room for both.

Two buildings on this street hold its whole history in their walls.

First, between Second and Third Avenues, find number 12, with its ornate front. High on the facade, in raised letters, is its original German name, which translates as the German-American Shooting Society. === DEEPER Who Invented the Egg Cream? | food
You heard that an egg cream contains neither egg nor cream. So where did the name come from? Nobody knows, and New Yorkers have argued about it for generations.

The most famous story involves a candy store owner named Louis Auster, who sold egg creams at his shop near Second Avenue and Seventh Street, a block from here, around the turn of the twentieth century. His family says he invented the drink, made his own chocolate syrup in secret, and turned down a fortune from a big ice cream chain that wanted the formula. The last batch of that syrup, they say, was made in the 1970s. The sociologist Daniel Bell credited someone else, a candy store man known as Uncle Hymie, on Second Avenue near Eighth Street. Brooklyn has its own claimants.

As for the name, take your pick. Some say egg comes from the Yiddish word echt, meaning genuine, so an egg cream was the real thing. Some say customers asked for a chocolate A cream, made with grade A milk, and the A became egg. Others think the name simply made a cheap drink sound rich. The dullest explanation may be the right one. Dictionary researchers have found nineteenth-century drinks called egg creams that really did contain eggs and cream, and the name may have stuck after the ingredients changed.

What is not in dispute is the method: a tall glass, a little cold milk, a hard blast of seltzer to raise a foam, and chocolate syrup stirred in. The classic syrup is Fox's U-bet, made in Brooklyn. Order one, and you are drinking an argument that is more than a century old.

=== DEEPER The Merchant Prince's Missing Bones | legends
In the spring of 1876, one of the richest men in America, Alexander Turney Stewart, was laid in his family's vault in this churchyard. Stewart was an Irish immigrant who had built a dry goods shop into the first great American department store, first the Marble Palace on Broadway at Chambers Street, then the even bigger Iron Palace, a few blocks west of here on Broadway at Tenth Street.

On the morning of November seventh, 1878, the church's sexton noticed fresh earth over the Stewart vault. Someone had dug down in the night, broken open the coffin and carried off the body.

The theft was real, and it made headlines around the world. Letters arrived demanding a ransom, at one point reported as a quarter of a million dollars, and the executor of the estate, Judge Henry Hilton, refused to pay. Detectives chased false leads for years. What came next is the part that has never been proved. According to the story told ever since, Stewart's widow, Cornelia, quietly made her own deal and paid about twenty thousand dollars, and one night, on a lonely country road, a bag of bones was handed over. The remains were said to have been placed in the Cathedral of the Incarnation in Garden City, on Long Island, the planned town Stewart had founded, where his widow built the cathedral in his memory.

No one has ever shown that the bones were really his, or even that the exchange took place. No one was convicted of the theft. Somewhere, perhaps, Alexander Stewart is still missing.
=== DEEPER Stuyvesant's Pear Tree | nature
Walk over to Third Avenue and up to Thirteenth Street, and look for a small tablet on the northeast corner. It marks the spot where, for more than two centuries, a single pear tree grew.

According to tradition, Peter Stuyvesant brought the tree from Holland in 1647, the year he arrived to take charge of New Amsterdam, and planted it on his farm. The farm became the city, but the tree stayed. As the street grid crept north and Third Avenue was cut through, the old pear tree survived at the corner, and New Yorkers came to see it as a living link to the Dutch colony. It was said to bear fruit almost to the end.

The end came in 1867. A section of its trunk was saved, and it is now in the collection of the New-York Historical Society.

In 1890, the Holland Society, whose members descend from the colony's early Dutch settlers, put up a bronze tablet that reads: On this corner grew Petrus Stuyvesant's pear tree. The tablet later went missing from the corner for decades, and was put back in 2005. A couple of years before that, a new pear tree was planted at the corner in memory of the old one.

It is a small thing to look for, but think of what it means. A tree planted by the last Dutch governor of New Amsterdam was still putting out blossoms every spring while horse cars rolled down Third Avenue and soldiers came home from the Civil War. For more than half of New York's history, you could have picked a pear from Peter Stuyvesant's garden.

=== STOP The Yiddish Rialto
=== DEEPER The Jewish King Lear | people
The Yiddish stage made its own royalty, and the greatest of them was Jacob Adler. Born in Odessa, he reached New York after years of touring, tall and commanding, and his fans called him the Great Eagle, since adler means eagle.

In 1892, a Russian-born writer named Jacob Gordin wrote a play for him called The Jewish King Lear. Gordin moved Shakespeare's story to a prosperous Jewish family in nineteenth-century Russia, borrowing too from Turgenev's King Lear of the Steppes: a proud father divides his fortune among his daughters and is cast out by those he trusted. Adler's performance was a sensation, and the play became his signature, revived again and again. It also marked a turning point. Yiddish theater had leaned on improvised clowning and melodrama, and Gordin insisted that actors speak the lines as written and play real people. Some historians date the golden age of Yiddish theater in New York from that production.

Stars like Adler and his rival Boris Thomashefsky, whose grandson is the conductor Michael Tilson Thomas, had fans so devoted that they formed clubs, called patriots, to cheer their favorite and jeer the competition.

A stroke ended Adler's career in 1920, and he died in 1926. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported that fifty thousand people attended his funeral. His children carried the name on. Luther Adler acted on Broadway and in Hollywood, and Jacob's daughter Stella became the acting teacher you heard about, who passed the passion of the Yiddish stage on to generations of American actors.
=== DEEPER The Thirty-Dollar Song | culture
In 1932, the composer Sholom Secunda and the lyricist Jacob Jacobs wrote a love song for a Yiddish musical comedy called I Would If I Could. The show is long forgotten, but one song from it is not: Bei Mir Bistu Shein, which means, roughly, to me you're beautiful.

Secunda, a trained composer who wrote for the Second Avenue stage for decades, sold the rights to a small Brooklyn publisher of Yiddish music for thirty dollars, and split the money with Jacobs. For a song from a Yiddish operetta, it seemed a fair price.

Then the song escaped from Second Avenue. The songwriter Sammy Cahn told the story that he first heard it at the Apollo Theater in Harlem, sung in Yiddish by two Black performers, Johnnie and George, to an audience that went wild for it. Others claimed they had found it first. Either way, Cahn and Saul Chaplin wrote English lyrics, and in November 1937 three young sisters from Minnesota, the Andrews Sisters, recorded it, with the title spelled the German way. It became the most popular song in America the following year, and launched their career. The money went to the publishers and the record companies, not to the men who wrote it.

In 1961 the copyright ran out and the rights came back to Secunda and Jacobs, who at last earned something from their song. By then, millions of people who had never heard of the Yiddish Rialto had sung along to a tune born a few blocks from here.
=== DEEPER The Yiddish Algonquin | food
After the curtain came down on Second Avenue, the actors went out to eat, and for more than forty years the place to go was the Café Royal, at the corner of Second Avenue and Twelfth Street.

From 1908 until 1952, the Royal was the living room of the Yiddish world. Actors, playwrights, poets, critics and producers argued over coffee and cookies, made deals, settled feuds and started new ones. The journalist and poet Judd Teller called it the Yiddish writers' Algonquin and the Yiddish actors' Sardi's. The playwright Hy Kraft based a 1942 Broadway comedy, Café Crown, on it.

Two years after the Royal closed, the Second Avenue Deli opened a couple of blocks south, at Tenth Street, and took up its role. Its founder, Abe Lebewohl, an immigrant who arrived after the Second World War, built it into an institution famous for pastrami, chopped liver and matzo ball soup. He loved the Yiddish theater, and in the 1980s he set the stars of the Yiddish Walk of Fame into the sidewalk outside his door.

On March fourth, 1996, Lebewohl was shot and killed while taking the day's receipts to the bank. The crime has never been solved. His family kept the deli going, and when it lost its lease, they moved it uptown. The stars stayed behind in the Second Avenue sidewalk, worn down by the shoes of everyone who has walked over them since.
=== STOP Tompkins Square Park & the General Slocum
=== DEEPER The Island at the End of the Voyage | history
You heard that the General Slocum's captain kept steaming forward instead of turning for shore. He was heading for North Brother Island, a small island in the East River off the Bronx.

The island held Riverside Hospital, the city's hospital for contagious diseases, and as the burning boat bore down on it, the hospital's staff ran to the water. Nurses, doctors, orderlies and even patients waded in and formed human chains, pulling the living and the dead from the river. The boat finally grounded about twenty-five feet from the island's shore.

A few years later, the island received its most famous patient. Mary Mallon was an Irish-born cook and a healthy carrier of typhoid fever, who had passed the disease to the families she cooked for. In 1907 she was taken to North Brother Island and held there against her will. She was released in 1910 on the promise that she would never cook again, broke it, and in 1915 was sent back. She spent the rest of her life on the island, and died there in 1938. The newspapers called her Typhoid Mary.

The hospital closed in the 1940s. The island later housed veterans and then a drug treatment program for teenagers, and by the 1960s it was abandoned. Its buildings are slowly collapsing into the trees, and the public is not allowed to land. Today it is a bird sanctuary, and for some years it held one of the largest colonies of nesting herons in the city.
=== DEEPER No Ordinary Criminal | politics
After the General Slocum burned, New York wanted someone to blame, and there was plenty of blame to go around. The boat had passed a federal inspection only weeks before. Investigators found its cork life preservers so old that they crumbled to dust, and they found that the company making new ones had hidden iron bars inside some of them to bring them up to the legal weight. The cork company's managers were indicted, and so were the steamboat company's officers and the inspectors. None of them was convicted.

In the end, only one man went to prison: the captain, William Van Schaick. In 1906 a federal jury found him guilty of criminal negligence, for failing to train his crew and keep the boat ready for fire. The judge told him, you are no ordinary criminal, I must make an example of you, and gave him the maximum sentence, ten years. He served about three and a half years in Sing Sing. President Theodore Roosevelt refused to pardon him, but in December 1912, President William Howard Taft did.

The disaster did change the rules. A federal commission blamed careless inspection as well as the owners, and the government tightened its requirements for life preservers, lifeboats and fire drills on passenger steamboats.

Many of the victims were buried together at the Lutheran cemetery in Middle Village, in Queens, where a monument honors the dead who were never identified. And the fountain here, with its two children gazing out to sea, was put up two years after the fire, a place for a neighborhood that had lost so many to remember them.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Hebe's Fountain | look
Look for a little temple of four columns sheltering a drinking fountain. Above the columns, read the four words carved on its sides: Faith, Hope, Charity and Temperance. This is the Temperance Fountain, put up in 1888, and it was meant to save souls one sip at a time.

It was the gift of Henry Cogswell, a San Francisco dentist who made his fortune in the Gold Rush years and spent it on drinking fountains. His idea was simple: if a man could get a free drink of cold water on the street, he might walk past the saloon. He once pledged to build one fountain for every hundred saloons in San Francisco. Here, he worked with a local group called the Moderation Society, formed to fight drunkenness on the Lower East Side.

On top stands Hebe, the cupbearer of the Greek gods, modeled on a famous marble by the Danish sculptor Bertel Thorvaldsen. The original figure was zinc. By the 1980s the fountain was covered in graffiti and someone had sawed off Hebe's hands, and in 1992 she was replaced with the bronze you see now.

Not everyone loved Cogswell's gifts. Brooklyn tore out the fountain he gave it. In San Francisco, on New Year's 1894, a band of young men who considered his statue of himself an eyesore threw a rope around it and pulled it down. One of them was Gelett Burgess, soon to be famous for a little poem that begins, I never saw a purple cow.
=== DEEPER The Name on the Park | names
The park is named for Daniel D. Tompkins, and he deserves to be better remembered.

Tompkins was elected governor of New York in 1807, while still in his early thirties. During the War of 1812, when the federal government was slow to pay, he borrowed money on his own name to raise and pay the militia that defended New York harbor. After the war, neither the state legislature nor Congress would fully repay him, and he spent years fighting accusations over his accounts.

He is also the governor who finished off slavery in New York. A law passed in 1799 had promised freedom only to children born after that year, and only once they were grown. In 1817, at Tompkins's urging, the legislature set a final date: on July fourth, 1827, slavery in New York would end. When the day came, many Black New Yorkers held their celebrations on July fifth instead, to avoid trouble from white crowds on the holiday.

That same year, 1817, Tompkins became vice president under James Monroe, and served two terms. By the end his health and his fortune were both ruined. He died in 1825, three months after leaving office, in the Staten Island village he had founded and named Tompkinsville. He was fifty years old.

He is buried in the churchyard of St. Mark's in-the-Bowery, a few blocks from here. The square was named for him in the decade after his death, and it has spent nearly two centuries living up to his restless, embattled name.
=== STOP Loisaida & the Nuyorican Poets Cafe
=== DEEPER The Poet Who Named Loisaida | names
Start with the map. Manhattan bulges out to the east here, so when the planners laid out the street grid in 1811, they added four avenues east of First Avenue and gave them letters, A, B, C and D. That is where the nickname Alphabet City comes from.

The name Loisaida came later, and it came from a poet. Bittman Rivas, known to everyone as Bimbo, came to New York from Puerto Rico and made his life here as a poet, playwright, actor, teacher and community organizer. In 1974 he wrote a poem called Loisaida, in a mix of English and Spanish, about the neighborhood he loved. The word took the English name, Lower East Side, and spelled it the way his Puerto Rican neighbors said it. Some people give a share of the credit to his friend and fellow organizer, Chino Garcia.

The word spread because it named something real. In the 1970s, as landlords abandoned buildings and the city cut services, residents began rebuilding the neighborhood themselves, with sweat equity, community gardens and cultural centers, and Loisaida became their banner. It went up on murals and posters, and into the names of community groups.

Eventually it reached the street signs, when Avenue C was given the second name Loisaida Avenue. Bimbo Rivas died in 1992. Every year, the Loisaida Festival fills the avenue with music, food and dancing, and the word he wrote down in a poem is still how many people here name their home.
=== DEEPER Domes on the Empty Lots | architecture
In the 1960s, two young men who had come up through the neighborhood's street gangs, Carlos Chino Garcia and Angelo Gonzalez, helped start an antipoverty group with a name that borrowed from President Johnson's programs: the Real Great Society. In 1968 the group hosted a lecture by Buckminster Fuller, the inventor famous for the geodesic dome, a shelter built from a lattice of triangles that is light, cheap and astonishingly strong. Some of the young men in the audience decided to build domes themselves.

In 1970, six of them formed a new group and named it with the first letters of their first names: Chino, Humberto, Angelo, Roy, Anthony and Sal. That spells CHARAS. Fuller's associate, Michael Ben-Eli, came down to teach them solid geometry and spherical trigonometry. They worked out the math, cut and joined the struts, and raised their first dome on a vacant lot beneath the Manhattan Bridge. A photographer named Syeus Mottel followed them for months, and his 1973 book, CHARAS: The Improbable Dome Builders, carried an introduction by Fuller himself.

In 1979 CHARAS leased an abandoned school, the old Public School 64 on East Ninth Street between Avenues B and C, and turned it into El Bohío, named for the thatched huts of the Puerto Rican countryside. For two decades it held a theater, studios, classes and meeting rooms for the neighborhood. Then, in 1998, the city auctioned the building to a developer who had given money to Mayor Giuliani's campaigns, and at the end of 2001 CHARAS was evicted. The empty school, its murals painted over, became a symbol of everything the neighborhood felt it had lost, and the fight over its future went on for years.
=== DEEPER Scatter My Ashes | people
Miguel Piñero, one of the founders of the Nuyorican Poets Cafe, lived the life he wrote about. He was born in Puerto Rico in 1946 and brought to the Lower East Side as a small child. He grew up poor, ran with gangs, used heroin, and was in and out of jail. In the early 1970s, serving time at Sing Sing for armed robbery, he joined a prison theater workshop, and there he wrote Short Eyes, a raw drama set in a house of detention, about what happens when a man accused of molesting a child is thrown in among the other inmates.

The play opened in a church, moved to the Public Theater with Joseph Papp's help, and then to Lincoln Center. In 1974 it won the New York Drama Critics' Circle Award for best American play. Piñero was suddenly famous. He wrote for television, acted in films and in shows like Miami Vice, usually as a criminal, and kept writing poems. He never escaped his addictions.

In one poem he asked for one last thing: after he had climbed up on a tenement sky, as he put it, he wanted his ashes scattered through the Lower East Side. He died in June 1988, at forty-one. His friend Miguel Algarín led a procession through the neighborhood, and they did as he had asked, scattering his ashes along the streets he had written about.

Another poet of the Cafe's early days, Pedro Pietri, wrote Puerto Rican Obituary, a long poem about workers who spent their lives chasing a dream that never arrived. In 2001, Benjamin Bratt played Piñero in a film about his life. But the Cafe itself, still filling up on poetry nights, may be the memorial Piñero would have liked best.
