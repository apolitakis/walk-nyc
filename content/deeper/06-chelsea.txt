=== DEEPER Melville on the Waterfront | people
You heard that Herman Melville, the author of Moby-Dick, spent nearly twenty years as a customs inspector on these docks. Here's how one of America's great novelists ended up checking cargo.

Melville was born in New York in 1819, the grandson of two heroes of the Revolution. When he was a boy, his father's business failed and his father died, and the family fell on hard times. As a young man, Herman went to sea. He sailed on a whaling ship to the South Pacific, deserted it, and lived for a time among the islanders of the Marquesas. His first book, Typee, based on those adventures, was a hit in 1846, and for a few years he was a celebrity.

Then his books grew stranger and more ambitious, and readers drifted away. Moby-Dick, published in 1851, sold poorly and puzzled many reviewers. By the 1860s, Melville was in debt and needed a steady income.

In December 1866, he was appointed an inspector of customs for the port of New York, at four dollars a day. For the next nineteen years, he walked the wharves along the Hudson, checking cargo and paperwork, through summer heat and winter wind.

He never stopped writing. In his off hours he wrote poetry, including a book-length poem called Clarel, about a pilgrimage to the Holy Land. After he retired, he began a short novel about a young sailor, Billy Budd. When he died in 1891, he was nearly forgotten. Billy Budd sat unpublished among his papers until 1924, when a new generation of critics rediscovered him, and his reputation soared.

So as you walk toward the river, picture a gray-bearded inspector with a notebook, checking crates on a pier at the end of a street named for his own grandfather, with no idea that his whale book would one day be called the great American novel.
=== DEEPER The White Fort | names
Gansevoort Street is one of the oldest names on this part of the waterfront, and it began with a fort.

In 1812, as war with Britain loomed again, a small fort was finished on the riverbank here, between what are now Gansevoort and West Twelfth Streets. It was built of red sandstone, with openings for twenty two cannon, but its walls were whitewashed, so New Yorkers called it the White Fort. Officially, it was named Fort Gansevoort, after Peter Gansevoort, a hero of the Revolution, who died that same year.

Gansevoort's fame came from the summer of 1777. As a young colonel, he commanded Fort Stanwix, in the Mohawk Valley of upstate New York, when a British force and its Native American allies laid siege to it. Gansevoort refused to surrender. After about three weeks, the British gave up and withdrew, a blow to the British campaign that ended in defeat at Saratoga that fall.

A legend grew up around the siege: that the defenders stitched together an American flag from scraps, including soldiers' shirts and a woman's red petticoat, and that it was the first Stars and Stripes ever flown in battle. The story is told with pride in upstate New York, but historians can't confirm what that flag looked like, or whether it was the Stars and Stripes at all.

The White Fort here never fired a shot in anger. The British never attacked Manhattan during the War of 1812, and by the 1850s the fort was gone, swallowed by the growing waterfront.

All that remains is the name: on the street, on the old market, and on the whole district, which for a long time was simply called Gansevoort. And through Peter Gansevoort's grandson, Herman Melville, the name reaches into American literature too.
=== DEEPER Florent and the Night Market | culture
For more than twenty years, the most famous place to eat on these cobblestones was a diner that never closed.

In 1985, a Frenchman named Florent Morellet took over an old luncheonette on Gansevoort Street called the R and L, which had been feeding meat workers since 1943. He kept the counter and the booths, renamed it Florent, and stayed open twenty four hours a day. At the time, the neighborhood was rough. By day, butchers in bloody white coats pushed carcasses along the overhead rails. So as you walk past the boutiques, imagine a lit diner window at four in the morning, a butcher and a drag queen sharing the counter, and a menu board that told the truth.

It's a sign of the times. This waterfront was built to bring cargo in from the river. Now its newest buildings are designed to keep the river out.
=== DEEPER The Gardens on the Tracks | nature
Look at the plants along the High Line. They look wild, as if they'd seeded themselves. That's the idea.

When the trains stopped in 1980, the viaduct became an accidental meadow. Seeds blew in on the wind or arrived with birds, and tough plants took root in the gravel between the rails: grasses, wildflowers, even small trees. You heard that photographs of that wild landscape helped win support for saving the line.

When the park was designed, the Dutch garden designer Piet Oudolf set out to capture that feeling, not by copying it, but by composing it. He is a leader of a style sometimes called the New Perennial movement, which uses grasses and long-lived flowering plants arranged as if in a natural meadow, and values how plants look in every season, not just when they bloom. On the High Line, he used hundreds of kinds of plants, many of them native to North America, in a series of zones suited to the changing sun, shade and wind along the line.

Watch how the gardens change through the year. In spring come bulbs and fresh green shoots. In summer, the flowers and grasses are at their height, buzzing with bees. In fall, the grasses turn gold and copper. And in winter, instead of cutting everything down, the gardeners leave the dried stems and seed heads standing, where they catch the frost and feed the birds.

Then, each year in late March, gardeners and volunteers do what's called the spring cutback, clearing away the old growth so the new season can come through. Even then, they leave some stems standing, because native bees and other pollinators nest inside them.

So the High Line's gardens are a kind of performance: a designed wilderness, tended every day to look as if no one tends it at all.

You heard that the Carpathia brought the Titanic's survivors here. Its part in the story began with a lucky moment.

Back in his cabin, getting ready for bed, he listened in one more time, and heard them. He ran to wake his captain, Arthur Rostron.

The Carpathia was about fifty eight miles away. Rostron turned the ship around and drove it at top speed through waters full of ice, with extra lookouts posted, while the crew prepared blankets, hot drinks and the dining rooms for survivors. The Carpathia reached the scene in the dark before dawn, after the Titanic had gone down, and spent hours picking up survivors from the lifeboats.

Then came the slow trip back to New York, with the world desperate for news. On the evening of April 18th, in a cold rain, the Carpathia steamed up the Hudson. It didn't go straight to its own pier. Survivors came down the gangways into a crush of relatives and reporters. Some were met by family. Many had become widows that week. Relief groups were waiting to help the many who had lost everything.

Captain Rostron became a hero. Congress awarded him a gold medal, and a group of survivors, led by Margaret Brown, the woman later remembered as the Unsinkable Molly Brown, presented him with a silver cup. So the arch here also remembers a ship that turned toward the ice when another was in trouble.
=== DEEPER The Lusitania's Last Sailing | politics
You heard that the Lusitania sailed from this pier in May 1915, and was sunk by a German submarine. The warning came in the morning newspapers.

On Saturday, May 1st, the day the ship was to sail, a notice from the German Embassy appeared in New York papers. It reminded travelers that Germany and Britain were at war, that the waters around the British Isles were a war zone, and that anyone sailing there on British ships did so at their own risk. In some papers, it was printed right next to Cunard's advertisement for the Lusitania's sailing.

Some passengers were alarmed, and a few canceled. But most shrugged it off. The Lusitania was one of the fastest ships on the ocean, and many people believed no submarine could catch her. The ship left the pier a little after noon, delayed by the transfer of passengers from another ship.

Six days later, on May 7th, off the southern coast of Ireland, a single torpedo struck. The great liner sank in about eighteen minutes.

The sinking caused fury in the United States, but not war, at least not yet. Three days later, President Woodrow Wilson gave a speech in Philadelphia in which he said that there is such a thing as a man being too proud to fight. Critics mocked the line. Wilson sent Germany a series of stern protests, and his Secretary of State, William Jennings Bryan, resigned rather than sign one of them, fearing it would drag the country into war.

The United States didn't enter the war until April 1917, nearly two years later, after Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare. But the Lusitania became a rallying cry, used on recruiting posters and in speeches for the rest of the war.

So this pier saw off one of the turning points of the century, on a spring day, with a warning in the newspaper that almost everyone ignored.
=== DEEPER Westway and the Striped Bass | nature
The park you're standing in exists partly because of a fish.

By the 1970s, the old elevated West Side Highway along the river was falling apart. In 1973, a section of it collapsed near Gansevoort Street under the weight of a truck. The piers along the shore were rotting. Planners proposed a grand replacement called Westway: a highway buried in a tunnel inside new land, made by filling in the Hudson along this shore, with parks, apartments and offices built on top.

Westway had powerful supporters, and well over a billion dollars of federal highway money behind it. But opponents argued that the money would be better spent on subways and buses, and environmental groups went to court. Their unlikely champion was the striped bass. Young striped bass spend the winter in the sheltered water among the old piers, and filling in the river would destroy that refuge.

In 1982, a federal judge, Thomas Griesa, blocked the project, ruling that government agencies had played down the harm to the fish. The agencies tried again, and in August 1985 Griesa ruled against them a second time, finding that testimony they had given was tainted. The next month, facing a deadline, New York gave up on Westway and traded the federal highway money for transit and local roads.

Instead of a buried highway, the West Side eventually got a surface boulevard, and the river's edge got a park. In 1998, the state created Hudson River Park, which runs along the shore from Battery Park City up to Fifty Ninth Street, and includes the water around the old piers, where fish and other river life are protected.

So when you see the rows of old wooden pilings sticking out of the water, don't think of them only as ruins. For the young striped bass, they're shelter, and they helped save this shoreline.

=== DEEPER The Oreo's Mysteries | legends
The Oreo became the best-selling cookie in the world, and almost everything about its beginnings comes with a question mark.

Start with the fact that it wasn't first. In 1908, four years earlier, a rival company, Sunshine Biscuits, had introduced Hydrox: two chocolate cookies with a cream filling. When the Oreo arrived in 1912, it was, in effect, an imitation. But Nabisco had bigger bakeries and a bigger advertising budget, and over the decades the Oreo buried its rival. Today, many people assume Hydrox was the copy.

The first Oreos were sold on March 6th, 1912, to a grocer in Hoboken, New Jersey, just across the river.

Then there's the name. You heard that nobody knows where it came from. The theories are fun. One says it comes from or, the French word for gold, because the early packages were gold colored. Another points to a Greek word for mountain, because an early test version was shaped like a little hill. Another notices the letters r and e, from the word cream, sandwiched between two O's, like the filling between two cookies. Nabisco has never settled the question.

And then there's the design. The pattern stamped on today's Oreo dates to 1952, and is generally credited to a Nabisco employee named William Turnier, who is said to have started in the company mailroom. Over the years, people have read secret meanings into the little crosses and circles, linking them to the medieval Knights Templar or to secret societies. There's no evidence that anyone at Nabisco meant any of that, and the company has never explained the design.

So the next time you twist one open, remember that you're holding a small mystery that started on this block.

=== DEEPER Union Inland Terminal Number One | politics
You heard that this building was built by the Port Authority as a freight terminal. To understand why, it helps to know what the Port Authority was for.

New York's harbor is shared by two states, and for decades New York and New Jersey squabbled over it while freight piled up. Most of the railroads from the west ended on the New Jersey side of the Hudson, so goods had to cross the river on barges and ferries, an expensive, slow and chaotic system. In 1921, the two states signed a compact creating the Port of New York Authority, an independent agency to run the region's port and transportation as one system. It was one of the first agencies of its kind in the country.

The Port Authority's early projects changed the region: two bridges to Staten Island, then the George Washington Bridge in 1931, and control of the new Holland Tunnel, which had opened in 1927. Trucks could now drive under the river. So the Port Authority imagined a new kind of freight hub: a terminal in the middle of Manhattan, where trucks would carry goods to and from the railroad lines and the shipping piers on the Hudson, instead of every shipper making its own trips to the waterfront.

This building was the result. Its ground floor and basement were the terminal, Union Inland Terminal Number One, and the floors above were rented out as warehouses and workshops. Trucks drove into giant elevators, said to be the largest truck elevators ever built at the time, able to lift forty thousand pounds, and rode up to the tenants' doors. The name, Number One, shows the planners were thinking big.

As trucks took over from trains, the freight terminal faded, and the Port Authority eventually sold the building. But its strength and size kept it useful, first for manufacturers, then for the telephone and internet companies, and now for Google.

So this massive block is a monument to a big idea about freight, and to a building so well made that it kept finding new lives.
=== DEEPER The Meet-Me Room | underfoot
Somewhere in this building is a room where the internet shakes hands.

It's thousands of networks, run by phone companies, cable companies, internet providers, banks and tech firms. For data to pass from one network to another, their cables have to physically meet somewhere. Buildings where many networks come together are called carrier hotels, and the room inside where they connect is called a meet-me room. So today, software engineers work on some floors, while on others, racks of humming equipment pass data from one network to the next.

The next time a message from your phone reaches a friend across town, there's a fair chance it passed through a room like this one, maybe this very one, where cables from rival companies are plugged in side by side.

=== DEEPER A Palace of Shared Apartments | architecture
You heard that the Chelsea was built as one of the city's first cooperative apartment buildings. The idea behind it came from its architect, Philip Hubert.

In the 1880s, respectable middle class New Yorkers lived in private houses if they could afford them. === DEEPER Who Wrote The Night Before Christmas? | legends
You heard that some scholars doubt Clement Clarke Moore wrote A Visit from St. Nicholas. Here's the case, and the case against it.

The poem first appeared on December 23rd, 1823, in a small newspaper in Troy, New York, with no author's name. How it got there is unclear. One story says a family friend who had heard it at the Moores' sent it in. It was reprinted year after year, still anonymous. Moore's name was attached to it in a collection of poems in 1837, and in 1844 he included it in a book of his own poetry.

But the family of another New Yorker, Henry Livingston Junior, a farmer, surveyor and occasional poet from the Hudson Valley, insisted that he had written it, and had recited it to his own children years before it was published. Livingston had died in 1828, and his family's claim came years later, with no manuscript to back it up.

In 2000, a professor named Don Foster, famous for identifying anonymous authors from their writing style, took up the Livingston case. He argued that the poem's bouncy, cheerful rhythm matched Livingston's other light verse, and didn't sound like Moore, a serious scholar. He also pointed to the reindeer. In the first printing, two of them were named Dunder and Blixem, Dutch words for thunder and lightning. Livingston, who had Dutch roots, knew the language. Moore did not. Later versions changed the names to Donder and Blitzen.

Moore's defenders answered back. They pointed out that Moore wrote playful verse too, that he claimed the poem openly while people who could have contradicted him were still alive, and that parts of the Livingston family story had been shown to be wrong.

No one can prove it either way. The poem that helped create the American Santa Claus has an author on the plaque, and a question mark in the footnotes.
=== DEEPER Captain Clarke's Chelsea | names
Why is this neighborhood named after a district of London? Because of an old soldier, and the place where old soldiers retired.

In the middle of the 1700s, a retired British officer named Thomas Clarke bought farmland here, on the Hudson, a few miles north of the little city. He named his estate Chelsea, after the Royal Hospital Chelsea in London, the grand home for retired and wounded soldiers founded in the late 1600s, where veterans in scarlet coats still live today.

Clarke's daughter, Charity, married a young clergyman named Benjamin Moore, and their son, born in 1779, was Clement Clarke Moore. Clement inherited the estate from his mother in 1813, and he's the one who later divided it into the house lots you see around you.

Clement's father had his own place in history. Benjamin Moore became the Episcopal bishop of New York, and president of Columbia College. In July 1804, he was called to the bedside of Alexander Hamilton, who had been mortally wounded in his duel with Aaron Burr. Hamilton asked for holy communion. Moore hesitated, because the church condemned dueling. Only after Hamilton assured him that he repented of his part in the duel, and bore no ill will toward Burr, did the bishop give him the sacrament. Hamilton died on the afternoon of July 12th, the day after the duel.

So the name of this neighborhood carries a chain of stories: a veterans' home in London, an old soldier's farm, a bishop at a famous deathbed, and a scholar who may or may not have written the most famous Christmas poem in America.

=== DEEPER Following the Art to Chelsea | culture
You heard that the galleries came here from SoHo. One dealer's story shows how that happened.

In October 1968, Paula Cooper opened a gallery in a loft on Prince Street, the first gallery in what would become SoHo. Her first show was a benefit for a student group protesting the Vietnam War. Over the next decades she championed Minimalist and conceptual artists, and SoHo grew into the center of the contemporary art world.

By the 1990s, SoHo had become a shopping district. Rents had soared, and the big open lofts that artists and dealers loved were turning into boutiques. Cooper looked west, to Chelsea's garages and warehouses, which were cheap, enormous, and a long walk from anywhere. In 1995 she bought a building on West Twenty-First Street, and in 1996 she opened there, in a soaring space that people compared to a cathedral. Other major dealers made the same move around the same time, and within a few years, Chelsea had replaced SoHo.

The galleries here are free and open to anyone, usually Tuesday through Saturday. Don't be shy about walking in. The people at the front desk may look forbidding, but you're welcome to look, and many will gladly tell you about the work. Try walking one of the gallery blocks, between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues in the low Twenties, and ducking into whatever catches your eye. You might find a single huge sculpture filling a former garage, a quiet room of drawings, or something you can't quite explain.

The art world keeps moving. As rents here have climbed, some galleries have moved again, to the Lower East Side or to Tribeca. But for now, these blocks remain one of the greatest free art museums in the world, spread across hundreds of storefronts.
=== DEEPER A Freight Yard in the Sky | companies
The name Starrett-Lehigh joins a family of builders and a railroad, and the building tells the story of a partnership that didn't last.

The Lehigh Valley Railroad, which carried coal and freight from Pennsylvania, had no tracks into Manhattan. Like other railroads, it stopped at the New Jersey shore. So freight cars from its yard in Jersey City were loaded onto car floats, long barges with rails on their decks, and towed across the Hudson to its freight terminal here, where they were rolled ashore.

In 1930, the railroad joined with the Starrett family's real estate company to replace that old terminal with something new: a building where trains could pull in on the ground floor, and trucks could ride elevators to any of the nineteen floors above. The builders were Starrett Brothers and Eken, the same firm that was putting up the Empire State Building at the very same time. The designers were two brothers, Russell and Walter Cory, engineers who had studied at night at Cooper Union, working with the architect Yasuo Matsui.

The timing was terrible. The building opened in the depths of the Depression, when few businesses could afford to expand. William Starrett, one of the family's leading builders, died in 1932, and the railroad bought out the building, but by 1933 it was losing money. In 1944 the railroad gave up on it, and in 1966 the tracks were taken out.

The truck elevators, though, kept running, and in recent years they have even carried food trucks up into the building, like their freight-hauling ancestors.

So look up at those ribbons of windows and imagine freight cars floating across the river, rolling off the barges and into the ground floor of a skyscraper.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Trees and Stones | look
Walk over to West Twenty-Second Street, between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues, and look at the sidewalk. Beside many of the street trees stands a tall, narrow column of dark stone, like a small standing stone from some ancient site. It's a work of art, and part of a much bigger one.

The German artist Joseph Beuys began a project called 7000 Oaks in 1982, at a great art exhibition in the city of Kassel. He had seven thousand basalt stones dumped in a huge pile in front of the museum there, and announced that each stone would be moved only when a tree was planted beside it. Over the next five years, as the trees went in around the city, the pile shrank. The project was finished in 1987, the year after Beuys died. He saw it as social sculpture: an artwork made by a whole community, and a way of greening a city.

In 1988, the Dia Art Foundation, whose building on this street you heard about, brought the idea to New York, planting five trees with basalt stones outside its doors. In 1996, Dia added more, along this block. Today there are dozens of pairs here, though construction has sometimes forced a few of the stones to be taken away for a while.

Look at how the trees and stones change together over time. The stones stay the same. The trees keep growing, until one day they'll tower over their stone companions. That contrast was the point.

There's one more connection worth knowing. In 2003, Dia opened a huge museum up the Hudson, in the town of Beacon, in a former factory where the National Biscuit Company once printed its boxes. So a trail of art and crackers runs from these blocks all the way up the river.

=== DEEPER The Stadium That Wasn't | politics
You heard that the city once planned a football stadium on this site. The fight over it was one of the fiercest in modern New York politics.

In the early 2000s, Mayor Michael Bloomberg and his deputy mayor, Dan Doctoroff, wanted to bring the Olympic Games to New York in 2012. The centerpiece was to be a stadium over the rail yards, built for the New York Jets, who played their home games in New Jersey. It would host the Olympics first, and then the Jets, with hundreds of millions of dollars of public money for the platform and the roof, including three hundred million dollars from the state.

The opposition was fierce. Many West Side residents didn't want a stadium in their neighborhood, and many critics thought the money should go elsewhere. The most determined opponent was Cablevision, the company that owned Madison Square Garden, a few blocks east, which feared the competition. It fought the stadium hard, and even made its own bid for the rail yards.

In March 2005, the transit authority, which owned the yards, chose the Jets' bid. The last hurdle was an obscure state board, the Public Authorities Control Board, where three officials each held a veto: the governor, the leader of the State Senate, and the speaker of the Assembly. On June 6th, 2005, the representatives of the Senate leader, Joseph Bruno, and the Assembly speaker, Sheldon Silver, declined to vote yes, and the stadium was dead.

A month later, the International Olympic Committee chose London for 2012. The Jets eventually built a new stadium in New Jersey, together with the Giants. And the city turned to a different plan for the yards: rezone the area, extend the subway, and let developers build the towers you see now.

Some people still wonder what this place would be like with a stadium in it. Others are glad it never happened. Either way, the land where it would have stood is now some of the most expensive real estate in the country.
=== DEEPER The Subway That Came to Meet It | underfoot
You heard that the city extended the 7 train to a new station here. That station is one of the deepest in the subway system, and its story says a lot about how Hudson Yards was built.

Before the extension, the far West Side was a long walk from the nearest subway. Developers wouldn't build big towers here without one. So the city made an unusual bet. Instead of waiting for state and federal money, it paid for the extension itself, about two and a half billion dollars, by selling bonds through a new agency, the Hudson Yards Infrastructure Corporation. The bonds were to be paid back with future tax revenue from the buildings the subway would attract. In other words, the city borrowed against a neighborhood that didn't exist yet.

Tunnel boring machines dug a mile and a half of new tunnel from Times Square. The new station opened in September 2015, the first completely new station in the subway system since 1989, about a hundred and twenty five feet below the street.

To get people up and down from that depth, the station was given something new to the New York subway: two inclined elevators, which ride up and down at an angle alongside the escalators. They also caused trouble. Problems with the inclined elevators and the fire safety systems helped delay the station's opening by almost two years.

When you ride the long escalators down, notice the curved ceiling and the mosaics and the sheer depth of the place. Then remember what it represents: a subway built before the neighborhood, on the promise that a neighborhood would come. This time, it did.
=== DEEPER The Shed, a Building on Wheels | architecture
Look at the building next to the Vessel, the one with the quilted, puffy outer shell. That's The Shed, and part of it moves.

The Shed was designed by Diller Scofidio and Renfro, one of the firms behind the High Line, working with the Rockwell Group. It opened in 2019 as an arts center for every kind of performance and exhibition. Its fixed base building holds galleries and theaters. But its outer shell, a steel framework more than a hundred feet tall, sits on giant wheels, each six feet across, running on rails.

When the shell rolls out over the plaza next door, it creates a huge enclosed hall, about seventeen thousand square feet, for concerts and big events. When it rolls back, the plaza becomes open public space again. The shell weighs about eight million pounds, yet it travels the length of the plaza in about five minutes, moving at a quarter of a mile an hour. The engineers borrowed the technology from the giant gantry cranes that load and unload ships at container ports, a fitting choice on a waterfront where freight once ruled.

The shell's skin is made of a tough, clear plastic called ETFE, puffed into cushions like a quilt. It's far lighter than glass and lets daylight in.

The building is named for Michael Bloomberg, whose philanthropy helped pay for it. It's the Bloomberg Building. Next door, the honeycomb of stairs called the Vessel took the opposite approach: less a building than a giant sculpture you can climb.

Together, they say something about this whole neighborhood: built on top of a working rail yard, borrowing tools from the docks and the railroads, and still, at heart, a piece of the industrial West Side.
