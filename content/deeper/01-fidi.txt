=== DEEPER A Fort Built for Crossfire | look
Walk around the outside of Castle Clinton and look at the wall that faces the water. The openings set into it are embrasures, or gun ports, and there were twenty eight of them, built for cannon that could throw a thirty two pound iron ball more than a mile out into the harbor. The stone around them is about eight feet thick. Now notice which way the gate faces: toward the city, not the sea. A fort puts its front door on the side it trusts.

Look across the water to Governors Island and find the round, red stone fort on its western point. That is Castle Williams, built in the same years as this one. The two were designed as a pair, so that any warship heading for the city would sail into fire from both sides at once. Neither was ever tested.

Now walk to the railing at the water's edge, just north of the fort, and look for a group of bronze sailors out in the water. This is the American Merchant Mariners' Memorial, by the artist Marisol, dedicated in 1991. It is based on a photograph taken in 1942 by the crew of a German submarine, showing survivors of the American tanker Muskogee on a raft after their ship was torpedoed. None of those men was ever seen again. Find the sailor stretched across the edge of the raft, reaching for a man in the water. At high tide, the harbor rises over the man in the water.

Back toward the fort, the tall granite slabs watched over by a bronze eagle are the East Coast Memorial, carved with the names of Americans lost at sea in the western Atlantic during the Second World War.

And the carousel whose riders sit inside glowing, oversized fish is the SeaGlass Carousel, opened in 2015 as a nod to the decades when this fort was the city's aquarium. The loose, meadowy gardens all around it were designed by the Dutch plantsman Piet Oudolf, a few years before he planted the High Line.
=== DEEPER The Bridge That Never Got Built | underfoot
Wherever you stand in this park, you are standing on fill. When the fort was finished in 1811, the shoreline ran roughly along the curve of State Street, behind you, and the fort sat out in the water at the end of its bridge. Over the following decades the city kept dumping earth and rubble into the harbor until the fort was simply swallowed by the land.

The things buried under the lawn now are far more ambitious. In the late 1930s, Robert Moses wanted a giant suspension bridge from here to Brooklyn. Its Manhattan approach would have consumed much of Battery Park, and preservationists fought it hard. The decisive blow came from Washington: the War Department ruled that a bridge downstream of the Brooklyn Navy Yard could, if it were bombed, fall into the channel and trap the fleet. So Moses went under the harbor instead. The Brooklyn Battery Tunnel, now officially named for Governor Hugh Carey, opened in 1950. At more than nine thousand feet, it was long described as the longest continuous underwater vehicle tunnel in North America, and its traffic is running somewhere beneath you right now.

The subway is down there too. The old South Ferry station on the 1 line was built as a tight loop, so trains could turn around without reversing. In 2009, the city replaced it with a new, straighter station. Three years later, in October 2012, Hurricane Sandy drove the water at the Battery to the highest level ever recorded here, nearly fourteen feet above the average low tide. The sea poured over the wall and filled the brand new station almost to its ceiling. The transit authority dusted off the century old loop next door and ran trains through it for four more years while the new station was rebuilt.

So the oldest layer here is the fort, the newest is the storm damage, and in between are a tunnel, a station, and a ghost loop, all on land that used to be harbor.
=== DEEPER The Stars and Strangers of Castle Garden | people
Before Castle Garden took in immigrants, it took in celebrities, and it is worth picturing the crowds.

In 1824, the Marquis de Lafayette, the French hero of the American Revolution, came back for a farewell tour of the country, and New York welcomed him here with a reception for many thousands of people. In 1842, Samuel Morse laid an insulated wire along the harbor floor from the Battery to Governors Island to prove that a telegraph signal could travel underwater. The demonstration was cut short when a passing ship pulled up his cable with its anchor.

Then came Jenny Lind. Barnum sold the tickets for her first concert by auction, and the very first ticket went for two hundred twenty five dollars to John Genin, a hatter whose shop happened to be next door to Barnum's museum on Broadway. The newspapers suspected the two men had arranged the whole thing. If they had, it worked for both of them. Genin became famous overnight, and he went on to sell a Jenny Lind hat.

Five years later, the same building became the Emigrant Landing Depot, and the crowds were very different. Ships anchored out in the harbor, and passengers were brought ashore here by barge. Inside, they were examined and registered, and they were offered services that seemed remarkable at the time: honest money changers, railroad tickets at fair prices, a labor exchange that matched new arrivals with employers, and help writing letters home. Outside waited the runners: men who spoke your language, grabbed your luggage, and steered you to boarding houses and ticket agents that would take everything you had. Keeping them away from new arrivals was one of the depot's main jobs.

When the federal government took over immigration in 1890, it moved the work to a temporary station nearby. Ellis Island opened on the first day of 1892. The first person registered there was Annie Moore, a teenager from County Cork in Ireland, traveling with her two younger brothers.

=== DEEPER Standard Oil at 26 Broadway | companies
John D. Rockefeller's company was born in Cleveland, but by the 1880s the center of American money was here, and Standard Oil moved its headquarters to 26 Broadway.

What was Standard Oil, legally? For a time, it was a trust. In 1882, the shareholders of dozens of separate oil companies handed their stock to a small board of trustees, who ran them all as a single machine. The word trust in antitrust comes from arrangements like this one.

The machine was ruthless. It negotiated secret rebates from the railroads on its shipments, undercut competitors until they gave up, and then bought them out. The journalist Ida Tarbell, whose own father's oil business had been squeezed by Rockefeller's maneuvers, spent years in courthouses and archives documenting exactly how it was done. Her series in McClure's Magazine, published as a book in 1904 called The History of the Standard Oil Company, is one of the founding works of investigative journalism.

In 1911, the Supreme Court ordered the company split into thirty four pieces. Standard Oil of New Jersey became Exxon. Standard Oil of New York, which went by the name Socony, became Mobil. Standard Oil of California became Chevron. Exxon and Mobil merged back together in 1999.

Here is the twist. Rockefeller owned about a quarter of the company, and once it was broken up, the pieces turned out to be worth far more than the whole had been. The breakup made him richer than ever.

Now look up at the building again. Its lower floors bend with the curve of Broadway, but the tower above them is turned to line up with the other skyscrapers of lower Manhattan, so the top seems slightly twisted against its base. The building took its present form in stages during the 1920s. The company eventually moved uptown, but the oil lamp on the roof stayed behind.
=== DEEPER The Bull, the Fence, and the Subway House | look
Start with the Charging Bull, and notice which parts of it shine. The bronze has darkened almost everywhere except the nose, the horns, and, around the back, the parts that visitors rub for luck. Those polished patches are a map of a tourist ritual. Arturo Di Modica paid for the bull himself, about three hundred sixty thousand dollars, and it weighs more than three tons. For decades it has stood here on a temporary permit.

In 2017, a second bronze appeared, facing it down: Fearless Girl, a small girl with her hands on her hips, commissioned by an investment firm to promote women on corporate boards. Di Modica objected that she changed the meaning of his work. In 2018 she was moved a few blocks away, to face the Stock Exchange instead.

Now look closely at the tops of the old iron fence posts around the green. Their rough, cut-off tops are usually explained as the scars of July 1776, when the crowns were sawed off.

Just south of the green, at the edge of Battery Park, find the small building of tan brick and stone with curving Flemish gables. It is a subway entrance, built in 1905 as a control house, a ticket hall for the city's first subway line, by that line's architects, Heins and LaFarge. It is one of only three of these control houses still standing.

Finally, the Custom House. Above the windows on the main front, look for the carved heads in the keystones. They were meant to represent the races of mankind as people classified them in 1907, which is itself part of the history on display. Look higher, and you'll find the head of Mercury, the Roman god of commerce, worked into the capitals of the columns. Inside the oval rotunda, the murals of ships and harbor scenes were painted in the 1930s by Reginald Marsh, a painter better known for his crowded scenes of Coney Island.
=== DEEPER Twenty Four Dollars | legends
You may have heard that the Dutch bought Manhattan from the Native Americans for twenty four dollars' worth of beads. Almost every part of that sentence is shaky.

The only record written at the time is a letter dated November 1626 from Pieter Schaghen, a Dutch official, reporting news just arrived from the colony. Our people, he wrote, have purchased the island Manhattes from the Indians for the value of sixty guilders. That is it. No deed survives. The letter does not mention beads. It does not say who sold the island, where the deal was made, or even who made it for the Dutch. Peter Minuit, the colony's director, usually gets the credit, and that is likely, but the letter does not name him.

The twenty four dollar figure is a nineteenth century conversion that has been repeated ever since without much thought for what money was worth in 1626. Nobody can turn sixty guilders cleanly into modern dollars. And the payment was probably not trinkets. Trade on this coast ran on practical goods such as cloth, metal kettles, axes and hoes.

Then there is the question of what each side believed it was doing. To the Dutch, it was a sale. The Lenape did not have a tradition of selling land outright, and many historians think they understood agreements like this one as permission to share and use the land, something to be renewed with further gifts, not a permanent surrender.

As for where it happened, a plaque in Inwood Hill Park, at the northern tip of the island, marks a legendary spot beneath a great tulip tree. There is no evidence for it. A spot near this green, beside the Dutch fort, has as good a claim as anywhere.

Look for the tall flagpole at the edge of Battery Park, a gift from the Netherlands in 1926. Its base commemorates the purchase, legend and all.
=== DEEPER Broadway, Whitehall and Beaver | names
The streets that meet here carry four centuries of history in their names.

Broadway began as a Lenape trail running up the island. The Dutch widened it into a road heading north from their fort, the broad way. Whitehall Street, running south from the green, is named for a house. Peter Stuyvesant, the last Dutch director of the colony, built himself a large residence near the water, and under the English it came to be called the White Hall.

Beaver Street honors the animal that paid for the colony. Beaver fur, felted into hats, was New Netherland's great export. Look for the seal of the City of New York on any city building or vehicle and you'll find two beavers on its shield, along with two flour barrels and the four sails of a windmill. The barrels are there because, for a stretch of the late seventeenth century, New York held a monopoly on sifting and packing flour for export, and the trade made the town rich.

Marketfield Street, a short lane just east of the green, recalls the market field of New Amsterdam, where cattle were traded. Bridge Street, nearby, was named for a bridge over the canal that once ran up Broad Street. Stone Street was named for its stones.

State Street, curving along the park's edge, follows roughly where the shoreline ran at the end of the eighteenth century, when a row of fine houses faced the water here. One survives: the James Watson House at 7 State Street, the brick Federal style house with a curved porch of slender columns, built in the 1790s and enlarged a few years later. Today it belongs to the shrine of Elizabeth Ann Seton, who lived next door and became the first person born in the United States to be named a saint.

=== DEEPER The Great Fire of 1835 | history
Almost everything on Stone Street exists because of one winter night.

On December 16th, 1835, a fire broke out in a warehouse near Hanover Square. It was one of the coldest nights anyone could remember, far below zero, with a gale blowing off the water. The fire engines of the day pumped from street cisterns and from the rivers. The cisterns froze. Firefighters chopped holes in the ice of the East River, and the water froze in their hoses.

The fire burned for most of two days and destroyed about seventeen blocks and hundreds of buildings, nearly the entire commercial heart of the city between Wall Street and the East River. The grandest loss was the Merchants' Exchange on Wall Street, with its marble dome. Inside stood a brand new marble statue of Alexander Hamilton. Sailors tried to carry it out, but had to run when the dome came down, and the statue was destroyed.

The fire was finally stopped by blowing up buildings in its path to create firebreaks, using gunpowder fetched from the Brooklyn Navy Yard.

The money damage spread far beyond the burned blocks. Most of the city's fire insurance companies were wiped out by the claims, which meant many merchants were never paid at all. And the city drew a lesson about water. Earlier that same year, voters had approved a great aqueduct to bring fresh water from the Croton River in Westchester. The fire made the case unanswerable. The Croton Aqueduct opened in 1842, and it is the ancestor of the water system that still supplies the city today.

Now look at the buildings around you. They are the rebuilding: plain, sturdy warehouses and counting houses of brick and stone, put up quickly in the years after the fire, on the same narrow lots and along the same crooked lanes. The city had a chance to straighten these streets and didn't take it. That is why Stone Street still bends the way it did when the Dutch laid it out.
=== DEEPER The Dutch City Under the Pavement | look
Walk to the plaza in front of 85 Broad Street, the big office tower at the corner of Pearl and Broad, and look down. Lighter stone set into the pavement traces the walls of buildings that stood here more than three hundred fifty years ago.

One outline marks the Stadt Huys, the City Hall of New Amsterdam. It was built in the early 1640s as the City Tavern, facing the water, and became the town hall in 1653. Another outline traces the Lovelace Tavern, built around 1670 by the English governor Francis Lovelace. Look for the glass panels set into the sidewalk, protected by brass rails. Through them you can see the tavern's actual foundations, found by archaeologists before this tower was built.

The tower itself has a secret. Its builders wanted to erase this block of Stone Street, and the city objected. The compromise: the old path of Stone Street runs through the building's lobby as a curving corridor. Walk in, and you are walking along a colonial street, indoors.

Now look at Broad Street. It is unusually wide for this part of town, and there is a reason. It was a canal. The Dutch turned a marshy inlet here into a canal called the Heere Gracht, much like the canals of home, and the English filled it in by 1676. The width of the street is the width of the water.

Back on Stone Street, find number 57, the building with the stepped gable on top that looks like it came straight from old Amsterdam. It didn't. It was given that gable in 1903, by the architect C. P. H. Gilbert, at a time when New Yorkers were rediscovering, and romanticizing, their Dutch past. There are a few more stepped gables of the same vintage nearby on South William Street. They are a revival, not a survival, and they tell you as much about 1903 as about 1650.
=== DEEPER Delmonico's and the Invention of Dining Out | food
When the Delmonico brothers opened their café in 1827, eating out in New York mostly meant a tavern or a boarding house, where you ate what was served when it was served. The family built something new: a restaurant, with separate tables, linen, a wine cellar, and a printed menu listing hundreds of dishes, in French and English.

The real builder of the business was a nephew, Lorenzo Delmonico, who came over as a young man and ran the family firm for decades. As fashionable New York moved uptown, so did Delmonico's, with branches near Union Square, then Madison Square, and finally Fifth Avenue. Its kitchen was run for decades by a French chef named Charles Ranhofer, who in 1894 published his life's work, a cookbook called The Epicurean, with thousands of recipes.

Now for the famous dishes, and how much to believe.

Lobster Newberg. The story goes that a sea captain named Ben Wenberg demonstrated a lobster dish he'd picked up on his travels, and Delmonico's put it on the menu as Lobster à la Wenberg. After the captain and Lorenzo quarreled, the dish was renamed by flipping the first letters: Newberg. It's a wonderful story, often repeated, and impossible to prove.

Baked Alaska. Ranhofer served a cake with ice cream inside a browned meringue shell and called it Alaska, Florida, for the contrast of hot and cold. The name is usually linked to the American purchase of Alaska in 1867.

Eggs Benedict has two rival origin stories: one involving a Delmonico's regular, Mrs. LeGrand Benedict, who asked for something new; and one involving a Wall Street broker, Lemuel Benedict, who said he ordered it at the Waldorf Hotel in 1894 to cure a hangover. Both stories were told decades after the fact.

And the Delmonico steak? Nobody agrees on which cut it was.

As for the marble columns at the Beaver Street door, they are said to have come from the ruins of Pompeii. Take that one as legend, too.
=== DEEPER Black Sam and the Poisoned Peas | legends
Samuel Fraunces is one of the most intriguing figures of Revolutionary New York, and almost every story about him comes with an asterisk.

He was born in the West Indies around 1722, and people at the time called him Black Sam. For a long time that nickname was taken as evidence that he was of African descent, and he was celebrated as a Black patriot of the Revolution. Other historians point out that the nickname may have referred to his complexion, and that some records describe him as white. The honest answer is that we don't know.

Some things we do know. His tavern was a hub of patriot New York. After the war, Congress formally thanked him for his help to American prisoners held by the British during the occupation. And when George Washington became president, Fraunces became steward of the presidential household, running the kitchens in New York and then in Philadelphia.

Now, the legend. In June of 1776, a plot was uncovered among some of the soldiers guarding Washington, encouraged by the colony's loyalist governor and mayor. One guard, Thomas Hickey, was court-martialed for mutiny and sedition and hanged before a crowd said to number twenty thousand. The very next day, the first ships of a huge British fleet appeared off Sandy Hook.

In the nineteenth century, the story grew. Fraunces's daughter Phoebe, it was said, was working as Washington's housekeeper and learned that Hickey had poisoned a dish of peas meant for the general. She warned him, he threw the peas out the window, and the chickens that ate them died. There is no record from the time of the peas, the warning, or the unlucky chickens.

Fraunces Tavern carries a modern scar as well. On January 24th, 1975, a bomb planted by a Puerto Rican nationalist group exploded in a building attached to the tavern during the lunch hour, killing four people.
=== STOP Wall Street, Federal Hall & the Stock Exchange
=== DEEPER The Panic of 1907 | companies
In October 1907, an attempt to corner the stock of a copper company collapsed, and the men behind it turned out to be tied to some of the city's trust companies, the lightly regulated cousins of banks. Depositors panicked. On October 22nd, a run on the Knickerbocker Trust, on Fifth Avenue, drained about eight million dollars in three hours, and the bank shut its doors. Weeks later its former president shot himself.

The United States had no central bank and no lender of last resort. So the country turned to a seventy year old banker. J. Pierpont Morgan directed the rescue from his offices here and from his library on Thirty Sixth Street. He decided which institutions were worth saving and which were not. He persuaded the Treasury Secretary to deposit government money in New York banks. When brokers on the Stock Exchange could no longer borrow and the exchange itself was about to close early, he summoned the bank presidents and raised twenty five million dollars to lend on the floor in a matter of minutes.

The climax came on the first weekend of November. Morgan gathered the heads of the trust companies in his library and, as the story goes, locked the door and pocketed the key, keeping them there until nearly dawn, when they agreed to put up twenty five million dollars to prop up their weakest members. The same weekend, he arranged for United States Steel, the giant he had created, to buy a struggling company called Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad, whose shares were propping up loans at a teetering brokerage. President Theodore Roosevelt agreed not to object on antitrust grounds.

It worked. But Washington drew a lesson: no private citizen should ever again hold that much power over the nation's money. Investigations followed, then a secret meeting of bankers and a senator on Jekyll Island, Georgia, in 1910, and finally the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. Morgan died that same year. The central bank his rescue made necessary sits a few blocks north of here, on Liberty Street.
=== DEEPER Three Days on the Trading Floor | people
Three moments on the floor of the exchange behind those columns.

October 24th, 1929, Black Thursday. Prices are falling off a cliff. In the early afternoon, Richard Whitney, the exchange's vice president and the floor broker for Morgan's bank, walks to the post where United States Steel trades and loudly bids two hundred five dollars a share for ten thousand shares, well above the last sale. He moves from post to post placing similar orders on behalf of a pool of the city's biggest bankers. For a day, the market steadies, and Whitney is a hero. The next week the crash resumes anyway. Whitney becomes president of the exchange in 1930. Then, in 1938, it comes out that he has been stealing, from clients, from the New York Yacht Club, and from the exchange's own fund for the families of deceased members. He goes to Sing Sing prison.

August 24th, 1967. A group of young activists led by Abbie Hoffman joins a public tour, reaches the visitors' gallery above the floor, and showers the traders with dollar bills. Trading stops as brokers scramble for the money, some cheering, some booing. Soon afterward, the exchange enclosed the gallery behind glass.

December 28th, 1967. Muriel Siebert, a broker who had been turned down by nine of the first ten men she asked to sponsor her application, buys a seat on the exchange for four hundred forty five thousand dollars. She becomes the first woman member in its history, which by then was well over a century and a half long. For years, she said, there was no women's restroom anywhere near the floor, until she pushed the exchange to install one.

The gallery where Hoffman stood was closed to the public after September 2001, and the floor is no longer open to tours. But the building is still where the opening and closing bells ring every trading day.
=== DEEPER Facades of Wall and Broad | look
Start with the Stock Exchange's pediment. The figures up there are not stone. The original marble sculptures were enormously heavy and began to crumble within a few years. In 1936 they were replaced with copies made of sheet copper coated with lead and painted to look like marble, at a fraction of the weight. What you see is a very good fake.

At the foot of the exchange, the small bronze girl with her hands on her hips is Fearless Girl, who used to stare down the bull at Bowling Green.

Now Federal Hall. Its front is modeled on the Parthenon in Athens, with eight fluted Doric columns, and it is one of the finest Greek Revival buildings in the country, designed by the firm of Ithiel Town and Alexander Jackson Davis. Inside, under a coffered dome, you can find an exhibit on Washington's inauguration. The statue of Washington out front, by John Quincy Adams Ward, was unveiled in 1883 on the centennial of the British evacuation of New York.

Look across the intersection at 23 Wall Street, the Morgan bank. It is only a few stories tall, on some of the most valuable land on earth. That was the point. The House of Morgan did not need to rent out offices or advertise its name. Look again at the scars in its stone. Historians today suspect an Italian anarchist named Mario Buda of planting the 1920 bomb, but he was never charged, and he left the country soon afterward.

Finally, look west toward Broadway, at One Wall Street, the limestone tower whose walls are folded like a curtain. It was built in 1931 for the Irving Trust Company, designed by Ralph Walker, and it hides one of the most spectacular rooms in the city: a banking hall covered from floor to ceiling in red and gold mosaic, by the artist Hildreth Meière.
=== DEEPER The Wall, and the Market at Its Foot | history
The wall that named this street went up in 1653, when New Amsterdam feared an attack from New England during a war between the Dutch and the English. It was a palisade of upright logs with an earthen bank behind it, running from river to river. Much of the colony's heavy labor was done by Africans enslaved by the Dutch West India Company, and historians believe they helped build it.

Slavery was not incidental to colonial New York. It was built into the place. In the eighteenth century, roughly one in five people in the city was enslaved, one of the largest urban enslaved populations in the colonies.

In 1711, the city council made the foot of Wall Street, at the waterfront, the official place where enslaved people were to be hired out and sold. It was known as the Meal Market, and it operated until 1762. In 2015 the city placed a marker near the spot, at the corner of Wall and Water Streets, a few blocks east of here.

The colony's fear of the people it enslaved produced one of the darkest episodes in its history. In 1741, after a series of fires, including one at the governor's house inside the fort, authorities announced that they had uncovered a plot by enslaved Africans and poor whites to burn the city. On flimsy testimony, much of it from a sixteen year old indentured servant named Mary Burton, who had been promised a reward, the courts convicted dozens. Thirteen Black men were burned at the stake. Seventeen Black men and four white people were hanged. The trials were held in the old City Hall, on the site of Federal Hall, right where you are standing.

New York passed a law for gradual emancipation in 1799 and ended slavery entirely only in 1827. Even after that, the city's banks, insurers, and shippers stayed bound to Southern cotton. In January 1861, as the South was seceding, Mayor Fernando Wood proposed that New York City secede too, and become a free city that could keep trading with both sides.
=== STOP Trinity Church
=== DEEPER The Doors and the Stones | look
Before you go into the churchyard, look at Trinity's bronze doors. There are three sets, made in the 1890s as a memorial to John Jacob Astor the Third, paid for by his son William Waldorf Astor, and overseen by the architect Richard Morris Hunt. They were inspired by Ghiberti's famous bronze doors on the baptistery in Florence. The main doors, facing Wall Street, are by the sculptor Karl Bitter, who won a competition for them. Their panels show scenes from the Bible, and somewhere among them, as a thank you, Bitter slipped in a portrait of Hunt, the man who picked him.

Now enter the churchyard on the north side and look for one of the smallest stones, worn and leaning. It marks Richard Churcher, a boy of five who died in 1681, and it is the oldest surviving gravestone in the churchyard, carved before Trinity was even founded. On its back are a skull and crossbones and a winged hourglass, the standard reminders of the period that time flies and death comes for everyone.

Also on the north side is a tall brownstone Gothic monument, a sort of miniature spire. This is the Soldiers' Monument, put up in 1852 to the American prisoners of war who died in the British jails of occupied New York during the Revolution. Whether any of them are actually buried in this churchyard is disputed.

Then walk out to Broadway and look down at the sidewalk. Set into the pavement along the avenue, from Bowling Green up toward City Hall, are strips of granite with names and dates. Each one marks a ticker tape parade up this stretch of Broadway, the route New Yorkers call the Canyon of Heroes. The custom began in 1886, when office workers threw ticker tape out of their windows during the celebration of the Statue of Liberty's dedication. See how many names you recognize: astronauts, generals, presidents, champions, and the first responders of 2001.
=== DEEPER Trinity, the Landlord | companies
In 1705, Queen Anne granted Trinity a large farm on the west side of Manhattan, stretching from about where the World Trade Center is now up toward Greenwich Village. Over the next three centuries the parish used that land like an endowment. It gave away parcels to help found dozens of other churches and institutions, including King's College, the school that became Columbia University, which began on land Trinity provided just a few blocks north of here.

The grant was challenged for more than a century. The heirs of Anneke Jans, a widow of New Amsterdam who had owned a farm within the grant in the 1600s, argued that the land had been transferred without the consent of all of her descendants. Generation after generation of her descendants sued Trinity. They lost every case.

Trinity's role as a landlord has not always been a happy one. By the late nineteenth century, many of its holdings in the old west side neighborhoods were crowded tenements. In the 1890s it fought the city's new requirement that tenement owners provide running water on every floor, and in 1908 the muckraking journalist Charles Edward Russell published a scathing exposé of Trinity's tenements, calling the parish one of the city's worst slumlords. Embarrassed, the church began clearing and rebuilding.

In the twentieth century, Trinity turned the area north of Canal Street, where its remaining land was concentrated, into a printing district, full of sturdy loft buildings. Today that area is known as Hudson Square, and as you heard on the SoHo and Tribeca tour, it has been remade around media and technology companies.

Trinity remains one of the larger landlords in Manhattan, with a commercial real estate portfolio worth billions of dollars. Its income supports the parish, its grantmaking, and its work on housing and homelessness. The church at the end of Wall Street is, among other things, one of the oldest real estate businesses in America.
=== DEEPER Hamilton's Circle | people
Alexander Hamilton's monument is just one of several graves in this churchyard tied to his life.

Start with the duel itself. On the morning of July 11th, 1804, Hamilton and Aaron Burr met on a ledge below the cliffs at Weehawken, New Jersey. Hamilton was hit and died the next day, in a friend's house in Greenwich Village. The pistols they used belonged to Hamilton's brother-in-law, John Church. Today they are owned by JPMorgan Chase, the bank that descends in part from Burr's Manhattan Company.

Eliza Hamilton, buried beside him, outlived her husband by half a century. She spent much of it defending his reputation, gathering his papers, and running the first private orphanage in New York, which she helped found in 1806. She died in 1854, at the age of ninety seven.

Her sister Angelica Schuyler Church, brilliant, witty and a close friend of Hamilton's, is buried here too, in a family vault in the churchyard. She corresponded with Jefferson and Lafayette, and was among the best connected women of her age, at home in London, Paris and New York.

Look also for the stone of Hercules Mulligan, the Irish born tailor and early member of the Sons of Liberty. During the British occupation, he passed along what he overheard from British officers who came to his shop to be measured, and he is remembered as one of Washington's spies. He was also an early friend of Hamilton's when the young man first arrived in New York from the Caribbean.

Ever since the musical, Hamilton's grave has become a pilgrimage site. Visitors leave pennies, notes and flowers on it, which would probably have amused the man whose face is on the ten dollar bill.
=== DEEPER Captain Kidd, Charlotte Temple, and the Treasure Below | legends
Trinity attracts legends the way old churches do. Here are three, checked.

First, the pirate. When the first Trinity Church was going up in the late 1690s, Captain William Kidd, then a respectable New York sea captain with a house on Pearl Street, lent the builders the runner and tackle from his ship, rope and pulleys for hoisting stone. The church's own records support this, and Kidd also rented a pew. A few years later he was arrested for piracy and hanged in London in 1701. The part about Kidd personally hoisting the steeple is embellishment, and so are the endless rumors that he buried treasure somewhere in New York.

Second, the grave of a woman who never lived. In the north churchyard, near the oldest gravestones, is a stone inscribed Charlotte Temple. Charlotte Temple is the heroine of a novel by Susanna Rowson, published in the 1790s, about a young English girl seduced, brought to New York and abandoned. It was one of the best selling American novels of its century, and for generations readers came here to weep over a grave belonging to a character in a book. Nobody knows for certain who, if anyone, lies under the stone, or who carved the name. It may have been a stonecutter's whim, or a marketing stunt.

Third, the treasure under the church. In the 2004 movie National Treasure, Nicolas Cage's character follows a trail of clues to a secret chamber deep beneath Trinity Church, filled with the lost treasure of the Knights Templar. There is no chamber. There is a church, a crypt of ordinary size, a lot of bedrock, and, a few blocks away under Liberty Street, a vault full of actual gold, which belongs to other people.
=== STOP The Federal Reserve & Chase Manhattan Plaza
=== DEEPER Inside the Gold Vault | underfoot
The gold vault of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York sits about eighty feet below the street, beneath the waterline of the harbor, on Manhattan bedrock. Bedrock is the reason it can exist: few other foundations could carry the weight. The vault holds about half a million gold bars, on the order of six thousand tons, and almost none of it belongs to the United States. It belongs to foreign governments, central banks, and international organizations, held here free of charge.

There is no conventional door. The only way in is through a passage cut in a steel cylinder that weighs about ninety tons, set in a frame of steel and concrete. To close the vault, the cylinder is rotated a quarter turn, and it drops slightly to make an airtight and watertight seal. Once it's shut, it stays shut until the next business day.

Inside are more than a hundred numbered compartments. Each holder's gold sits in its own compartment, identified by number, not by name. When one country pays another in gold, staff simply move the bars from one compartment to the other. The work is done by gold stackers, who handle bars weighing around twenty eight pounds each. They wear protective covers made of magnesium over their shoes, in case a bar slips.

During the Second World War and the Cold War, many countries kept their reserves here, an ocean away from the dangers of Europe. More recently, some have taken gold home. Germany brought three hundred tons back to Frankfurt from New York between 2013 and 2017.

The vault has also starred in at least one movie. In the 1995 action film Die Hard with a Vengeance, villains tunnel in and empty it with dump trucks. The real vault, for the record, has never been robbed.
=== DEEPER Chase, Rockefeller, and the Octagon | companies
The bank whose tower stands across Liberty Street carries a borrowed name. The Chase National Bank was founded in 1877 and named in honor of Salmon P. Chase, Lincoln's Treasury Secretary and later Chief Justice. Chase had nothing to do with it; he was simply a famous name in finance.

In 1955, Chase merged with the Bank of the Manhattan Company, the bank that began as Aaron Burr's water company. The result was Chase Manhattan, and David Rockefeller, grandson of the founder of Standard Oil, rose through its ranks to run it in the late 1960s and 1970s. Rockefeller money ran deep at Chase. His uncle Winthrop Aldrich had led the Chase National Bank in the 1930s.

The building on this plaza was a declaration of faith in downtown at a time when corporations were leaving for Midtown. It was also an engineering statement. When it opened in 1961, it was one of the first International Style towers downtown, with columns on the outside and open floors within.

In the same year, the bank unveiled a new symbol: an abstract octagon made of four wedges around a square, designed by the firm Chermayeff and Geismar. At the time, banks still mostly used their names or old-fashioned emblems, and the octagon became one of the first modern abstract corporate logos in America. Its descendant is still the logo of JPMorgan Chase.

That bank, formed by a merger in 2000, is the corporate heir of Chase National, the Manhattan Company, and J. P. Morgan's firm all at once. Stand here and you are near the beginnings of all three.
=== DEEPER Liberty, Maiden Lane and Golden Hill | names
The streets around you were renamed by the Revolution, and some of their older names are worth hearing.

Liberty Street, where you're standing, was called Crown Street before independence. After the war, New Yorkers took the crown down. King Street, a block south, became Pine Street. Queen Street was folded into Pearl Street in 1794. If you have noticed that King Street and Prince Street survive farther uptown in SoHo, that's because they were laid out later, in areas the patriots didn't bother renaming.

Maiden Lane, the street just north of the Federal Reserve, is said to take its name from the Dutch, Maagde Paatje, the maidens' path, because a brook ran here and young women came to wash clothes in it. That explanation goes back a long way, but like many charming name stories, it's hard to confirm.

Nassau Street, a block west, is named for the House of Orange-Nassau, the Dutch royal line of King William the Third, who ruled England when the name was given. John Street is usually traced to a landowner named John Harpending.

Gold Street, a few blocks northeast, takes its name from a hill called Golden Hill, perhaps for the grain or wildflowers that grew on it. Golden Hill gave its name to one of the opening fights of the Revolution. On January 19th, 1770, Sons of Liberty and British soldiers brawled in the streets near here after soldiers cut down a liberty pole, a tall flagpole the patriots had raised. Several men were wounded, and some accounts say one died. It happened six weeks before the Boston Massacre, and New Yorkers have been pointing that out to Bostonians ever since.

Finally, look for the little triangle where Maiden Lane meets William Street, home to a group of black steel sculptures by Louise Nevelson. It is called Louise Nevelson Plaza, one of the few public squares in the city named after an artist, and the sculptures are hers.
=== STOP 9/11 Memorial & the Oculus
=== DEEPER Little Syria and Radio Row | history
Before the World Trade Center, this was a neighborhood, and before that, another one.

From the 1880s into the twentieth century, the streets just south and west of here, especially Washington Street, were the heart of Little Syria, home to immigrants from what are now Lebanon and Syria, most of them Christians. There were Arabic newspapers, import shops selling lace and linens, coffee houses and churches. Kahlil Gibran, author of The Prophet, published in its Arabic newspapers and helped found a celebrated circle of Arab American writers in New York. Much of Little Syria was torn down in the 1940s for the approaches to the Brooklyn Battery Tunnel. Look for the former St. George's Syrian Catholic Church at 103 Washington Street, a narrow building with a terra cotta front, now a city landmark. It is one of the last traces of that neighborhood.

North of it, the blocks around Cortlandt Street became Radio Row. Starting in the 1920s, hundreds of small shops packed these streets selling radios, tubes, parts, and later televisions and surplus electronics. If you were a ham radio operator or a tinkerer, this was your bazaar.

When the Port Authority announced the World Trade Center in the early 1960s, the merchants of Radio Row fought back. Led by a shopkeeper named Oscar Nadel, they took the fight to court and to the press, arguing that a public agency was seizing private businesses to build an office complex. They lost. The courts upheld the plan, and the Supreme Court declined to hear their appeal. Demolition began in 1966.

Underneath it all ran the Hudson Tubes, the train line to New Jersey, which since 1909 had ended here at the Hudson Terminal, where twin twenty two story office buildings stood above the tracks. So the World Trade Center replaced a pair of buildings with another pair. The trains still run today as PATH.
=== DEEPER The Bathtub and the Buried Ship | underfoot
The ground you are standing on was once the bed of the Hudson River, and the whole site is, in effect, a bathtub.

To build the original towers, engineers needed a deep basement below the water table, right beside the river. They used a technique then rarely tried in America: they dug trenches in narrow sections down to bedrock, kept them from collapsing with a heavy clay slurry, then filled them with concrete. The result was a continuous underground wall around the site, called the slurry wall. After September 11th, engineers feared the wall would give way, because the basement floors that had braced it from inside were destroyed. If it had, the river could have flooded the site and the train tunnels beneath it. The wall held, while crews braced it again from the inside. A section of it is now on view inside the September 11th museum, below the plaza.

The dig for the new site turned up something stranger. In the summer of 2010, workers excavating near the southern edge of the site found the hull of a wooden ship, about thirty feet of it, buried more than twenty feet down. It had been sunk on purpose, as part of the fill used to extend the shoreline in the late 1700s. Scientists later studied the rings in its timbers and matched them with old trees and historic buildings in Philadelphia, including Independence Hall. The oak for the ship had been cut around 1773, near Philadelphia. The workers had found a merchant sloop from the era of the Revolution, used as rubbish to build land.

That's how most of lower Manhattan's western edge was made: ships, piers, and rubbish, sunk and filled and built over. And the World Trade Center's own excavation, in the 1960s, provided the earth for the next extension: Battery Park City, which you can see to the west.

Underneath the plaza today run PATH trains, subway lines, and the corridors that connect them all to the Oculus. The site is less a piece of ground than a deep, layered machine.
=== DEEPER Reading the New Towers | architecture
The new World Trade Center was planned around a master plan by the architect Daniel Libeskind, but each of its towers has a different designer, and many of them carry symbols worth knowing.

Start with One World Trade Center, by David Childs of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill. Its square footprint is about the same size as each of the original towers. As it rises, its corners are cut back, so the square at the bottom twists into a square at the top turned forty five degrees, and the sides become eight tall triangles. The roof is at one thousand three hundred sixty eight feet, the height of the original North Tower. The edges of the parapet around the roof mark both that height and one thousand three hundred sixty two feet, the height of the South Tower. The spire takes it the rest of the way to one thousand seven hundred seventy six. The bottom of the tower is a windowless, fortified base about twenty stories tall, wrapped in glass fins to soften it, a reminder of how security shaped the whole project.

Seven World Trade Center, also by Childs, was the first building to be rebuilt, in 2006. Three World Trade Center, with the bold diagonal steel bracing on its outside, is by Richard Rogers's firm. Four World Trade Center, the quiet, reflective tower facing the memorial, is by the Japanese architect Fumihiko Maki, designed to almost disappear into the sky.

Look for the small, domed white church at the edge of Liberty Park. It's the new St. Nicholas Greek Orthodox Church and National Shrine, designed by Santiago Calatrava. The original St. Nicholas, a tiny parish church in a former house, was crushed by the falling South Tower. The new one opened in 2022 and glows at night.

And the cube of pale marble is the Perelman Performing Arts Center, which opened in 2023. Its marble is thin enough that, after dark, the building glows from within like a lantern.
=== DEEPER Trees, Names and Liberty Park | look
Look at the trees on the memorial plaza. Most of them are swamp white oaks, close to four hundred of them, chosen from nurseries within a few hundred miles of New York, and also near Washington and near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, the other places where the planes came down on September 11th. They are planted in rows that line up from some angles and dissolve from others, so the grove keeps changing as you walk through it.

The Survivor Tree, the Callery pear, stands out because it's different: its bark, lower on the trunk, is still rough and marked where it was burned. Look for the new growth, smoother and lighter, above the old wound.

At the pools, run your hand along the bronze parapets where the names are cut. Visitors often press paper over a name and rub it with a pencil. Look among the names for the phrase "and her unborn child." It appears after the names of several pregnant women who were killed, at the request of their families.

Then walk to Liberty Park, the elevated garden on the south side of the site, built on top of the security center for vehicles. On its terrace is Fritz Koenig's Sphere, which you may have heard about at Battery Park; it was moved here in 2017, to stand once again within sight of where it was first installed. The large bronze horseman nearby is the America's Response Monument, often called the Horse Soldier. It honors the Special Forces soldiers who went into Afghanistan on horseback in the weeks after the attacks. And along the park's south side, look for a long wall covered in greenery, planted to soften the security building below.

Finally, look back at the Oculus. When it opened, critics argued about whether it looked like a bird taking flight or a stegosaurus. Calatrava's sketches show a child releasing a dove.
=== STOP St. Paul's Chapel
=== DEEPER Inside and Around the Chapel | look
If the chapel is open, step inside, and look past the altar at the large gilded sunburst above it. It shows Mount Sinai wrapped in clouds and rays of light, with the tablets of the Ten Commandments, and it is called the Glory. It was designed by Pierre L'Enfant, the French engineer who had served in the Revolution and who would go on to lay out the plan of Washington, D. C.

The Glory has a practical origin. Outside, on the Broadway side of the chapel, the Continental Congress had commissioned a memorial to General Richard Montgomery, killed leading the American attack on Quebec on the last day of 1775. It was the first monument the United States ever ordered. Benjamin Franklin arranged for a French sculptor to carve it, and it was installed against the chapel's east wall in the late 1780s. But its back blocked the chapel's great east window. L'Enfant's sunburst was designed, in part, to hide it. In 1818, Montgomery's remains were brought from Quebec and placed in a vault beneath the monument. Look for it under the portico on the Broadway side.

Back inside, find Washington's pew, and the pew of George Clinton, the first governor of New York. Notice the cut glass chandeliers, and the white and pale colors of the interior, which make the space feel brighter than a typical Gothic church. This is a Georgian church, all classical proportion and light.

Outside, in the churchyard, look for the tall obelisk near Broadway. It marks Thomas Addis Emmet, an Irish revolutionary who fled to New York and became one of its leading lawyers and the state's attorney general. And near the back of the chapel, look for a bell in a small frame. It is the Bell of Hope, a gift from the City of London, cast at the same London foundry that made the Liberty Bell and Big Ben. It is rung each September 11th.
=== DEEPER The Occupied City | history
St. Paul's survived a great fire in 1776, and it is worth understanding what kind of city that fire burned.

In the summer of 1776, the British arrived with one of the largest military and naval forces ever sent across an ocean, hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of soldiers. Washington's army was beaten on Long Island at the end of August, escaped across the East River at night, and gave up Manhattan within weeks. On September 21st, a few days after the British marched in, fire broke out near Whitehall and swept up the west side of the city, burning perhaps a quarter of it, including Trinity Church. The British believed patriots had set it. That has never been proven.

The very next day, the British hanged a young American captain they had caught spying, Nathan Hale. The exact spot is unknown, but it was in open country well uptown, probably on the East Side, perhaps near today's Sixty Sixth Street. Whether he really said, I only regret that I have but one life to lose for my country, is not certain. The famous line comes from an American officer's account, published decades later, of what a British officer told him.

For the next seven years, New York was the British headquarters in America and a crowded, half-burned garrison town. Loyalists poured in from the countryside. So did people escaping slavery, because the British promised freedom to those who left patriot masters. American prisoners of war suffered terribly in improvised jails, in old sugar warehouses downtown, and above all in prison ships moored in Wallabout Bay in Brooklyn, where thousands died.

The British finally left on November 25th, 1783, Evacuation Day, celebrated in New York for more than a century afterward. The story goes that they left the Union Jack nailed to a flagpole at the Battery, with the cleats removed and the pole greased, and a young sailor had to climb it to take it down. And when they left, they took thousands of formerly enslaved Black Loyalists with them, to freedom in Nova Scotia, refusing American demands to return them.
=== DEEPER The Capital That Got Away | politics
For about a year and a half, this small corner of Manhattan was the seat of the federal government, and it's worth knowing how New York lost it.

Washington lived first in a house on Cherry Street, near where the Brooklyn Bridge approach now runs, and then in a larger mansion on Broadway, south of Trinity. Congress met at Federal Hall. The Supreme Court held its first sessions in the Royal Exchange at the foot of Broad Street. Jefferson, the first Secretary of State, rented a house on Maiden Lane.

The big fight of 1790 was over debt. Alexander Hamilton, the Treasury Secretary, wanted the federal government to take over the war debts of the states. That would establish the nation's credit, and it would also bind wealthy creditors to the new government. But Southern states, especially Virginia, had paid off much of their own debt and resented paying for everyone else's. The plan was stuck.

So was the question of a permanent capital. Northerners wanted it in the North, Southerners on the Potomac. In June 1790, Jefferson invited Hamilton and James Madison to dinner at his house on Maiden Lane. Out of that evening, or so Jefferson later described it, came a deal: Madison would let Hamilton's debt plan pass, and Hamilton would round up votes to put the permanent capital on the Potomac, with Philadelphia as a temporary capital for ten years. Historians argue about how much was really settled over that dinner, as opposed to in many other conversations. But both bills passed that summer.

In August 1790, Congress left New York for Philadelphia, and Washington never lived here again. New York lost its role as the political capital. It would have to settle for becoming the capital of almost everything else.
