=== STOP Grand Army Plaza & the Arch
=== DEEPER The Grand Army | names
The plaza takes its name from the Grand Army of the Republic, the great organization of Union veterans of the Civil War.

It was founded in 1866, the year after the war ended, and it grew into one of the most powerful political forces in the country. At its peak, around 1890, it had hundreds of thousands of members, with posts in nearly every town in the North. Its members campaigned for veterans' pensions and for the care of old soldiers, and several of them became president.

The Grand Army also gave the country a holiday. In 1868, its commander, General John Logan, issued an order setting aside May 30th as a day to decorate the graves of the Union dead with flowers. It was called Decoration Day. Over the years it grew to honor Americans who died in all the nation's wars, and it became Memorial Day.

The arch in front of you went up in the Grand Army's heyday, when Brooklyn was full of men who had served in the war. The plaza itself was renamed for the Grand Army years later, as its members were dying off. Manhattan has a Grand Army Plaza too, at the southeast corner of Central Park, named for the same veterans.

The last surviving member of the Grand Army, Albert Woolson, who had enlisted as a drummer boy, died in 1956, at the age of one hundred six. With him, the Grand Army of the Republic came to an end. Its name lives on here, at one of Brooklyn's busiest crossroads.
=== DEEPER Brooklyn's Own Sculptor | people
The chariot on top of the arch, and the great bronze groups of the Army and the Navy on its sides, are the work of Frederick MacMonnies, whose winged figures on the Washington Square Arch and fountains at the New York Public Library you may have heard about on other tours. MacMonnies was a Brooklyn boy.

He was born in Brooklyn in 1863. As a teenager, he went to work in the studio of Augustus Saint-Gaudens, the leading American sculptor of the day, and learned the trade there. Then he went to Paris, where he spent most of his career.

His first big success at home stands in City Hall Park in Manhattan: a statue of Nathan Hale, the young American spy, his arms bound, about to be hanged.

Then came a scandal. His Bacchante, a nude, laughing woman dancing with a baby on one arm and a bunch of grapes held high, was given to the new Boston Public Library by its architect, Charles McKim, in 1896. Proper Boston was horrified by the tipsy, naked figure, and the statue was sent away. McKim gave it to the Metropolitan Museum of Art instead, where it has been admired ever since.

Late in his career, MacMonnies made one more statue that caused an uproar. Civic Virtue, unveiled near City Hall in 1922, showed a muscular nude youth standing over two writhing women, the sirens of vice. Women's groups protested that it showed a man trampling women. It was moved out to Queens in 1941, and in 2012 it moved again, to Green-Wood Cemetery, here in Brooklyn, where it stands today.

So the triumphal arch at the gate of Brooklyn's great park is crowned by the work of a Brooklyn-born artist who was never afraid of a little scandal.
=== DEEPER The Library Built Twice | architecture
You heard that the Central Library, across the plaza, was completed in 1941. It was begun almost thirty years earlier, to a completely different design.

The first plan, by the architect Raymond Almirall, was a grand Beaux-Arts palace, with columns and domes, in the spirit of the arch and the Brooklyn Museum. Work began before the First World War. But money ran short, and construction stopped with only part of one wing built, along Flatbush Avenue. For years, the unfinished hulk sat at the edge of the plaza.

In the 1930s, the library turned to new architects, Alfred Morton Githens and Francis Keally. They built on what was already there, but threw out the Beaux-Arts design. In its place they created the building you see: smooth pale stone, almost no ornament, and a facade that curves with the plaza, with nearly all the decoration gathered around the entrance.

The result is one of the city's great buildings of its era, part classical temple, part modern machine. Look at how the walls rise in plain, flat planes, and how the tall entrance, with its gilded figures, glows against them. It's as if the architects saved all their ornament for the one place where you walk in.

=== DEEPER The Painting and the Mayor | politics
In the fall of 1999, the Brooklyn Museum found itself at war with the mayor of New York.

The museum was showing Sensation, an exhibition of young British artists from the collection of the advertising executive Charles Saatchi. One work set off the fight: The Holy Virgin Mary, by Chris Ofili, a painting of a Black Madonna, adorned with a lump of elephant dung and surrounded by tiny collaged pictures cut from pornographic magazines.

Mayor Rudolph Giuliani, who hadn't seen the show, called it sick and disgusting. He announced that unless the museum canceled the exhibition, the city would cut off its funding and try to evict it from its building, which the city owns.

The museum refused, and took the city to federal court. In November 1999, a judge ruled that the city had violated the First Amendment by punishing the museum for the content of an exhibition, and ordered the funding restored.

Ofili, who was raised Catholic, said the dung, a material he used in many of his paintings, was not meant as an insult. In December, a visitor slipped behind the painting's protective barrier and smeared white paint across it. It was cleaned and went back on view.

All the controversy did what controversy usually does. Crowds lined up around the block to see the show.
=== DEEPER Carnival on the Parkway | culture
Every Labor Day, Eastern Parkway, the boulevard in front of the museum, becomes the stage for one of the largest street festivals in North America, the West Indian American Day Carnival.

Caribbean New Yorkers began holding carnival celebrations in Harlem in the 1940s, carrying on the pre-Lenten carnival of Trinidad and other islands. In the late 1960s, as the Caribbean community grew in Crown Heights and Flatbush, the festival moved to Brooklyn, onto the wide parkway that Olmsted and Vaux designed.

Today the crowds number in the hundreds of thousands, and by some counts more than a million. Masqueraders dance down the parkway in enormous costumes of feathers and sequins, some so big they ride on wheels, behind trucks stacked with speakers booming soca and calypso. The air smells of jerk chicken, curry goat and roti from the stands along the route.

Before dawn that morning comes a second, wilder celebration, whose name comes from the French for daybreak. Revelers covered in paint, powder and mud dance through the streets to steel bands until the sun comes up.

The steel pan, made from an oil drum, was invented in Trinidad in the twentieth century, and it's the national instrument there. In the days before the parade, some of the best steel bands in New York, many of them with dozens of players, compete in a contest called Panorama, on the grounds behind the museum.
=== DEEPER Cherry Season | nature
You heard that the Botanic Garden's cherry trees burst into bloom each spring. The show lasts for weeks, because the garden grows dozens of kinds, and they take turns.

The earliest open in late March. Next come the Yoshino cherries, with clouds of single, pale pink flowers that fade almost to white, the same kind that ring the Tidal Basin in Washington. Among the last are the Kanzan cherries, with heavy, ruffled double flowers of deep pink, which bloom along the Cherry Esplanade, usually in late April.

Most of these trees produce little or no fruit worth eating. They were bred in Japan, over centuries, for their flowers alone.

In Japan, the custom of hanami, flower viewing, goes back more than a thousand years. People gather under the blossoms to picnic, drink and write poems, and the brief life of the flowers became a symbol of the beauty of passing things.

The exact timing depends on the weather. A warm March brings the blossoms early, and a cold spring holds them back, so the garden tracks the bloom tree by tree on its website.

Scientists watch those dates closely too. In Kyoto, records of the blossoms go back more than a thousand years, to court diaries and poems, and in recent decades the trees there have bloomed earlier than at almost any time in that long record, a sign of a warming climate.
=== STOP The Long Meadow
=== DEEPER Where the Ice Stopped | nature
As you may have heard on the Central Park North tour, the last ice age scraped its way across Manhattan. Here in Brooklyn is where it stopped, and the park's hills are what it left behind.

About twenty thousand years ago, a sheet of ice, in places more than a thousand feet thick, covered New England and New York down to about here. As it crept south, it scraped up rock, sand and soil and pushed them along like a giant bulldozer. Where the ice reached its limit and began to melt, it dropped that load, building a long, rough ridge of sand, gravel, clay and boulders. Geologists call it a terminal moraine. This one, the Harbor Hill Moraine, runs across Staten Island, through Brooklyn and Queens, and out along the spine of Long Island.

You're standing on it. The park's hills, including its highest point, Lookout Hill, and the ridge the Americans tried to defend in 1776 are all part of the moraine, and so are the hills of Green-Wood Cemetery, nearby.

South of the moraine, rivers of meltwater spread sand and gravel into a broad, flat plain, which is why Flatbush and the rest of southern Brooklyn are so level. As the ice melted away, the sea rose and flooded the lowlands, shaping the harbor and the bays around Brooklyn.

The ice also left behind boulders carried from far to the north, called erratics. Keep an eye out for big, lonely rocks around the park.

Olmsted and Vaux put all of it to work. The Long Meadow rolls over gentler ground, the Ravine cuts into the moraine's steep slopes, and the lake spreads across the flatter land to the south.
=== DEEPER The Cemetery Inside the Park | history
Somewhere in the park's woods, behind a fence, is a place few visitors ever see: a cemetery that's older than the park itself.

It's the Friends Cemetery, a burial ground of the Quakers, the Religious Society of Friends, laid out in the 1840s, when this was still farmland. When the park was created, the cemetery was allowed to stay, and the park grew up around it.

It's still a working cemetery, run by the Quakers, and it's closed to the public, though the park has occasionally offered tours. In keeping with the Quaker tradition of plainness, the gravestones are small, simple and much alike, with no grand monuments to show who was rich and who was poor.

Its best-known grave belongs to the actor Montgomery Clift, the star of A Place in the Sun and From Here to Eternity. Along with Marlon Brando and James Dean, Clift helped bring a new, raw and intensely emotional style of acting to the movies, and he was nominated for four Academy Awards. A car crash in 1956 scarred his face, and his health declined. He died in 1966, at forty-five.

So as you cross the meadow, remember that the park has a quiet neighbor inside its own borders, one that was here first.
=== DEEPER The Park That Nearly Fell Apart | politics
By the 1970s, Prospect Park was in trouble. The city was nearly bankrupt, and the parks department lost much of its budget and staff. Buildings were vandalized and closed, the woods were eroding, and many Brooklynites stayed away, afraid of crime.

In 1980, the city appointed Tupper Thomas as the park's first administrator, a new job created so that one person would be responsible for the whole park. She stayed for more than thirty years.

In 1987, she and a group of supporters founded the Prospect Park Alliance, a private nonprofit that raises money from neighbors, foundations and businesses, and works with the city to care for the park. The model came from Manhattan, where the Central Park Conservancy had shown, a few years earlier, how private money could help bring a public park back.

The Alliance and the city have since restored much of the park, from the Boathouse to the woods of the Ravine, and today the park counts millions of visits a year.

The arrangement has its critics. They point out that parks in wealthy neighborhoods can raise far more private money than parks in poor ones, and they worry that this widens the gap between them. Supporters answer that without the Alliance, this park might never have recovered.
=== STOP The Battle of Brooklyn & Lefferts House
=== DEEPER The Night March | legends
You heard that the British slipped around the American lines through the Jamaica Pass, guided by local loyalists. The best-known account of that night comes from a boy.

On the evening of August 26th, 1776, some ten thousand British soldiers set off quietly from Flatlands, heading east and then north through the darkness, with General William Howe and General Henry Clinton at their head. Near the pass stood a tavern called Howard's Half-Way House. In the small hours of the morning, British officers woke the tavern keeper, William Howard, and his teenage son, and ordered them to guide the army through the hills.

Years later, the son told the story. General Howe, he said, asked for a glass of liquor at the bar, and then told his father that he had no choice: he must show them the way. They led the column along a narrow road through the pass. There, the British found only five young American officers on horseback, keeping watch, and captured them without a shot.

By morning, the British were behind the American lines. At about nine o'clock, signal guns fired, and the attack came from front and rear at once.

How much of the Howard story is exactly right, no one can say. The son was a boy at the time, and he told it many years later. But the night march itself is well documented, and it won the battle.
=== DEEPER Enslaved in Flatbush | history
You heard that the Lefferts family, like many Dutch families in Brooklyn, held people in slavery. Kings County, as Brooklyn was called, was one of the strongholds of slavery in the North.

In the first federal census, in 1790, roughly a third of the people living in Kings County were enslaved, a larger share than anywhere else in New York State. On the farms of Flatbush, many, perhaps most, white households held at least one enslaved person.

They did much of the work of these farms: plowing, planting and harvesting, cooking and cleaning, and driving wagons of produce to the ferry for the markets of Manhattan. Their names survive mostly as first names in wills, account books and census lists.

The war offered some of them a way out. The British promised freedom to enslaved people who escaped from rebel masters to their lines, and when the British army left New York in 1783, it carried away thousands of Black Loyalists, men, women and children, some of them from Brooklyn, to new lives in Nova Scotia and beyond.

The Lefferts household was among them, and the museum in the house works to tell their stories as fully as the records allow.

As you may have heard on the East Village tour, New York ended slavery slowly, and the last enslaved New Yorkers were freed only in 1827. Afterward, free Black Brooklynites built communities of their own. The best known, Weeksville, which you may have heard about on the Central Park North tour, grew up about two miles east of here.
=== DEEPER Cookies and Coleslaw | food
Some of the most American foods have Dutch names, and on a farm like the Lefferts', you might have eaten them all.

The Dutch called a little cake a koekje, and New Yorkers turned it into cookie. Their cabbage salad, koolsla, became coleslaw. Wafels became waffles, and crullers, twisted strips of fried dough, take their name from a Dutch word for a curl. The Dutch also fried balls of sweet dough in hot fat and called them olykoeks, oily cakes, among the ancestors of the American doughnut.

The farms of Flatbush fed the growing city. They grew wheat, corn and rye, potatoes and cabbages, and their produce went by wagon to the Brooklyn ferries and across the river to Manhattan's markets.

Dutch ways lasted long after English rule began. Families in Flatbush spoke Dutch at home into the 1800s, kept Dutch Bibles, and worshipped at the Flatbush Reformed Dutch Church, about a mile south of here, whose congregation dates to 1654.

The Dutch left words beyond the kitchen, too. The stoop of a Brooklyn brownstone comes from stoep, and boss comes from baas, master. So when you eat a cookie or a spoonful of coleslaw, you're speaking a little Dutch, the language these farmhouses knew.
=== STOP The Boathouse, the Lake & the Ravine
=== DEEPER Birds on the Flyway | nature
Prospect Park is one of the best places in New York to watch birds, and the busiest seasons are spring and fall.

The park lies along the Atlantic Flyway, one of the great migration routes of North America. Each spring, millions of birds fly north along the coast by night, and at dawn, tired and hungry, they drop down wherever they can find trees, water and insects. In a city of concrete, Prospect Park's woods are an oasis. In May, the Ravine and the shores of the Lullwater can fill with warblers, tiny songbirds in yellows, oranges and blues, on their way from Central and South America to forests farther north. More than two hundred kinds of birds have been seen in the park.

In winter, ducks arrive from the north and gather on the lake, and red-tailed hawks hunt over the meadows all year.

Not every bird has been welcome. In January 2009, a flock of Canada geese knocked out both engines of an airliner leaving LaGuardia Airport, and its pilot glided it down onto the Hudson River, where everyone aboard survived. Afterward, officials set out to reduce the number of geese near the city's airports. Early one morning in July 2010, federal wildlife workers rounded up several hundred geese on Prospect Park's lake, and killed them.

Many Brooklynites were outraged. They held vigils by the lake, and some argued that geese in a park miles from the runways posed little danger. The debate over how to share the city with its geese still goes on.
=== DEEPER The Elm and the Poet | culture
Near the Boathouse, look for a strange tree: low, gnarled and weeping, its twisted branches spreading like a great umbrella over a knotted trunk. It's the Camperdown Elm, planted here in 1872, and it was saved by a poem.

Every Camperdown elm descends from a single odd tree found in Scotland in the early 1800s, on the estate of the Earl of Camperdown, near Dundee: a wych elm whose branches grew down and sideways instead of up. Gardeners copy it by grafting branches onto the upright trunk of an ordinary elm, which is why the tree looks like an upside-down umbrella.

By the 1960s, like much of the park, the tree was in bad shape: rotting, cracked and neglected. Then the poet Marianne Moore came to its defense. She was one of the great poets of her century, with a Pulitzer Prize to her name, and she was known around town for her tricorn hat and black cape. Moore lived in Brooklyn for decades, in Fort Greene, and she loved the borough, its parks and, famously, the Brooklyn Dodgers. In 1967 she published a poem called The Camperdown Elm, in The New Yorker, praising the tree's strange beauty and warning that it was dying. She called it our crowning curio, and said that we must save it.

The poem worked. Admirers raised money, tree surgeons filled its cavities and braced its limbs, and the elm has been cared for ever since. It's still here, more than a century and a half after it was planted.
=== DEEPER Concrete Before It Was Modern | architecture
You heard that the Cleft Ridge Span, near the lake, is one of the earliest concrete structures in the country. In the 1870s, concrete was still a new and experimental building material in America, and this bridge was built with a French version of it, called Béton Coignet, after François Coignet, the industrialist who promoted it.

The builders cast it in molds, like a cake, and the molds gave the blocks their patterns. Ornament that would have taken a stone carver weeks could be turned out again and again.

The American company that sold the material, the New York and Long Island Coignet Stone Company, built its own headquarters near the Gowanus Canal in 1872 and 1873, out of the same stuff, as a kind of advertisement. It's one of the oldest concrete buildings in the city. It still stands, at Third Avenue and Third Street, restored and protected as a landmark beside a supermarket, and you can see it if you walk on toward the canal.

A century and a half later, it's hard to imagine a city without concrete. This modest bridge, and that little building in Gowanus, are among the places where New York's concrete age began.
=== STOP Park Slope Brownstones
=== DEEPER What Went Wrong in the Sky | history
You heard that two airliners collided over Staten Island on December 16th, 1960, and that one of them fell here. Here's how investigators worked out what happened.

It was a gray, snowy morning, and both planes were flying through clouds, guided by radio beacons and by air traffic controllers. The United jet, a new DC-8 from Chicago, was bound for Idlewild, now Kennedy Airport. The TWA plane, a propeller-driven Super Constellation from Ohio, was headed for LaGuardia.

Controllers had told the United jet to stop at a point in the sky and circle there until it was cleared to land. It never stopped. The jet flew on, miles beyond that limit, into the path of the TWA plane. In Park Slope, its wreckage set fire to a row of buildings, including the Pillar of Fire Church, and firefighters fought the flames for hours in the snow.

Why? Early in the flight, the United crew had reported that one of their two navigation radio receivers wasn't working, and the airline later argued that a ground beacon the crew relied on was unreliable. The government's Civil Aeronautics Board put the blame on the flight itself: it had gone beyond its clearance limit and out of the airspace it had been given.

The investigators had a new tool. The United jet carried a flight data recorder, a sealed box that logged its speed, altitude and heading. This crash is remembered as the first in which investigators relied on a recorder like that to work out what happened. It helped show how far, and how fast, the jet had gone. Today such recorders are required on airliners. They're called black boxes, though they're painted bright orange, so they can be found in the wreckage.
=== DEEPER The Gum That Built a Mansion | companies
A few blocks from here, on Eighth Avenue at the corner of Carroll Street, stands a grand Romanesque mansion of brownstone and brick, built in 1888 for Thomas Adams Junior. The money behind it came from chewing gum.

The story begins on Staten Island, with an exiled Mexican general, Antonio López de Santa Anna, the man who had led Mexico's army at the Alamo. Living there in the late 1860s, Santa Anna had a supply of chicle, the dried sap of the sapodilla tree, which people in Mexico and Central America had chewed for centuries. He hoped to sell it as a cheap substitute for rubber. An inventor named Thomas Adams experimented with it for months, trying to turn it into rubber goods, and failed.

Then, as the story goes, Adams remembered that the stuff was meant for chewing. He boiled the chicle, rolled it into little balls, and sold them in drugstores. Chewing gum, made until then mostly from spruce resin or paraffin wax, had found its ideal material. Adams went on to make Black Jack, a licorice-flavored gum, and Tutti-Frutti, sold from vending machines on the platforms of New York's elevated trains. In 1899, his company joined other gum makers to form the American Chicle Company, whose brands later included Chiclets. Santa Anna never saw a fortune from it. He went home to Mexico, and died there, poor, in 1876.

His son, Thomas Adams Jr., helped run the business, and built the mansion on the proceeds. Its architect was C. P. H. Gilbert, whose houses on Montgomery Place you heard about.
=== DEEPER Working for Your Groceries | food
You heard that members of the Park Slope Food Coop work shifts in exchange for the right to shop there. Here's how that works.

Every adult member works a shift of two hours and forty-five minutes, once every four weeks: unloading trucks, stocking shelves, bagging nuts and grains from bulk bins, running a register, or cleaning. Only members may shop, and there are many thousands of them. Because the members supply most of the labor, the coop can sell its food for much less than ordinary stores.

The coop takes its rules seriously. Miss a shift, and you owe make-up shifts, and if you fall far enough behind, you can lose your shopping privileges until you've caught up.

It's also famous for its democracy. Members debate everything, from plastic bags to boycotts, at monthly general meetings, and in the coop's own newspaper, the Linewaiters' Gazette.

The shelves lean toward organic produce, much of it from farms in the region. So the next time you see a Park Slope neighbor carrying groceries home, it may be someone who stocked those very shelves.
=== STOP The Old Stone House & the Gowanus Canal
=== DEEPER The Lord Who Wasn't | people
You heard that the Maryland soldiers charged under Lord Stirling. He was an American general with a Scottish title that Britain never recognized.

He was born William Alexander, in New York City, in 1726, the son of a Scottish-born lawyer and a successful merchant. He grew rich in trade, and in supplying British troops in the French and Indian War. He believed he was the rightful heir to a dormant Scottish title, the Earl of Stirling, and sailed to Britain to claim it. A Scottish jury agreed, but the House of Lords refused to recognize the claim. He came home and called himself Lord Stirling anyway, and most Americans went along. He lived like a lord, too, on a grand estate in Basking Ridge, New Jersey, and ran up enormous debts.

When the Revolution came, he sided with the patriots and became a general. At the Battle of Brooklyn, he commanded the American right, along the Gowanus road. When the British flank march trapped his men, he saw that their only escape was across the Gowanus marsh, and he led the Marylanders forward against the British at this house to buy them time. When the attacks failed, he reportedly refused to surrender to the British, and gave his sword instead to the Hessian commander, General von Heister.

He was exchanged for a British prisoner, went back to war, and served with distinction at Trenton and at Monmouth. He died in Albany in January 1783, a few months before the peace treaty was signed.
=== DEEPER Looking for the Marylanders | underfoot
You heard that most of the Maryland Four Hundred were killed or captured here. Where the dead were buried is still a mystery.

They belonged to the First Maryland Regiment, among the best-drilled troops in the army. Their colonel, William Smallwood, was away in Manhattan that day, sitting on a court-martial, so Major Mordecai Gist led them into the fight.

After the battle, the fallen were buried hastily, near where they fell. According to tradition, the Marylanders were laid in six long trenches dug in a farm field, in an area that later became Third Avenue, just a few blocks west of here. One local historian has spent years combing old records and maps, and by his count, more than two hundred fifty Marylanders may lie there.

Since then, the ground has been farmed, filled, paved and built over, with houses, factories, garages and warehouses. In 2017, before a lot on 9th Street, between Third and Fourth Avenues, could become a school, the state ordered an archaeological dig. Archaeologists found a well, a cistern, a privy and part of the brick wall of an old farmhouse, along with nineteenth century trash, but no soldiers.

The searchers haven't given up. They've called for ground-penetrating radar to look under the pavement nearby, and for care whenever the ground in the area is opened up. So far, the trenches have not been found.

The Marylanders do have a monument: a column in Prospect Park, at the foot of Lookout Hill, put up in their honor in the 1890s.

So as you walk toward the canal, remember that somewhere under these streets, perhaps beneath your feet, lie some of the first soldiers to die for the newly declared United States.
=== DEEPER Buttermilk, Oysters and a Whale | legends
Gowanus has collected a few good stories over the years.

Start with the name. It's usually said to come from Gouwane, a Lenape leader who lived along the creek when the Dutch arrived.

Then the oysters. In colonial times, Gowanus oysters were famous, and they were said to be shipped across the Atlantic as a delicacy. Some accounts claim they grew as big as dinner plates. The size may have grown in the telling, but the oysters were real, and celebrated, until pollution and overharvesting wiped out the beds.

The water that the old flushing tunnel pumps into the canal comes from Buttermilk Channel, between Brooklyn and Governors Island, and that name has two stories. In one, the channel was once so shallow that at low tide, cows could wade across it to graze on the island. In the other, farm women rowing milk to the Manhattan markets found that the rough water had churned it into buttermilk by the time they landed. Neither has been proven, and both have been told for a very long time.

And one story from our own time is entirely true. In April 2007, a young minke whale swam up the Gowanus Canal, one of the most polluted waterways in the country. Crowds gathered on the bridges, and New Yorkers named it Sludgie. Rescuers hoped it would find its way back out to sea, but it died before it could get out.
