=== STOP The Empire State Building
=== DEEPER Al Smith's Consolation Prize | politics
You heard that Al Smith had just lost the 1928 presidential election when he took on this building. The two facts are connected.

Smith, the Lower East Side boy you may have heard about at Foley Square, had become a four-term governor of New York and, in 1928, the Democratic nominee for president. His campaign chairman was John J. Raskob. Smith was buried in a landslide by Herbert Hoover, after a campaign full of attacks on his religion, and he came home without a job. Raskob and his longtime partner, Pierre du Pont, gave him one: president of the company building the tallest tower in the world. Smith was its public face, the handshakes and the speeches. On opening day, two of his grandchildren cut the ribbon, and it was Hoover, the man who had beaten him, who pressed the button in Washington to switch on the lights.

Now a story, told later and probably polished in the retelling. Raskob is said to have pulled a thick pencil from a drawer, stood it on its end, and asked the architect, William Lamb, Bill, how high can you make it so that it won't fall down? True or not, the building does look a little like a pencil.

Smith's politics took a turn in this tower's early years. He had urged Franklin Roosevelt to run for governor in 1928, but he turned bitterly against the New Deal. In 1934, Raskob and the du Pont family helped found the American Liberty League to fight it, and Smith became one of its loudest voices, attacking his old ally's program in a famous speech in 1936. That fall, Roosevelt was reelected in one of the biggest landslides in American history.

Smith died in 1944. His name lives on in the Al Smith Dinner, a Catholic charity dinner held each fall, where in many election years both presidential candidates have shown up in white tie to roast each other, and themselves.
=== DEEPER The Men on the Beams | people
You heard the steel went up at about four and a half stories a week. Here are some of the men who put it there.

In 1930, the builders hired Lewis Hine to photograph the work. Hine was a former schoolteacher who had spent years photographing children at work in mills, mines and factories for the National Child Labor Committee, pictures that helped build the case for child labor laws. Now he turned his camera on the men building the tallest tower in the world, and he went up on the steel with them. His best-known picture from the job, called Icarus, shows a young ironworker swinging from a cable with the city far below. Hine later gathered many of these images in a book called Men at Work, a tribute to the skill and nerve of working people.

His pictures show how a riveting gang worked. One man heated the rivets in a small coal forge until they glowed, then tossed them with tongs to a catcher, who caught them in a tin can. Two more men set each rivet in its hole and hammered it home.

A sizable share of the ironworkers were Mohawk men from Kahnawake, near Montreal. Their tradition in high steel went back to the 1880s, when a railroad bridge was built across the Saint Lawrence River onto their land, and the bridge company hired young Mohawk men, who proved superb on the beams. Outsiders liked to say that Mohawks had no fear of heights. The ironworkers themselves have always said that's a myth: they feel it like anyone else, and work anyway. Generations of them commuted to New York for the big jobs, and their sons and grandsons later helped raise the World Trade Center.

The building came in ahead of schedule and under budget, helped by the Depression, which had pushed down the cost of labor and materials. Officially, five workers died during construction. Rumors at the time put the toll much higher, but the official count remains five.
=== DEEPER Oscar of the Waldorf | food
You heard about the Waldorf-Astoria, the grand hotel that stood here before the skyscraper. Its most famous employee wasn't a chef.

Oscar Tschirky was a Swiss immigrant who started working in New York restaurants as a teenager, and when the Waldorf opened in 1893, he became its maître d'hôtel, the man who ran the dining rooms. He knew every regular's name, table and tastes, and the whole city came to know him simply as Oscar of the Waldorf. He stayed with the hotel for about half a century, and moved with it to Park Avenue in 1931.

His most famous creation is a salad. According to the usual story, Oscar introduced it at a grand charity ball at the hotel in 1896, and it appeared in a cookbook he published that same year. The original Waldorf salad was very simple: diced apples and celery, bound with good mayonnaise. The walnuts came later, added by other cooks, and stuck.

The Waldorf turns up in another food story you may have heard at Delmonico's, in the Financial District. In one version of the origin of eggs Benedict, a stockbroker with a hangover ordered toast, poached eggs, bacon and hollandaise at the Waldorf in 1894, and Oscar liked the idea enough to put a version of it on the menu. Like most famous food stories, it was told decades after the fact.

Oscar's real art was hospitality, and the hotel was built for it. A long corridor joined the two halves of the Waldorf-Astoria, and well-dressed New Yorkers paraded up and down it to see and be seen. It's said to have been nicknamed Peacock Alley, and the new Waldorf on Park Avenue named a restaurant after it.

So as you look up at the Empire State Building, picture the dining rooms that stood here first, where a waiter from Switzerland decided what society ate, and gave America a salad that's still on menus a hundred and thirty years later.
=== STOP Herald Square & Macy's
=== DEEPER The Owl-Mad Publisher | people
You heard that James Gordon Bennett Junior sent Henry Morton Stanley to find Livingstone. Here's the man himself.

Bennett inherited the Herald from his father, who had founded it and made it one of the best-selling papers in the country. The son was rich, reckless and impossible. In 1877, he was engaged to a young woman from a prominent New York family. On New Year's Day, he turned up at her family's party drunk and behaved so badly that the engagement was off. The story told for a century is that he relieved himself in the fireplace, but that part may be legend. Her brother, Frederick May, went after Bennett, and within days, the two men met for a duel at a remote spot in Delaware called Slaughter Gap. Shots were fired, nobody was hurt, and Bennett soon left for Europe.

He spent most of the rest of his life in France, running the Herald by cable, and in 1887 he founded a Paris edition, which lived on for more than a century as the International Herald Tribune. He paid for adventures as well as news. Besides Stanley's expedition, he backed an attempt to reach the North Pole aboard a ship called the Jeannette, which was crushed by the Arctic ice in 1881. Its captain and many of its crew died. He also put up trophies for races in cars, balloons and airplanes, and the Gordon Bennett balloon race is still flown today.

And he loved owls. He kept live ones, and when Stanford White designed the Herald building here, Bennett had a row of bronze owls set along its roof, with electric eyes that flashed at night. Look at the monument: two of those owls are still perched on it. Bennett even planned a tomb in the shape of a giant owl, about a hundred and twenty-five feet tall, to be designed by White. It was never built. White was shot dead in 1906, as you may have heard at Madison Square, and Bennett died in France in 1918.
=== DEEPER Where the Movies Began | culture
Stand in front of Macy's and picture a music hall. Before the store was built, part of this block held Koster and Bial's Music Hall, a popular variety house, with singers, dancers, acrobats and comics. On the night of April 23rd, 1896, the bill ended with something new.

After the live acts, the lights went down, and a projector threw moving pictures onto a large screen: six short films, each lasting about a minute. Thomas Edison's company had been selling peep-show machines, where one person at a time bent over a box to watch a tiny film. This was different. A whole audience sat in the dark and watched the same images together, larger than life. Others had projected films before, but this was the show that caught on, and the night is often remembered as the start of the movie business in America.

The machine was billed as Edison's Vitascope. In fact, it was mostly the work of a young inventor named Thomas Armat. The businessmen who bought the rights thought Edison's name would sell more tickets, and Edison agreed to manufacture it and take the credit. He was in the audience that night.

The program included dancers, a comic boxing match and waves breaking on a beach in England, a sight that seemed astonishing on a screen.

Koster and Bial's closed in 1901 to make way for Macy's. Look for a plaque on the Macy's building marking the spot. Within a decade, storefront movie theaters called nickelodeons were spreading across the city, and the music halls that had launched the movies were losing their audiences to them.
=== DEEPER Balloons Over Broadway | companies
You heard the giant balloons joined the Thanksgiving parade in 1927. They came from a puppeteer.

Tony Sarg was a marionette maker who also designed Macy's holiday window displays, filled with moving puppets that drew crowds to the glass. For the 1927 parade, he came up with huge figures filled with air, and worked with the Goodyear tire and rubber company to build them. The first star was Felix the Cat, the most famous cartoon character of the day. Sarg is said to have called his creations upside-down marionettes: instead of hanging from strings, they floated above the street, held down by handlers walking below.

Those first balloons were awkward, and handlers sometimes propped them up on poles. For 1928, Sarg switched to helium, and Macy's tried a stunt. At the end of the parade, the balloons were let go, to drift away over the city. They were built to leak slowly and come down somewhere days later, and each carried a return address. Macy's offered a reward of a hundred dollars to anyone who brought one back. It didn't go smoothly. By one account, of the first five released, two came down in the water, and a third was torn to shreds by neighbors fighting over it. The aviator Clarence Chamberlin, famous for flying across the Atlantic, once snagged a balloon in midair with his plane. The releases ended in the early 1930s.

The first parade, in 1924, had no balloons at all. It had floats, bands and live animals borrowed from the Central Park Zoo. Within a few years, the balloons had taken the animals' place.

Today, the balloons are still designed and built in Macy's own studio, and the night before the parade, crowds come to watch them being inflated near the American Museum of Natural History, a show of its own.
=== STOP The Garment District
=== DEEPER Shall We Save New York? | history
You heard the garment industry moved into this district in the 1920s. It didn't simply drift here. It was pushed.

In the early 1900s, as the industry outgrew the Lower East Side, many shops moved into new loft buildings along lower Fifth Avenue and the streets around it, and they kept creeping north. At lunchtime and at the end of the day, thousands of garment workers poured onto the sidewalks of Fifth Avenue, which the avenue's fashionable stores considered their own. The merchants decided the crowds were bad for business.

On March 5th, 1916, the Fifth Avenue Association, the merchants' organization, ran advertisements in the city's newspapers under the headline, Shall We Save New York? The ads warned that the loft factories were swallowing the shopping district, and the merchants pledged to stop buying from manufacturers who stayed in the area around them. The campaign fed into the zoning law the city passed that same year, the first comprehensive one in the country. Besides shaping the skyscrapers, as you may have heard at the start of this tour, the law divided the city into districts for homes, for business and for industry, and helped keep new factories away from the shopping blocks.

So the garment makers moved west, to the blocks beyond Sixth Avenue, which included much of the old Tenderloin, a district of saloons, dance halls and brothels. Developers put up tall new loft buildings for them, and by 1924, the lofts reached all the way to Ninth Avenue. A new garment center rose here, close to Penn Station and the department stores of Herald Square, but out of sight of Fifth Avenue's customers.

It's an early example of how zoning could be used not just for light and air, but to decide who belongs where.
=== DEEPER Pins and Needles | politics
You heard that the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union was one of the most powerful unions in the city. In 1937, it also produced a Broadway hit.

The union ran education and culture programs for its members: classes, choruses, sports teams and drama groups. It took over a small theater, renamed it the Labor Stage, and asked a young songwriter, Harold Rome, to write a musical revue. The cast was made up of union members, garment workers who rehearsed after work: cutters, basters and sewing machine operators. The show was called Pins and Needles.

It opened on November 27th, 1937, and the opening number set the tone: Sing Me a Song with Social Significance. The sketches and songs poked fun at bosses, at dictators abroad and at politicians at home. Critics liked it, and audiences kept coming.

In March 1938, the cast performed at the White House for President and Eleanor Roosevelt. The show was rewritten again and again to keep up with the news, moved to a larger Broadway house, the Windsor Theatre, in 1939, and closed in June 1940 after one thousand one hundred and eight performances, one of the longest runs Broadway had seen up to then.

The union kept singing. Decades later, as garment jobs were moving overseas, its television commercials showed workers singing Look for the Union Label, urging shoppers to buy clothes made by union members in America. By then the union was fighting a losing battle against cheaper imports. But for a few years in the 1930s, the people who worked these sewing machines were the toast of Broadway.
=== DEEPER Press Week | culture
You heard that New York is home to Fashion Week. It began with a publicist and a war.

Before the Second World War, American fashion took its cues from Paris. Manufacturers on Seventh Avenue sent buyers to the Paris collections and copied what they saw, and most American designers worked anonymously, their names hidden behind the manufacturers' labels. Then, in 1940, Germany occupied Paris, and the flow of French fashion stopped.

Eleanor Lambert saw an opening. She was a publicist who had worked with artists before turning to fashion, and she already ran the International Best-Dressed List, the annual ranking of the world's most stylish people. In 1943, she staged what she called Press Week: a series of shows of American designers' collections, at the Plaza Hotel, for fashion editors from newspapers across the country. To make sure they came, she paid for their travel. The editors went home and wrote about American designers by name, many of them for the first time. Press Week is the ancestor of today's New York Fashion Week.

Lambert spent the rest of her long life promoting American fashion. In the early 1940s, she helped create the Coty awards for American designers, and in 1962, she founded the Council of Fashion Designers of America, which still gives the industry's top American awards. The council also credits her as the force behind the Metropolitan Museum's Costume Institute benefit, today's Met Gala, and behind the Battle of Versailles, a fashion show at the palace in 1973 where, by most accounts, the Americans stole the show from the French.

In the 1990s, the shows gathered under big white tents in Bryant Park, the next stop on this walk, until they moved to Lincoln Center in 2010. Lambert kept working into her late nineties and died in 2003, at the age of one hundred.
=== STOP Bryant Park & the New York Public Library
=== DEEPER Reservoir Square's Lost Wonders | architecture
You heard about the Crystal Palace, the iron and glass hall built here for New York's world's fair in 1853. Just across 42nd Street, the fair had a rival attraction.

It was the Latting Observatory, a wooden tower about three hundred and fifteen feet tall, put up by a promoter who guessed, correctly, that fairgoers would pay to look down on the city. For three years, it was the tallest structure in New York, taller than the spire of Trinity Church. From the top, visitors could look out over farms, rocky lots and shanties to both rivers. But the fair lost money, the crowds faded, and in 1856, the tower burned.

The Crystal Palace lasted two years longer. It was supposed to be fireproof, a building of iron and glass, but it had plenty of wood in its floors and fittings, and it was full of exhibits. On October 5th, 1858, a fire broke out inside and spread with terrible speed. By one account, the whole building was gone in about forty minutes, its great dome collapsing into the flames. The cause was never settled.

For years afterward, the open ground beside the reservoir was known as Reservoir Square. It was renamed for William Cullen Bryant in 1884, a few years after his death, and when the reservoir came down to make way for the library, the park began to take the shape you see now.

So as you sit here, picture the view in 1853: a glass palace glittering in the sun, a wooden tower rising beside it, and the massive stone walls of the reservoir, at what was then the northern edge of the city.
=== DEEPER The Orphans of Fifth Avenue | history
Walk toward Fifth Avenue and look a couple of blocks north. In 1863, on Fifth Avenue between 43rd and 44th Streets, stood the Colored Orphan Asylum, a home and school for Black children. It had been founded in 1836 by two Quaker women, and by the time of the Civil War, it was home to more than two hundred children.

That July, the federal government began drafting men for the Union army. The law let a man buy his way out for three hundred dollars, a sum far beyond most working men, and anger at the draft exploded into one of the deadliest riots in American history. For four days, mobs, many of them poor Irish immigrants, attacked draft offices, police, the homes of abolitionists and, above all, Black New Yorkers, who were beaten and murdered in the streets.

On the first day, July 13th, a huge crowd surrounded the orphanage. The staff gathered the children, two hundred and thirty-three of them, and led them out a back door as rioters broke in the front, looted the building and set it on fire. The children were taken to shelter, and the building burned to the ground.

The asylum never came back to Fifth Avenue. It rebuilt uptown, first in Harlem and later in Riverdale, in the Bronx, and in 1944, it became the Riverdale Children's Association, carrying on its work with children under a new name.

It's hard to picture a mob burning an orphanage on this stretch of Fifth Avenue today. But the attack became one of the defining images of the draft riots, a reminder that the Civil War was fought in New York's own streets too.
=== DEEPER Under the Lawn | underfoot
Look at the lawn in front of you. Beneath it, and beneath the paths and the people, are the library's book stacks.

By the 1980s, the library had run out of room, and there was no space to expand on Fifth Avenue. So during the park's restoration, the lawn was dug up, and workers excavated a vast underground storage space, about thirty feet deep. The park was then rebuilt on top of it. The first level of the new stacks opened in 1991. A second, deeper level was fitted out years later and opened in the 2010s as the Milstein Research Stacks. Together, they hold millions of volumes, kept at the cool, steady temperature and humidity that old paper needs.

Down there, the books aren't shelved by subject, as they are in most libraries. They're shelved by size, which packs far more of them into the same space, and the library keeps track of exactly where each one sits.

When a reader in the Rose Main Reading Room asks for a book from the stacks, it rides up through the building on a book train, a system of small cars that run along tracks and climb between the stacks and the reading rooms. The current train went into service in 2016, replacing an older conveyor.

So the people lounging on this lawn are sitting on top of one of the great research collections in the world. In this park, the grass is a roof.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Lions and the Fountains | look
If you walk around to the library's Fifth Avenue steps, take a closer look at the two marble lions. You heard Mayor La Guardia named them Patience and Fortitude. They'd had other names before.

The lions were modeled by the sculptor Edward Clark Potter and carved by the Piccirilli Brothers, a family of Italian-born stone carvers who worked in the Bronx, from pink Tennessee marble. They were unveiled in May 1911, when the library opened. New Yorkers soon nicknamed them Lady Astor and Lord Lenox, after the library's founders, even though both lions have manes. Patience sits on the south side, to your left as you face the building, and Fortitude on the north. In December, they wear holiday wreaths.

Now look on either side of the entrance, at the two fountains set into the wall, by Frederick MacMonnies, the sculptor of the winged figures on the Washington Square Arch. One is Truth, a man seated on a sphinx, the symbol of mystery. The other is Beauty, a woman riding Pegasus, the winged horse. For about thirty years, the fountains sat dry, until a restoration in 2015 got the water flowing again.

Then look up, above the columns, at the six figures by the sculptor Paul Wayland Bartlett. They represent History, Drama, Poetry, Religion, Romance and Philosophy, a short list of what you'll find inside.
=== STOP Times Square
=== DEEPER The Smoke Ring Sign | companies
You heard that advertisers competed here to build the most spectacular signs. For a quarter of a century, the most famous one blew smoke rings.

It was the sign for Camel cigarettes, on the side of the Hotel Claridge, on Broadway, and it went up around 1941. It was the work of Douglas Leigh, a sign man who specialized in what the trade called spectaculars: signs that didn't just show a picture, but did something. This one showed a giant face, and about every four seconds, a perfect ring of what looked like smoke puffed from its mouth and drifted out over the crowd. It was really steam, pushed out in rings by a piston and a diaphragm behind the sign, a little like a drum being struck.

The face changed with the times. During the Second World War, it was a man in uniform, and over the years, others took his place. But the rings kept coming, day and night, until 1966, when the sign came down. A few years later, cigarette ads were banned from American television, though tobacco signs hung on in Times Square for decades.

Look around you at the screens that flicker and move. The idea that a sign should perform, not just sit there, runs straight from the smoking face to them.
=== DEEPER Father Duffy's War | people
You heard about the statue of Father Francis Duffy at the north end of the square. Here's who he was.

Duffy was born in 1871 in Cobourg, Ontario, in Canada, came to New York as a young man, and became a Catholic priest. When the United States entered the First World War, he went to France as chaplain of the 69th, New York's famous Irish regiment, which the army renamed the 165th Infantry. It was part of the Rainbow Division, so called because it drew units from states across the country. The regiment fought through some of the worst battles of 1918, and Duffy went with the men into the front lines, tending the wounded and burying the dead. He came home one of the most decorated chaplains in the army.

Two other men of the regiment became famous. One was William Donovan, a battalion commander nicknamed Wild Bill, who in the next war led the country's spy agency, the Office of Strategic Services. The other was the poet Joyce Kilmer, the author of Trees, who had transferred into the 69th with Duffy's help. Kilmer was killed in July 1918. When Duffy published his memoir, Father Duffy's Story, in 1919, it included a historical appendix by Kilmer.

After the war, Duffy served as pastor of Holy Cross Church, a few blocks west on 42nd Street, until he died in 1932. The statue, by Charles Keck, was dedicated in 1937, and two years later, this end of the square was named Father Duffy Square. In 1940, Hollywood told the regiment's story in the film The Fighting 69th, with Pat O'Brien as Father Duffy and James Cagney as a fictional soldier.
=== DEEPER The City's Front Yard | history
Times Square is where New York comes to celebrate the New Year. It's also where the city comes to hear big news together.

On November 7th, 1918, a wire service, the United Press, sent out a report from France that Germany had signed an armistice. It wasn't true, but nobody knew that yet. Across the country, offices emptied, whistles blew and crowds filled the streets. In New York, so much paper rained down from office windows that the wire service was reportedly made to pay for the cleanup. Four days later, on November 11th, the real armistice was signed, and the city celebrated all over again.

On August 14th, 1945, the crowds came for the end of the Second World War. People packed the square, reading the news ticker that wrapped around the Times tower, and when word of Japan's surrender came that evening, the square erupted. That day, the photographer Alfred Eisenstaedt caught a sailor bending a woman in a white uniform backward into a kiss. Published in Life magazine, it became one of the best-known photographs of the century. For decades, many people claimed to be the couple. Most researchers now identify them as George Mendonsa, a sailor on leave, and Greta Zimmer Friedman, a dental assistant who had come to America as a teenage refugee from Austria. They were strangers. Years later, Friedman said it wasn't her choice and it wasn't romantic: he simply grabbed her. The picture looks different once you know that.
=== DEEPER The Knickerbocker Door | underfoot
Under your feet is the oldest stretch of the subway, and a door to a hotel.

You heard that the first subway line opened in 1904 with a station here. It ran up the East Side to Grand Central, turned west under 42nd Street, and then headed north under Broadway. In 1918, the line was split in two and each half was extended, creating the separate East Side and West Side lines riders use today. The short crosstown piece under 42nd Street was left over, and it became the shuttle, running back and forth between Times Square and Grand Central. When you ride it, you're on the tracks of the original 1904 subway.

Now look at the southeast corner of 42nd Street and Broadway, at the red-brick building with the steep mansard roof. It opened in 1906 as the Knickerbocker Hotel, built by John Jacob Astor the Fourth, whom you may have heard about at the Empire State Building, and it was the first luxury hotel of the new Times Square. The tenor Enrico Caruso lived there, and on Armistice Day in 1918, he is said to have come to his window and led the crowd below in the Star-Spangled Banner. The hotel bar had a famous mural by Maxfield Parrish of Old King Cole. When the hotel closed, around 1920, the mural moved, and it now hangs in the King Cole Bar of the Saint Regis, another Astor hotel, on 55th Street.

The Knickerbocker had its own entrance from the subway. If you ride the shuttle, look on its Times Square platform for an old doorway with the word Knickerbocker above it. The building has been a hotel again since 2015, but that door no longer opens for guests.
=== STOP Shubert Alley & the Theater District
=== DEEPER The Brothers Who Took On the Syndicate | companies
You heard that the Shubert brothers built the largest theater empire in America. They built it by going to war.

At the turn of the century, the theater business was controlled by a combine called the Theatrical Syndicate, formed in 1896 by six men and led by the producers Marc Klaw and Abraham Erlanger. The Syndicate controlled the bookings of first-class theaters across the country. If a producer or a star wanted to tour, they played Syndicate houses on Syndicate terms, or they didn't play the big towns at all. Even the great French actress Sarah Bernhardt, touring America without the Syndicate, famously played some of her dates in a circus tent.

The Shuberts started out managing theaters in Syracuse and came to New York around 1900, leasing theaters and producing shows. Then, in 1905, Sam, the brothers' leader, died after a train wreck. According to the family's account, when Lee went to Erlanger to confirm a deal Sam had made, Erlanger refused, saying he wouldn't honor an agreement with a dead man. Lee and his younger brother, Jacob, known as J.J., set out to break the Syndicate.

They did it by building and buying theaters, in New York and in cities across the country, until stars and producers had somewhere else to go. Within about a decade, the Syndicate's grip was broken. But the Shuberts became what they had fought. They grew so dominant that in the 1950s, the federal government took them to court under the antitrust laws, and they had to sell off some of their theaters.

Lee and J.J. were famously difficult, and they quarreled with each other for decades. But between them, they ran the business for half a century.
=== DEEPER The Tapes Behind A Chorus Line | culture
You heard that A Chorus Line ran for fifteen years at the Shubert Theatre, right next to the alley. It began with a tape recorder.

At midnight on January 26th, 1974, the director and choreographer Michael Bennett gathered twenty-two Broadway dancers in a studio. They were gypsies, as chorus dancers call themselves, the performers who move from show to show without their names ever going up in lights. Bennett turned on a tape recorder and asked them to talk about their lives: how they started dancing, their families, their fears, what they'd do when they couldn't dance anymore. The session went on through the night, and there were more sessions afterward.

Those tapes became the raw material for the show. The characters on stage, dancers auditioning for a few places in a chorus line and telling their stories to an unseen director, were built largely from the real stories on the tapes, and several of the dancers who had been in the room ended up in the cast, playing versions of themselves.

The show was developed downtown at Joseph Papp's Public Theater, which borrowed about one point six million dollars to produce it. It opened there in April 1975, became the hottest ticket in town, and moved here to the Shubert that July. It won the Pulitzer Prize for drama and nine Tony Awards, and when it closed in April 1990, after six thousand one hundred and thirty-seven performances, it was the longest-running show in Broadway history, a record it held until Cats passed it in 1997.

For the Public Theater, it was a gold mine: its share came to more than thirty-eight million dollars. The dancers had signed away the rights to their stories for a dollar each, and later received a small share of the royalties. Some of them felt they had given away far more than they got.
=== DEEPER Ghost Lights and the Bishop of Broadway | legends
Theaters are full of superstitions, and Broadway has its share of ghosts. These are stories, and they're told here as stories.

Start with the ghost light. When a theater goes dark for the night, the last person out leaves a single bare bulb burning on a stand in the middle of the stage. There's a practical reason: a pitch-black stage is dangerous, and someone could walk off the edge into the orchestra pit. But theater people will also tell you the light is there for the ghosts, so they can perform when nobody's watching, or so they don't make mischief in the dark.

Now Broadway's most famous ghost. A short walk east on 44th Street, on the far side of Broadway, is the Belasco Theatre, built in 1907 and renamed in 1910 for its owner, David Belasco, a producer and director known for astonishingly realistic stage sets. He was a showman offstage too. He dressed in a dark suit with a clerical collar, and people called him the Bishop of Broadway. He kept a lavish private apartment upstairs in the theater.

Belasco died in 1931, but according to the stories, he never left. Actors and staff reported a figure in clerical dress watching from his old box, and it was said that if he appeared on an opening night, the show would be a hit. Some also reported a mysterious woman in blue. The sightings are said to have stopped around 1970, about when a famously nude revue moved into the theater, which, the joke goes, was more than the old man could take.

Nobody has ever proven any of it. But if you pass the Belasco late at night, glance up at the windows.
=== STOP Grand Central Terminal
=== DEEPER The Commodore | people
You heard that the first Grand Central was opened by Cornelius Vanderbilt in 1871. Everyone called him the Commodore, and he earned the title on the water.

Vanderbilt was born on Staten Island in 1794. As the family story goes, at sixteen he borrowed a hundred dollars from his mother to buy a sailboat, and ferried passengers and freight across the harbor. He was tireless and tough, and by his twenties he was captaining a steamboat called the Bellona, between New Jersey and New York, for an owner named Thomas Gibbons.

There was a catch. New York State had given a monopoly on steamboats in its waters to Robert Livingston and Robert Fulton, and Gibbons was running his boats in defiance of it. For years, Vanderbilt dodged the officers who came aboard to serve him with court papers, reportedly hiding in a secret compartment. The fight went to the Supreme Court, and in 1824, in Gibbons versus Ogden, the court ruled that trade between the states was for Congress to regulate, and the monopoly fell. It's still one of the most important cases on the power of the federal government, and it opened the waters to competition.

Vanderbilt built his own fleet of steamships and became one of the richest men in America. Then, late in life, he switched to railroads and took control of the New York Central. When he died in 1877, he was worth about a hundred million dollars, and he left nearly all of it to his son William. His biggest gift was a million dollars to found a university in Nashville, which was named for him: Vanderbilt University.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Mercury, the Clock and the Acorns | look
Step outside onto 42nd Street, and look up at the top of the terminal's front.

The sculpture group crowning the building is the Glory of Commerce, by the French sculptor Jules-Félix Coutan. In the middle stands Mercury, the Roman god of commerce and travelers, in his winged helmet. On one side is Hercules, though some say it's Vulcan, the god of the forge, and on the other is Minerva, the goddess of wisdom. Together, they're about forty-eight feet high and weigh some fifteen hundred tons. They were unveiled in 1914, a year after the terminal opened.

Just below Mercury is a clock thirteen feet across, made with glass from Tiffany. It's often called the largest piece of Tiffany glass in the world.

Below that, above the viaduct that carries Park Avenue traffic around the building, stands the bronze statue of Cornelius Vanderbilt. You may have heard in SoHo how it came here in 1929 from his old freight depot.

Now look for a smaller detail, and you'll find it everywhere: acorns and oak leaves, carved into the stone and worked into the metal, inside and out. The acorn was a Vanderbilt family emblem, a reminder of the old saying, great oaks from little acorns grow, a fitting motto for a fortune that, by family tradition, started with one small boat. Once you start spotting them, you won't stop.
=== DEEPER Guastavino's Vaults | architecture
You heard about the Whispering Gallery, downstairs near the Oyster Bar. Look closely at what it's made of.

The low arches there, and the vaulted ceiling of the Oyster Bar beyond, are built of thin terracotta tiles laid in a herringbone pattern. This is Guastavino tile, named for a Spanish builder, Rafael Guastavino, and his son, also named Rafael, who came to New York from Spain in the 1880s. They brought with them an old Mediterranean way of building, the tile vault, with deep roots in the region of Valencia: several layers of thin tiles set in fast-drying mortar, which can span wide spaces, needs little support while it's being built, and won't burn. In a city terrified of fire, that was a powerful selling point. In 1889, they founded the Guastavino Fireproof Construction Company.

Their vaults are all over New York. You may have heard about the tiles in the old City Hall subway station. They also built the great vaulted ceiling of the registry room at Ellis Island, where millions of immigrants were processed, after an explosion in 1916 damaged the old roof. When the room was restored decades later, only seventeen of its nearly thirty thousand tiles had to be replaced. And they built the vaults under the Queensboro Bridge, at 59th Street.

The Oyster Bar opened in 1913, a few weeks after the terminal, under those tiles. As for the whispering, the curved tile surfaces carry sound along the arch from one corner to the opposite one. Nobody is sure whether the effect was planned, or whether the builders simply discovered it once the vault was up.
=== DEEPER Track 61 | legends
Somewhere north of here, beneath Park Avenue, is a platform that has become one of New York's favorite secrets.

When the railroad buried its tracks, its train yards stretched for blocks under Park Avenue, and new buildings went up on top of them. When the new Waldorf-Astoria opened in 1931, on Park Avenue at 49th Street, it sat above part of the yards. A siding there, known as Track 61, had a platform and a freight elevator big enough to carry a car up into the hotel.

The legend says this was a secret presidential entrance. Franklin Roosevelt, who used a wheelchair and worked hard to hide it from the public, is said to have arrived in his own armored railroad car, had his automobile driven off the train and onto the elevator, and gone straight up into the Waldorf without anyone seeing him. And down on the platform, the story goes, his armored car still waits.

Here's what's documented. Roosevelt did use the siding, on October 21st, 1944, during his last campaign, arriving by train beneath the hotel. The rest is mostly myth. The old railroad car that has sat on the track for years is a baggage car, not Roosevelt's, and there's no evidence of a regular secret commute.

The legend survives because the place is real and the story is almost true. Track 61 is still down there, closed to the public, under the traffic of Park Avenue.

=== DEEPER Walter Chrysler's Locomobile | people
You heard that Walter Chrysler started out as a railroad mechanic. In a way, he never stopped being one.

Chrysler grew up in a railroad town in Kansas, the son of a locomotive engineer, and apprenticed as a machinist in the railroad shops, where, like many apprentices, he made his own tools. He moved from railroad to railroad, rising to manage locomotive works. In 1908, at an auto show in Chicago, he fell for a car, a Locomobile, that cost far more than he could afford. He put in his savings, borrowed the rest, and bought it.

Then he did something odd. He hardly drove it. For about three months, he took the car apart, studied every piece, and put it back together, again and again, until he understood exactly how it worked. That car was his education in the automobile business.

In 1912, he left the railroads, taking a big cut in pay, to run production at Buick, in Michigan, and he made its factories some of the most efficient in the industry. He became Buick's president, left General Motors a rich man, and was hired to rescue struggling carmakers, which led him to found his own company.

When his tower opened, he put his past on display. By one account, the tools he'd made as an apprentice were shown in a glass case near the observation floor, high in the building. And from 1930, three floors near the top held the Cloud Club, a private lunch club where executives ate high above the city.

Chrysler died in 1940, at sixty-five. The building that carries his name was paid for with his own money, a monument to a mechanic who never trusted a machine he hadn't taken apart himself.
=== DEEPER The Architect Who Lost His Fee | culture
You heard how William Van Alen won the race for the sky with a spire hidden inside his building. His reward was a lawsuit.

Van Alen had trained in New York and in Paris, at the École des Beaux-Arts, and he had a reputation as a daring designer. But he never signed a contract with Walter Chrysler. When the building was finished, he asked for the usual architect's fee of the day, six percent of the construction cost, which came to roughly eight hundred and forty thousand dollars. Chrysler refused to pay, and attacked Van Alen's character. Van Alen took him to court, and won.

The victory cost him. Clients were wary of an architect who had sued a famous millionaire, and the Depression had stopped most new building anyway. Van Alen never landed another major commission.

He did get one more famous moment. On January 23rd, 1931, the Society of Beaux-Arts Architects held its costume ball at the Hotel Astor, and several of the city's leading architects came dressed as their own buildings. A well-known photograph shows them lined up like a little skyline. Van Alen is dressed as the Chrysler Building, with its crown on his head, towering over the rest. William Lamb, one of the architects of the Empire State Building, came dressed as his tower, the one that would take Van Alen's title only a few months later. Others came as clubs and office buildings.

Look up at the crown again. It has outlived the lawsuit, the Depression and the loss of the height record. In the end, it's Van Alen's crown, not his fee, that people remember.
=== DEEPER Who Owns the Ground? | companies
Here's a question most people never ask about a skyscraper: who owns the ground under it?

For the Chrysler Building, the answer is a college: Cooper Union, the school founded by Peter Cooper that you may have heard about in the East Village. Cooper Union has owned this land since 1902. Walter Chrysler built his tower on it under a long-term lease, and every owner of the building since then has paid the college rent for the ground beneath it.

There's more. Because Cooper Union is a nonprofit, the land is exempt from property taxes, so the building's owners pay the college an amount equal to what the taxes would be, by one recent account more than twenty million dollars a year. For a college that offered every student a free education for more than a century, the land under this tower has been a lifeline.

It has also made owning the building a roller coaster. In 2008, an investment fund from Abu Dhabi paid eight hundred million dollars for most of the tower. But the ground rent was set to jump, and between 2017 and 2018, it rose from about seven point eight million dollars a year to thirty-two and a half million, with more increases scheduled. With rent like that, the building was worth far less. In 2019, it sold for about a hundred and fifty million dollars, a fraction of what it had fetched eleven years earlier.

The new owners struggled too. In 2024, after they fell more than twenty million dollars behind on the rent, Cooper Union moved to end the lease, and after a court fight, the college took the building back. It has since been looking for a new partner to run it.

So one of the most admired skyscrapers in the world is, in a sense, a college's endowment, with a stainless steel crown on top.
