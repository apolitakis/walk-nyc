=== DEEPER The Double House on 65th Street | politics
Walk a block south and half a block west, to East 65th Street between Park and Madison Avenues. Look for a wide, red brick townhouse with two front doors. It's really two houses built as one, and for a time it was the home of a future president.

In 1908, Sara Delano Roosevelt, a wealthy widow, had the architect Charles A. Platt design a double townhouse. She took Number 47 for herself, and gave Number 49 to her son, Franklin, and his wife, Eleanor. She also chose their furniture and decoration, and hired their household staff. The two houses were joined by doors on several floors, and Eleanor later recalled that she was never quite sure when she would be alone.

In August 1921, Franklin was struck by polio. After two months in the hospital, he insisted on coming here, rather than to the family estate at Hyde Park, and in these rooms he began the long, private struggle to rebuild his strength. Eleven years later he was elected president. In the months before he took office, this house was his base in New York while he assembled his administration.

Sara died in 1941. Hunter College, a few blocks away, acquired the house, and in November 1943 dedicated it as the Sara Delano Roosevelt Memorial House, a center for interfaith and interracial understanding. Today it's the home of Hunter's Roosevelt House Public Policy Institute.
=== DEEPER The Architect Who Broke Codes | architecture
You heard that many of the apartment buildings along Park Avenue were designed by Rosario Candela. He led a remarkable double life.

Candela was born in Sicily, came to New York as a young man, and studied architecture at Columbia. In the 1920s, as the old mansions gave way to apartment houses, he became the architect the rich trusted most. His specialty was the apartment that felt like a mansion: grand entrance galleries, high ceilings, and duplexes, fitted together inside the building like pieces of a three-dimensional puzzle. His most famous building is 740 Park Avenue, at 71st Street, where John D. Rockefeller Junior lived, and where a young Jacqueline Bouvier, the future Jacqueline Kennedy, spent part of her childhood.

In the 1930s, with the building boom over, Candela threw himself into another passion: codes. He studied the ciphers of a French army officer, Commandant Étienne Bazeries, and decrypted messages Bazeries had encoded in 1898. Starting in 1941, he taught a class in cryptography and cryptanalysis at Hunter College, a few blocks north of here, said to be the only course of its kind open to the public anywhere in the country. When the war came, he reportedly put those skills to work for the government.

So the next time you pass a Candela building, with its orderly rows of windows and its interlocking apartments hidden behind them, think of a man who saw both buildings and secret messages as puzzles to be solved.
=== DEEPER The Regiment at the Hindenburg Line | history
You heard that the Seventh Regiment was nicknamed the Silk Stocking Regiment. In the First World War, its men paid a terrible price.

When the United States entered the war, the regiment was reorganized as the 107th Infantry, part of a division made up of New York troops. On September 29, 1918, it attacked the Hindenburg Line, the great German defensive line in northern France, near the Saint Quentin Canal. Over the course of the war, the 107th counted nearly two thousand casualties: four hundred thirty-seven men killed, more than thirteen hundred wounded, and ninety-eight more who later died of their wounds. Much of that toll came in the one assault in September.

After the war, a memorial was commissioned, and the sculptor chosen was Karl Illava, who had served in the regiment as a sergeant. It was dedicated in 1927, on the ninth anniversary of the attack.

You can see it a few blocks west of here, on Fifth Avenue at 67th Street, set into the stone wall of Central Park. It shows a group of soldiers surging forward with their rifles, some of them helping wounded comrades along. The faces are specific, tired, and determined, because the man who sculpted them had seen the real thing.

The drill hall behind you, where the regiment drilled for generations, now hosts art. It's worth remembering that its first purpose was to prepare young New Yorkers for exactly this.

=== DEEPER Look Closer: More and Cromwell | look
You heard that the Frick's paintings hang as they would in a private home. Two of them hang in what may be the most pointed arrangement in any museum in New York.

In the room called the Living Hall, on either side of the fireplace, hang two portraits by Hans Holbein the Younger, the great painter of Henry the Eighth's court. On one side is Sir Thomas More, the king's chancellor, in a fur collar and a heavy gold chain of office, his face intelligent and wary. On the other is Thomas Cromwell, the king's chief minister, heavy-set and watchful, clutching a document. They face each other across the room.

In life, they were enemies. More refused to accept Henry's break with the Church of Rome, and Cromwell helped bring about his downfall. More was beheaded in 1535. Five years later, Cromwell himself fell from favor and was beheaded too. Between them, above the fireplace, hangs El Greco's portrait of Saint Jerome, a scholar and a saint, as if keeping the peace.

While you're inside, look for Frick's three paintings by Vermeer. Only about three dozen paintings by Vermeer survive, and this house holds three of them. One, Mistress and Maid, was the last painting Frick ever bought, in 1919, the year he died.
=== DEEPER Tell Him I'll See Him in Hell | legends
You heard that Frick and Carnegie had a bitter falling out. The story of how it ended is one of the most quoted lines of the Gilded Age, and it may be a legend.

The partnership had made both men enormously rich. But after Homestead it frayed, and around 1900 it collapsed in a fight over money. Carnegie tried to force Frick out of the company and buy back his share at a price Frick considered far too low. Frick took him to court, and in the end the dispute was settled with Frick still a very rich man. As far as anyone knows, the two never made peace.

Then comes the story. Years later, the tale goes, Carnegie, old and frail, sent word to Frick suggesting it was time they met and were reconciled. Frick sent back his answer: Tell him I'll see him in hell, where we both are going.

Did he really say it? The line turns up in many books, in slightly different words, and it has become more myth than record. What is certain is that Carnegie made more than one attempt at reconciliation, and that it never happened. And both men died in the same year, 1919, Carnegie in August and Frick in December.

As for the idea that Frick built this house to make Carnegie's mansion look like a miner's shack, you can judge for yourself. Carnegie's house is about twenty blocks up Fifth Avenue, at 91st Street, on this tour.
=== DEEPER The Garden That Was Saved | nature
Look at the walled garden beside the museum on East 70th Street, with its lawn, its pool, and its carefully placed trees and shrubs, seen through iron railings. It's a viewing garden, meant to be looked at, not walked in.

It was designed by Russell Page, one of the most admired English garden designers of the twentieth century, and it was created in 1977, along with a new reception pavilion, after the museum demolished three row houses on the site. Page composed it to be enjoyed from the sidewalk, in every season, like a picture in a frame.

In 2014, the Frick announced an expansion plan with a six-story addition that would have replaced the garden. Opposition grew quickly. Critics, including a former director of the museum, Everett Fahy, argued that the garden had been promised as permanent when the city's landmarks commission approved it in the 1970s. In June 2015, the Frick dropped the plan.

The museum came back with a smaller design that left the garden where it is, and the renovation you heard about went ahead. The new plan also called for the garden to be restored by Lynden Miller, the garden designer you may have heard about at the Conservatory Garden in Central Park.

It's a small victory, but a telling one. A little garden that no one is allowed to walk into turned out to be something New Yorkers would fight for.
=== STOP The Metropolitan Museum of Art
=== DEEPER The Night of the Aristotle | companies
On November 15, 1961, a crowd packed an auction room at the Parke-Bernet Galleries on Madison Avenue, a few blocks from here. The prize was Rembrandt's Aristotle with a Bust of Homer, from the collection of the late advertising executive Alfred Erickson.

The bidding was over in minutes. The Metropolitan Museum won the painting for two point three million dollars, the highest price ever paid for a painting at auction up to that time. When it went on display here, crowds lined up to see the record-breaking painting.

Look for it upstairs in the European paintings galleries. The philosopher Aristotle, in a broad black hat and white sleeves, wears a heavy golden chain across his chest, and rests his hand on a marble bust of the poet Homer. On the chain hangs a medallion that probably shows Alexander the Great, Aristotle's most famous pupil.

Rembrandt painted it in 1653, for a Sicilian nobleman who had asked for a philosopher. It's a painting about three great men, the poet, the philosopher, and the conqueror, and about what lasts. Aristotle seems to be weighing the gold on his chest against the blind poet under his hand. Three centuries later, the painting itself became a test of what a museum should pay for a masterpiece, and the Met decided it was worth the price.
=== DEEPER The Hot Pots | politics
You heard that the Met's collection spans five thousand years. Some of it, it turns out, came out of the ground illegally, and the museum has had to give it back.

In 1972, the Met bought a painted Greek vase called a krater, used for mixing wine with water, made around 515 BC and painted by Euphronios, one of the masters of Athenian vase painting. It cost about a million dollars, an astonishing sum for a vase, and the press nicknamed it the hot pot. Italian investigators believed it had been dug up illegally from an Etruscan tomb near Rome. Over the following decades the evidence piled up, and in 2008 the Met returned it to Italy.

In 2017, the Met paid about four million dollars for a gleaming gilded coffin made for an Egyptian priest named Nedjemankh, more than two thousand years ago, and built an exhibition around it. Then investigators from the Manhattan District Attorney's office showed that it had been looted from Egypt and sold with forged documents. In February 2019, the museum handed it over to be returned to Egypt, and said it had been deceived.

Those cases are part of a much larger reckoning. The district attorney's antiquities investigators have since seized many more objects from New York collectors, dealers, and museums, and museums now research where an object has been far more carefully before they buy it. Underneath it all is a question that's political as well as legal: who owns the past?
=== DEEPER Do Women Have to Be Naked? | culture
In 1989, a poster appeared that New Yorkers still quote. It showed a reclining nude, borrowed from a famous painting by Ingres, wearing a snarling gorilla mask. Its headline asked: Do women have to be naked to get into the Met Museum? Underneath, it said that less than five percent of the artists in the museum's modern art sections were women, but eighty-five percent of the nudes were female.

It was the work of the Guerrilla Girls, an anonymous group of women artists formed in New York in 1985, after the Museum of Modern Art mounted a huge survey of contemporary art in which only a small fraction of the artists were women. The members appear in public in gorilla masks, and take the names of dead women artists, like Frida Kahlo and Käthe Kollwitz. Their method is simple: they count.

The poster was designed as a billboard for a public art program, which turned it down. So the Guerrilla Girls rented advertising space on New York City buses instead, until the bus company canceled the ads.

They have gone back to count again more than once, and found the numbers stubborn. Their posters now hang in major museums around the world. As you walk through the galleries here, try counting for yourself.
=== STOP The Guggenheim & the Neue Galerie
=== DEEPER The Baroness and the Temple | people
You heard that Solomon Guggenheim was advised by an artist, the Baroness Hilla von Rebay. Without her, the building in front of you would not exist.

Rebay was a German painter from an aristocratic family. She met Guggenheim in 1927, when she was commissioned to paint his portrait, and she persuaded the copper magnate, then in his sixties, to collect what she called non-objective art: pure abstraction, which she believed could lift the human spirit. She steered him toward Kandinsky, and the collection grew. She kept painting, too, and her own abstract canvases are in the collection. In 1939, the foundation opened the Museum of Non-Objective Painting in rented space in Midtown, with Rebay as its first director.

In 1943, she and Guggenheim wrote to Frank Lloyd Wright, asking him to design a permanent home for the collection. What she wanted, she said, was a temple of the spirit. She had considered other architects, among them Walter Gropius and Le Corbusier. According to one account, she wasn't even sure Wright was still alive, and it was Guggenheim's wife, Irene, who knew better. The next year, they bought the land here on Fifth Avenue.

But after Solomon Guggenheim died in 1949, the family pushed Rebay out, and in 1952 the museum took his name. By the time Wright's building opened in 1959, the woman who had dreamed of it was no longer part of the story, and for decades her role was largely forgotten.

As you look at the spiral, remember that it began with a baroness asking for a temple.
=== DEEPER The Other Guggenheim | people
The Guggenheims were a large family, and Solomon was not the only one who collected modern art. His niece Peggy did too, in a completely different spirit.

Peggy Guggenheim was the daughter of Solomon's brother Benjamin, who went down with the Titanic in 1912. As the ship sank, he reportedly changed into evening clothes and said he was prepared to go down like a gentleman. She inherited a much smaller fortune than her uncles, moved to Europe, and threw herself into the world of the avant-garde, at one point setting out to buy a picture a day.

When the Second World War broke out, she shipped her collection to New York, and in 1942 she opened a gallery on West 57th Street called Art of This Century, with walls and lighting as unconventional as the art. There she gave a young painter named Jackson Pollock his first solo show, paid him a monthly stipend, and commissioned a huge canvas for her apartment, which he called Mural. Her gallery was a launching pad for the painters who would make New York, not Paris, the capital of the art world.

After the war, Peggy moved to Venice, to an unfinished palace on the Grand Canal, where she lived among her paintings and her little dogs until her death in 1979. Today the palace is a museum, run by the foundation named for her uncle. So in the end, the two Guggenheim collections, the uncle's and the niece's, ended up in the same family.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: The Spiral | look
You heard that the building is a single spiral ramp under a skylight. Look again, with Wright's own words in mind.

Wright called it an optimistic ziggurat. The ziggurats of ancient Mesopotamia were stepped towers that grew narrower as they rose. Wright turned the idea upside down, so the building grows wider as it climbs, opening toward the sky.

Inside, he meant the outer walls of the ramp to lean back slightly, so that paintings would lean back too, as if resting on an artist's easel. That was one of the features that angered painters, and in 1956, before the building even opened, a group of artists signed a letter of protest. Curators have been working with, and around, those walls ever since.

Now step back and look at the whole block. Along the facade, look for the museum's name, spelled out in lettering Wright designed himself. The tall, plain building rising behind the spiral is a tower added in 1992, by the architects Gwathmey Siegel, to give the museum more gallery and office space. Beside the spiral, look for the smaller round building, which Wright called the Monitor. It now holds the Thannhauser Collection, a gift of Impressionist and early modern paintings, including works by Picasso. Between 2005 and 2008, the museum restored the spiral's exterior, repairing cracks in the concrete and stripping away many layers of old paint.

In 2019, the Guggenheim was added to the World Heritage List, along with seven other buildings by Wright, the first modern architecture in the United States to be honored that way. So the building that critics once compared to a washing machine now sits on the same list as the Taj Mahal.
=== STOP Carnegie Hill: Andrew Carnegie's Mansion
=== DEEPER Libraries for Everyone | culture
You heard that Carnegie devoted himself to giving his fortune away. The gift people remember most is libraries.

As a boy near Pittsburgh, Carnegie worked long hours and had no money for books. A local man, Colonel James Anderson, opened his private library of a few hundred books to working boys on Saturdays, and young Andrew borrowed a book each week. He never forgot it.

Starting in the 1880s, Carnegie paid for public library buildings across America and around the English-speaking world, more than twenty-five hundred in all, about seventeen hundred of them in the United States. His usual bargain was that he paid for the building, and the town provided the land and promised to stock and run it.

In 1901, he made that offer to New York City on a grand scale: five point two million dollars for sixty-five branch libraries. The city hired a handful of leading architectural firms, among them McKim, Mead and White and Carrère and Hastings, to design them in a shared, dignified style, so a Carnegie branch is easy to spot in any neighborhood. Most still stand, and many are still in use, recognizable by their classical fronts and tall windows. One of the first to open, in 1902, is the Yorkville branch on East 79th Street, a short walk from the next stop.

Not everyone was grateful. Some towns turned his money down, and some workers said they would rather have had better wages than libraries with Carnegie's name on them. But generations of immigrant children learned English, and much else, at those reading tables.
=== DEEPER The Hewitt Sisters | people
You heard that the design museum in this house was founded by three granddaughters of Peter Cooper. Two of them, Sarah and Eleanor Hewitt, were its driving force.

Their father was Abram Hewitt, an iron manufacturer who served as mayor of New York in the late 1880s, and their grandfather Peter Cooper had founded Cooper Union, the free college you may have heard about on the East Village tour. Their family's country home, Ringwood Manor in northern New Jersey, is now a state park. The sisters, who never married, traveled in Europe and admired its museums of decorative arts, where designers and students could study real objects up close. In 1897, they opened their own museum, in their grandfather's building downtown.

They collected relentlessly: textiles and lace, wallpapers and drawings, furniture and metalwork, anything that could teach a designer how things had been made and decorated over the centuries. Today the collection numbers more than two hundred thousand objects. Their museum was meant to be a working library of objects, not a treasure house.

The museum outlived them. In the 1960s, Cooper Union decided it could no longer afford it, and designers and supporters rallied to save it. The Smithsonian Institution took over the collection, and in 1976 it reopened here, in Carnegie's mansion.

So the steel king's house holds a museum started by two determined sisters. It's also a fitting home for design: as you heard, Carnegie's house was itself a showcase of the newest technology of its day.
=== DEEPER The Warburgs of Fifth Avenue | people
You heard that the Jewish Museum, a block north, was the mansion of the banker Felix Warburg. The family behind it shaped finance and philanthropy on two continents.

Felix Warburg came from a banking family in Hamburg, Germany. In New York he married Frieda Schiff, the daughter of Jacob Schiff, the head of the investment bank Kuhn, Loeb, and one of the most powerful bankers in America. Schiff's money also helped Lillian Wald open the Henry Street Settlement, as you may have heard on the Lower East Side. And in 1904, when Japan went to war with Russia, Schiff helped Japan raise enormous loans, in part to strike at the czar, whose government persecuted Russian Jews. Japan won, and its emperor honored him.

Felix joined his father-in-law's firm and became one of the great philanthropists of his time. His brother Paul, also a banker in New York, was one of the men at the secret Jekyll Island meeting you may have heard about in the Financial District, and became one of the chief architects of the Federal Reserve. In 1914, Felix became the first chairman of the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, which sent aid to Jews suffering in Europe and beyond.

The house was designed by C. P. H. Gilbert, who built mansions for many of the era's richest families, in the style of a French Gothic château, reportedly to the dismay of Jacob Schiff, who thought it too showy. After Felix died in 1937, Frieda gave it to the Jewish Theological Seminary, and in 1947 it opened as the Jewish Museum. Look at the carved stonework: a château built by a German Jewish banker on Fifth Avenue, now devoted to Jewish art and culture.

=== DEEPER A Night at the Garden | politics
You heard that the pro-Nazi German American Bund held rallies here on 86th Street. Its biggest night in New York came not here but at Madison Square Garden, on February 20, 1939.

About twenty thousand people filled the arena. The Bund billed the event as a celebration of George Washington's birthday, and a giant portrait of Washington hung above the stage, flanked by swastika banners. Uniformed Bund guards lined the aisles. Speakers attacked Jews and mocked President Roosevelt, to roars of approval. Outside, thousands of counter-protesters gathered, and a huge force of police kept the two sides apart.

During the speech by Fritz Kuhn, the Bund's leader, a young Jewish plumber's helper from Brooklyn named Isadore Greenbaum rushed the stage. Bund guards tackled him and beat him, tearing at his clothes as the crowd laughed and cheered, until police pulled him away. He was arrested and fined.

Film of the rally sat largely forgotten for decades, until the filmmaker Marshall Curry turned it into a short documentary, A Night at the Garden, in 2017. It's only a few minutes long, and many who see it are shocked that it happened in New York at all.

Later that year, as you heard, Kuhn was convicted of embezzling the Bund's money, and the movement collapsed. But for one night, twenty thousand people cheered for it in the middle of Manhattan, and one man from Brooklyn tried to stop them.
=== DEEPER The Marx Brothers of 93rd Street | culture
Walk a few blocks northwest of here, to East 93rd Street between Lexington and Third Avenues. In a modest walk-up at Number 179, the Marx Brothers grew up.

There were five of them: Chico, Harpo, Groucho, Gummo, and Zeppo, born Leonard, Adolph, Julius, Milton, and Herbert. Their father, Sam, was a tailor from Alsace, famously not a very good one. The real force in the family was their mother, Minnie, who was determined to put her boys on the stage. Show business ran in her family: her brother, Al Shean, was a vaudeville star, half of the comedy team Gallagher and Shean.

The family lived here around the turn of the twentieth century, when Yorkville was crowded with immigrant families like theirs. Harpo left school early and taught himself the harp, in his own unorthodox way. Chico played piano in saloons and hustled at pool. Groucho had wanted to be a doctor, but his mother had other plans.

Their famous nicknames reportedly came from a fellow vaudevillian, during a card game in the 1910s. Minnie managed them through years of small-time vaudeville, until their act grew into the anarchic, fast-talking comedy that took Broadway by storm in the 1920s, and then Hollywood, with films like Animal Crackers and Duck Soup.

The building still stands. It's an ordinary tenement on an ordinary block, which is exactly the point: some of the most famous comedians in American history learned their timing in rooms like these.
=== DEEPER The Czech Hall | culture
You heard that Yorkville was home to Czechs, Slovaks, and Hungarians as well as Germans. A short walk south, at 321 East 73rd Street, the Czech community left a monument.

Many of the Czech immigrants who settled here worked in the cigar trade, rolling tobacco in factories and in their own tenement apartments. The Bohemian National Hall was built in the 1890s by Czech immigrants, designed by the architect William C. Frohne in a Renaissance Revival style. It was a social center for the whole community, with a ballroom, meeting rooms, and a restaurant, and for decades it hosted dances, plays, and lectures.

As the Czech community moved away, the building declined. In 2001, the old Czech benevolent association that owned it transferred it to the Czech Republic, which restored it. Today it houses the Czech consulate and a Czech cultural center, along with the original association, and a society devoted to the composer Antonín Dvořák.

Dvořák lived in New York in the 1890s, as director of a music conservatory, and it was here that he wrote his Symphony From the New World. It had its premiere at Carnegie Hall in December 1893, and became one of the most loved symphonies ever written.

The hall is a city landmark now, a reminder that Yorkville's immigrant story was never only German.
=== STOP Gracie Mansion & Carl Schurz Park
=== DEEPER The Fort and the Family | history
You heard that Archibald Gracie built his country house here in 1799. He wasn't the first to build on this point, and the first house here ended in gunfire.

Around 1770, a merchant named Jacob Walton, a Loyalist, built a house on the site. When the Revolution began, Washington's army took over the property, and American troops built a fort on the lawn, with cannons facing the East River and Hell Gate. On September 8, 1776, British guns across the water bombarded it, destroying the ramparts and the Walton house. In 1798, Gracie bought the land from Walton's heirs.

The Gracie name lived on in an unexpected way. Archibald Gracie's great-grandson, Colonel Archibald Gracie the Fourth, was a passenger on the Titanic in April 1912. He helped load women and children into the lifeboats, went down with the ship, and survived by clinging to an overturned collapsible boat in the freezing water. He wrote a book about the disaster, The Truth About the Titanic, but he never recovered his health, and died that December, before it was published. The house his great-grandfather built is now one of the oldest surviving wooden houses in Manhattan.

So the house you see stands on the ruins of a Revolutionary battery, and carries the name of a family whose story runs from a merchant's country estate to the deck of the Titanic.
=== DEEPER The Rescue at Spandau | people
You heard that Carl Schurz fled Germany after the failed revolutions of 1848. His escape, and what he did next, reads like an adventure novel.

As a student at the University of Bonn, Schurz joined the revolutionary movement along with his professor and mentor, Gottfried Kinkel. When the revolution was crushed in 1849, Schurz escaped from the besieged fortress of Rastatt through a sewer and fled to Switzerland. Kinkel was captured and sentenced to life in prison.

In November 1850, Schurz, just twenty-one, slipped back into Germany under a false name, bribed a guard at Spandau prison outside Berlin, and helped Kinkel climb out of the building at night and down a rope. The two raced to the coast and escaped by ship to Britain. The rescue made Schurz famous across Europe.

In London he married Margarethe Meyer, and the couple sailed for America. In Watertown, Wisconsin, in 1856, Margarethe opened what's considered the first kindergarten in the United States, for German-speaking children. Carl rose quickly too: a campaigner for Lincoln, minister to Spain, a Union general, a senator, a cabinet secretary, and a crusading reformer.

In 1910, the city renamed this park for him. The dignified statesman it honors was, as a young man, a jailbreaker. His formal monument, by Karl Bitter, the sculptor you may remember from the fountain at the Plaza, stands across town on Morningside Drive at 116th Street.
=== DEEPER The Payroll at the Bottom of Hell Gate | legends
You heard that Hell Gate's rocks wrecked hundreds of ships. One of those wrecks has drawn treasure hunters for more than two centuries.

In 1780, during the Revolutionary War, the British frigate Hussar tried to pass through Hell Gate. It struck a hidden rock called Pot Rock and sank. According to the legend, the ship was carrying the British Army's payroll in gold coin, by some accounts worth around two million pounds.

The British, the story goes, insisted there was little of value aboard. Hardly anyone believed them. For generations, salvagers came looking, with divers in primitive suits, diving bells, and dredges, and later with new underwater machines. The gold, if it was ever there, has never been found.

Was it really there? No one knows for sure. The rocks of Hell Gate were blasted away long ago, and if anything is left of the Hussar, it lies somewhere in the mud of the East River.

So as you watch the water churning past the promenade, there may or may not be a king's fortune somewhere beneath it. That's what makes it a good legend.
=== DEEPER Peter Pan and the Walker | names
The promenade you're standing on, built over the highway along the river, has a name: the John Finley Walk. Finley was the president of City College and later an editor at The New York Times, and a great lover of walking the city. The walk was dedicated in 1942, two years after his death.

Now look for a small bronze statue of Peter Pan in one of the park's quiet gardens. It was made in 1928 by the sculptor Charles Andrew Hafner, for the lobby of the Paramount Theater in Times Square, when that was one of the grandest movie palaces in the world. Peter Pan himself was still fairly new then, by stage standards: the play had opened on Broadway in 1905, with Maude Adams as the boy who wouldn't grow up. At the Paramount, in the 1940s, crowds of screaming teenagers lined up around the block to see a young Frank Sinatra. The statue came to this park in 1975. In 1999, vandals pried it loose and threw it into the East River. It was recovered and put back on its pedestal, this time more securely.

There's a good match between the two. A man who loved to walk, and a boy who never grew up, side by side above the river.
