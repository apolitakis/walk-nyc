=== STOP Washington Square Park & the Arch
=== DEEPER The Arch, Up Close | look
Walk over to the arch and look closely. You heard that the marble arch replaced a much loved wooden one. Its cornerstone was laid in 1890, and it was formally dedicated in 1895.

Look up at the winged figures carved into the spaces above the curve of the arch. They're by the sculptor Frederick MacMonnies. Then look along the top for an inscription, words attributed to Washington: Let us raise a standard to which the wise and the honest can repair. The event is in the hand of God.

Now walk around to the north side, the side facing Fifth Avenue, to see the two statues of Washington. As you face the arch from Fifth Avenue, on the left, on the eastern pier, is Washington as commander in chief, by Hermon MacNeil, from 1916, with figures of Fame and Valor at his back. On the right is Washington as president, from 1918, with figures of Wisdom and Justice, by Alexander Stirling Calder. New Yorkers often call them Washington at War and Washington at Peace.

That second sculptor comes from a remarkable family. His father, Alexander Milne Calder, made the giant statue of William Penn on top of Philadelphia's City Hall. His son, Alexander Calder, became one of the most famous artists of the twentieth century, known for his hanging mobiles. Three generations of sculptors, and the middle one is right here.

Notice, too, how late the statues came. Stanford White's plans had called for sculpture on the piers, but for about twenty years after the dedication the piers stood bare, until these two groups were finally carved.

Finally, go back into the park, stand south of the arch, and look north through it. The arch frames the start of Fifth Avenue, for generations the city's grandest street, so that the avenue begins with a ceremonial gateway at the edge of a village.
=== DEEPER The Hangman's Elm and the Potter's Field | legends
You heard that historians doubt anyone was hanged from the old elm in the northwest corner of the park. Walk over and look at it anyway. It's an English elm, and the city's parks department estimates it's more than three hundred years old, which would make it one of the oldest trees in Manhattan.

The stories attached to it come in several versions. In one, traitors were hanged from its branches during the Revolution. In another, it served as the gallows when this was a potter's field. The trouble is the map. Until 1827, the ground where the tree stands was private farmland, outside the burial ground, and the city added it to the square only after the cemetery had closed. No record shows anyone hanged from it.

There was, though, an execution in the potter's field itself, and it's well documented. Rose Butler, an enslaved teenager, was convicted of setting a fire in the house of the family who held her. The fire did little damage and hurt no one, but she was sentenced to death, and the courts upheld the sentence. She was hanged on a gallows built in the field, in front of a huge crowd, and buried in the ground nearby. Her story is far less famous than the tree's, and far more certain.

The burial ground itself is no legend either. Some estimates put the number of people buried here as high as twenty thousand. When workers dug the foundation for the arch's eastern pier in 1890, about ten feet down they found human bones, a coffin, and a gravestone dated 1803.

So if you sit on a bench here, you may be sitting above some of those forgotten New Yorkers, near a tree that is famous for something it almost certainly never did.
=== DEEPER Painters on the Square | culture
For more than half a century, one of America's greatest painters climbed four flights of stairs on the north side of this square to get to work.

Edward Hopper moved into the top floor of number 3, Washington Square North, in 1913, and lived and painted there until his death in 1967. His wife, the painter Josephine Nivison Hopper, joined him after they married in 1924. The building was a walk-up heated with coal, and the coal had to be hauled up four flights of stairs. Hopper painted the city as a place of quiet and solitude: empty streets in early morning light, lit windows at night, people alone in rooms. You can feel this neighborhood in much of it.

Hopper's first one-man show opened in January 1920 at the Whitney Studio Club, a block from the square on West Fourth Street. It sold nothing. The club was founded by the sculptor and heiress Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney, who bought and showed the work of living American artists when most museums wouldn't. In 1931 she opened the Whitney Museum of American Art in a row of houses on West Eighth Street, a block north of the square. The museum later moved uptown, and now stands at the foot of the High Line, but its first home is still there, now an art school, the New York Studio School. And the Whitney today holds the largest collection of Hopper's work anywhere.

Hopper was far from the first artist drawn here. In 1857, a few blocks north on West Tenth Street, the architect Richard Morris Hunt built the Tenth Street Studio Building, one of the first buildings in America designed especially for artists. Winslow Homer, Frederic Church and John La Farge all worked there. It was torn down in 1956.

So when you look at the dignified houses of the Row, imagine a tall, silent painter climbing the stairs with a load of coal, to a room where he painted the loneliness of the modern city.
=== STOP The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory
=== DEEPER The Shirtwaist Kings | companies
You heard that the owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, were tried and acquitted. In the trade they were known as the Shirtwaist Kings, and their story shows how the garment business worked.

The shirtwaist was the fashion of the age: a tailored blouse worn with a long skirt, made popular around 1900 by the confident Gibson Girl of magazine illustrations. Millions were sold, and New York was the center of the trade. Blanck and Harris were immigrants who had started small and built Triangle into one of the biggest blouse makers in the city, with hundreds of workers.

In 1909, Triangle was one of the shops where the great shirtwaist strike began, the walkout that grew into the Uprising of the Twenty Thousand. Many shops settled and recognized the union. Triangle's owners never did.

At the manslaughter trial, their lawyer, Max Steuer, one of the best trial lawyers in the city, had a key survivor, Kate Alterman, repeat her vivid testimony again and again, then suggested to the jury that her words sounded rehearsed. The jury acquitted both men in less than two hours.

The civil cases ended even worse for the families. In 1914, twenty three lawsuits brought by victims' families were settled for seventy five dollars per life lost. Meanwhile, the owners' insurance had paid them about sixty thousand dollars more than their reported losses, roughly four hundred dollars for every worker who died.

And in 1913, Max Blanck was in court again, this time for locking a door during working hours at his new factory. He was fined twenty dollars.

It's a story about two men, but also about a whole system: an industry with almost no rules, laws that barely punished anything, and workers with little power, until the fire changed the politics of the state.
=== DEEPER The Elevator Men | people
In the chaos of the Triangle fire, two men kept going back into it.

Joseph Zito and Gaspar Mortillalo ran the passenger elevators in the building. When the fire broke out, they began running their small cars up toward the burning floors, cramming in as many workers as they could and bringing them down to the street, again and again, as smoke filled the shafts. Some workers, desperate, jumped down the elevator shaft itself. Finally, the rails of one elevator buckled in the heat, and the cars could no longer climb.

No one knows exactly how many people they saved. Estimates for Zito alone run from about a hundred to a hundred and fifty. Their courage was reported in the newspapers at the time, but their names are far less known than the owners'.

Others helped too. On the tenth floor, where the owners had their offices, people fled up to the roof. In a taller building next door, used by New York University, a law professor and his students found ladders and helped the workers climb from the factory roof up to safety.

And there were the firefighters, who arrived within minutes, and found that the nets they held out could not hold people falling from so high.

When you stand at the corner, look at the building's entrance and imagine those two small cars rising into the smoke. The names on the memorial are the people who were lost. Many of the people who lived owed their lives to two elevator men who wouldn't stop.
=== DEEPER Naming the Dead | history
In the hours after the fire, the bodies were carried to a pier on the East River at Twenty Sixth Street, where a temporary morgue was set up, and laid out in rows of coffins. Through the night and the days that followed, long lines of people filed past, searching for daughters, sisters, sons and friends. Some victims could be recognized only by a ring, a shoe, or a detail of their clothing. The youngest of the dead were fourteen years old.

A handful of bodies could not be identified at all. On April 5th, 1911, unions and community groups organized a funeral procession for them, and in a cold, driving rain, hundreds of thousands of New Yorkers turned out, marching or standing silently along the route. The unidentified victims were buried together at the Evergreens Cemetery in Brooklyn.

For nearly a century, six of them stayed nameless. Then a researcher named Michael Hirsch spent years combing through old newspapers, records and family histories, and in 2011, just before the hundredth anniversary of the fire, he announced their names: Max Florin, Fannie Rosen, Dora Evans, Josephine Cammarata, Maria Lauletti and Concetta Prestifilippo. A new marker with their names was unveiled at the cemetery on April 5th, 2011, a hundred years to the day after the march in the rain.

Today every one of the hundred and forty six has a name. You heard that people chalk those names on the sidewalk here each year. A project called Chalk also sends volunteers to the sidewalks in front of the buildings where the victims lived, scattered across the city, so that each name returns, for a day, to the place that person called home.

So the memorial on this corner isn't only about how they died. It's also about a long, stubborn effort, lasting a century, to remember exactly who they were.
=== STOP MacDougal Street & the Folk Revival
=== DEEPER Dave Van Ronk, the Mayor of MacDougal Street | people
If the folk revival had a center of gravity on these blocks, it was a big, bearded, gravel-voiced singer named Dave Van Ronk.

Van Ronk grew up in Brooklyn and Queens, played in traditional jazz bands, shipped out as a merchant seaman, and by the late 1950s was singing old blues and folk songs in the coffeehouses here. He was a teacher as much as a performer. Younger musicians came to him to learn guitar parts and old songs, and to argue about music and politics. When Bob Dylan arrived in 1961, Van Ronk took him under his wing, let him sleep on his couch, and helped him get work at the Gaslight. Dylan later called him the king of the street.

Van Ronk told one story about their friendship many times. He had worked out his own arrangement of an old song, The House of the Rising Sun, and planned to record it. Dylan learned it and put it on his own first album before Van Ronk got the chance. Then, in 1964, a British band, the Animals, had a worldwide hit with a version that followed the same arrangement, and Dylan stopped playing the song, because audiences assumed he was copying the Animals. Van Ronk loved telling that story.

Van Ronk never became a star, and he didn't seem to mind. He kept playing the Village for decades, until his death in 2002. His memoir, The Mayor of MacDougal Street, was finished after his death by the writer Elijah Wald, and it inspired the Coen brothers' 2013 film, Inside Llewyn Davis, set in the Village folk world of the early 1960s.

In 2004, the city named a corner of Sheridan Square, where Barrow Street meets Washington Place, Dave Van Ronk Street. If you're heading west toward Christopher Street, look for the sign.
=== DEEPER Little Africa | history
Before this was an Italian neighborhood, and long before it was a bohemian one, the streets around Minetta Lane were a center of Black New York.

The roots go back to the 1600s, when the Dutch granted farmland in this area to formerly enslaved Africans. Later, the path along Minetta Brook was known as the Negroes' Causeway.

In the nineteenth century, after slavery finally ended in New York State in 1827, a Black community grew up here, on Minetta Lane and Minetta Street, and along Bleecker, Thompson and Sullivan Streets. It was crowded and poor, but it was a real community, home to waiters, laborers, laundresses, sailors and musicians. New Yorkers called it Little Africa. In 1890, the reformer Jacob Riis described it in his book How the Other Half Lives, mostly as a slum, with the condescension of his time.

The community was pushed and pulled away. After the Draft Riots of 1863, when white mobs attacked Black New Yorkers across the city, many families left Manhattan. Later, rising rents and new waves of immigrants pressed on those who stayed. By the turn of the century, Black New Yorkers were moving north, first to the Tenderloin, in the West Twenties and Thirties, and to San Juan Hill, near today's Lincoln Center, and then to Harlem. Italian immigrants filled the tenements they left behind, and within a generation, Little Africa was almost forgotten.

There's little to mark it now. But as you walk along Minetta Lane, remember that the Village's long history of outsiders making a home here didn't begin with the bohemians. It began with people who had far fewer choices about where they could live.
=== DEEPER Bleecker Street's Italian Village | food
Walk a block or two south and west of here, onto Bleecker Street, and you'll find what remains of the South Village's Italian past, much of it in the form of food.

By 1900, the blocks south of Washington Square had become one of the largest Italian neighborhoods in the city. Life centered on the church of Our Lady of Pompeii, on Carmine Street, whose parish was founded in 1892 to serve Italian immigrants. Its pastor for more than three decades, Father Antonio Demo, helped newcomers find their footing in a new country, and the little square beside the church, where Bleecker, Carmine and Sixth Avenue meet, is named Father Demo Square.

The food shops followed the people. Along Bleecker Street, you can still find a pork store, Faicco's, that has been making sausage for more than a century, and a cheese shop, Murray's, open since 1940 and now known across the country. Bakeries sell cannoli and pignoli cookies, and pizza by the slice gets folded and eaten on the sidewalk.

The Italian cafés on these streets were serving espresso long before the Beat poets and folk singers made coffeehouses the center of Village life. When the folk clubs of the 1960s needed cheap basements and storefronts, many of them found space in these Italian blocks, and the landlords, the neighbors and the musicians didn't always get along. Some older residents complained about the noise and the crowds, and those complaints were part of the background to the 1961 fight over folk singing in Washington Square.

Today the Italian population has thinned, and many of the old social clubs are gone. But stop in for a sandwich or a pastry, and you're tasting the neighborhood that sat between Little Africa and the folk revival, and outlasted both.
=== STOP Jefferson Market Library & Patchin Place
=== DEEPER The Committee to Start the Clock | politics
You heard that neighbors saved this building from demolition. Their campaign began with something small: a clock that didn't work.

By the late 1950s, the clock in the tower had stopped, a sad sign over an empty building. A Village resident named Margot Gayle decided to start there. With a group of neighbors, she formed a committee with a gloriously long name: the Committee of Neighbors to Get the Clock on Jefferson Market Courthouse Started. One Christmas, they sent Mayor Robert Wagner a telegram that played on the song All I Want for Christmas Is My Two Front Teeth. What they wanted was their clock.

It worked. In October 1960, the clock started again. The clock was a symbol, and once the neighbors had won it, they set their sights on the whole building. In 1961, a new group formed, the Committee for a Library in the Courthouse, and it persuaded the city to turn the courthouse into the library you see today.

Margot Gayle went on to become one of the city's great preservationists. She was a leader in the fight to protect SoHo's cast iron buildings, and she founded a group called the Friends of Cast Iron Architecture to champion them, at a time when many people saw those buildings as old factories ready for the wrecking ball.

Her method is worth remembering. Instead of starting with a huge demand, she started with something everyone could agree on, a stopped clock on a neighborhood landmark, and built from there. Each small win made the next, bigger one possible.

Look up at the tower. If the clock is keeping time, it's partly because a neighbor thought a stopped clock was a bad sign for a neighborhood.
=== DEEPER The House of D | people
The garden next door stands on the site of the Women's House of Detention, which everyone called the House of D. You heard that Angela Davis and Dorothy Day were held there. Many others passed through too.

The jail held women awaiting trial or serving short sentences, and it was famously overcrowded. Ethel Rosenberg was held here after her arrest in 1950, during the espionage case in which she and her husband, Julius, were convicted and later executed. Afeni Shakur, a member of the Black Panther Party, was held here as one of the Panther 21, accused of plotting bombings, and spent part of her time here pregnant with her son, the future rapper Tupac Shakur. She defended herself at trial, and she and her codefendants were acquitted in 1971.

Historians have also recovered another side of the House of D. Many of the women held here were lesbians, or didn't fit the gender rules of their time, and were arrested for it, or for sex work, or simply for being poor. The writer Hugh Ryan, in a history of the jail published in 2022, argues that it was a central place in queer New York, just a few blocks from the Stonewall Inn. The shouted conversations between the windows and the sidewalk that you heard about included lovers, and the street outside became a meeting place in its own right.

Conditions were harsh, and complaints mounted. In 1965, a college student named Andrea Dworkin, arrested at a protest against the Vietnam War, spoke out about brutal treatment by the jail's doctors. Her account made the newspapers and added to the pressure to close the jail, which finally happened in the early 1970s.

Today, roses grow where the jail once stood, in a garden tended by volunteers.
=== DEEPER The Market Behind the Name | food
Why is a library called Jefferson Market? Because for nearly a century, this block was a market, named for Thomas Jefferson.

In 1833, the city opened a public market here, one of the neighborhood markets where New Yorkers bought meat, fish, vegetables and butter from licensed stall keepers. Over the market rose a tall wooden fire lookout tower with a big bell. Watchmen scanned the rooftops of the growing village, and when they spotted smoke, they rang the bell in patterns that told firefighters which part of town to run to.

One of the butchers who took a stall here when the market opened, Thomas De Voe, stayed for nearly forty years and became the great historian of the city's markets. In 1862 he published The Market Book, a history of New York's public markets, and a few years later The Market Assistant, a guide to every kind of food sold in them, from game birds to oysters. He went on to become the city's superintendent of markets.

In the 1870s, the city replaced the old wooden sheds with a whole civic complex in the same colorful Victorian style: the courthouse you see, a jail behind it, and a new market hall. The courthouse tower took over the old lookout's job. The market carried on into the twentieth century, but neighborhood markets were giving way to grocery stores, and in the late 1920s the market and the old jail were cleared to make way for the Women's House of Detention.

So the name over the library door remembers a place where Villagers once haggled over chickens and cabbages, under a watchtower keeping an eye out for fire.
=== STOP The Stonewall Inn & Christopher Park
=== DEEPER The Sip-In at Julius' | politics
You heard that bars could be punished for serving gay customers. Three years before Stonewall, a few blocks from here, activists challenged that rule with a quieter kind of protest, and it worked.

The State Liquor Authority treated gay customers as a sign of a disorderly bar, so bartenders routinely refused to serve anyone they suspected of being gay. In April 1966, three members of the Mattachine Society, one of the country's early gay rights groups, Dick Leitsch, Craig Rodwell and John Timmons, decided to challenge the rule the way civil rights activists in the South had challenged segregated lunch counters. They called it a sip-in, and they brought reporters.

The men went from bar to bar, announcing that they were homosexual and asking to be served. At Julius', at the corner of West Tenth Street and Waverly Place, the bartender set out glasses, heard their declaration, and put his hand over the top of a glass. A photographer caught the moment, and the picture of that hand became famous.

The protest made the New York Times. The city's Commission on Human Rights said that gay people had the right to be served in bars, and court decisions over the next few years made it harder for the state to punish a bar just because its customers were gay. It didn't end police harassment, as the Stonewall raid would show. But it was an early step toward gay bars that could operate openly and legally.

Julius' is still pouring drinks, and it's one of the oldest gay bars in the city. In 2022, the city made it an official landmark, for what happened at its bar.

Craig Rodwell, one of the three, opened the Oscar Wilde Memorial Bookshop in 1967, one of the first bookstores devoted to gay and lesbian writers. After Stonewall, he helped propose the march you heard about, the one that became the first pride parade.
=== DEEPER Who Threw the First Brick? | legends
Ask who started the Stonewall uprising, and you'll get many answers. You heard that the details are still debated. Here's what the debate is about.

The most famous story says that Marsha P. Johnson, the Black drag queen and activist, threw the first brick, or a shot glass, at the police. But in an interview years later, Johnson herself said that by the time she got there, the uprising had already started. Sylvia Rivera, her friend and fellow activist, also told dramatic stories about that night, but her accounts changed from one telling to the next, and historians still argue about what she saw.

Another account centers on a woman dressed in men's clothes, who fought back as police pushed her toward a patrol wagon, and called out to the crowd to do something. Many people believe that woman was Stormé DeLarverie, a performer who later worked as a bouncer and was known around the Village as a protector of lesbian bars and the women in them. DeLarverie said in interviews that it was her, but it's never been confirmed.

You heard that the crowd threw coins. Many accounts say those coins carried a message: they mocked the payoffs the police took from the bar's owners.

Johnson and Rivera's greatest importance came afterward. In 1970 they founded a group called STAR, Street Transvestite Action Revolutionaries, which sheltered homeless young people, and for decades they fought to keep transgender people, especially transgender people of color, at the center of the movement.

Whoever threw the first brick, the uprising belonged to the whole crowd on the street that night: young, poor, many of them homeless or gender nonconforming, and out of patience.
=== DEEPER The Stonewall's Many Lives | history
The building across the street has been a pair of stables, a bakery, a restaurant, a Mafia bar, and the heart of a national monument.

It's actually two buildings, numbers 51 and 53 Christopher Street, built in the 1840s as stables. In 1930 they were joined at the ground floor and housed a bakery, and a few years later a restaurant opened here called Bonnie's Stonewall Inn.

By the mid 1960s, the restaurant had closed, and men connected to the Genovese crime family took over the space. In 1967, Tony Lauria, known as Fat Tony, reopened it as a gay bar. You heard it had no liquor license. To get around that, it called itself a private bottle club, and patrons signed a book at the door, often with made-up names. There was no running water behind the bar, so glasses were rinsed in tubs and used again, and the police usually tipped off the owners before a raid.

After the uprising, the bar didn't last long. It closed within months, and for years the space was rented to a string of other businesses, while the uprising itself grew into legend. A new bar took the old name in the 1990s.

Recognition came slowly, then all at once. In 1999, the building and the streets around it were listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and the next year they became a National Historic Landmark. In 2015, the city made the Stonewall Inn an official landmark, the first in New York designated mainly for its place in gay history. The national monument followed the next year.

Look at the front of the building and notice how modest it is: two plain old stables, joined and painted, with a sign over the door. History doesn't always choose grand buildings. Sometimes it chooses the place where people finally refused to leave.
=== DEEPER St. Vincent's and the AIDS Years | history
A few blocks north of here, at Seventh Avenue and Twelfth Street, stood a hospital that was woven into the life of the Village for a century and a half.

St. Vincent's Hospital was founded in 1849 by the Sisters of Charity, and it cared for the city in some of its worst moments. In 1912, survivors of the Titanic were brought here. On September 11th, 2001, its doctors and nurses lined up stretchers on the sidewalk, waiting for the wounded from the World Trade Center, most of whom never came.

In the 1980s, the hospital was at the center of another crisis. As AIDS spread through the city, and hit gay men in neighborhoods like this one especially hard, St. Vincent's opened what is often described as the first and largest AIDS ward on the East Coast, in 1984. Many of the Village's young men died there. The nurses and doctors who cared for them, at a time when much of the country looked away, are remembered with deep gratitude.

The hospital closed in 2010, deep in debt, and its buildings were turned into apartments. Across the street, on a triangle of land at Seventh Avenue, Twelfth Street and Greenwich Avenue, the New York City AIDS Memorial was dedicated on World AIDS Day, December 1st, 2016. A white steel canopy rises over a fountain, and the granite paving around it is engraved with lines from Walt Whitman's poem Song of Myself, arranged by the artist Jenny Holzer.

If you have a few minutes, walk up and stand under it. It's a quiet place in the middle of a busy intersection, for remembering a generation the Village lost.
=== STOP Bedford Street: Chumley's & the Narrowest House
=== DEEPER Edna St. Vincent Millay | people
You heard that the poet Edna St. Vincent Millay lived in the narrowest house on this street. In her day she was one of the most famous poets in America, and a symbol of a new kind of freedom.

Millay grew up poor in Maine, raised by her mother, a nurse, and she became famous young: she was twenty when her long poem Renascence was published, in 1912. She got her unusual middle name from St. Vincent's Hospital in New York, which had saved her uncle's life just before she was born. Her friends called her Vincent.

After college, she moved to Greenwich Village in 1917, acted with the Provincetown Players, and wrote poems that captured the mood of young women in the years after the First World War: independent, witty, and a little reckless. Her most famous lines, from a short poem called First Fig, are about a candle that burns at both ends. It will not last the night, she wrote, but it gives a lovely light.

In 1923, the year of her Pulitzer Prize, she married Eugen Boissevain, a Dutch businessman who was devoted to her career, and the two lived for a time in the tiny house here. In 1924, she joined a group of theater friends in founding the Cherry Lane Theatre, around the corner.

Millay and Boissevain soon moved to a farm in upstate New York called Steepletop, where she lived for the rest of her life. She died there in 1950. But the image of her in this little house, a famous poet in a home barely wider than a hallway, sums up the Village of the 1920s: talented, poor or pretending to be, and determined to live differently.
=== DEEPER Look Closer: Bedford and Commerce Streets | look
These few blocks hold some of the oddest and oldest houses in Manhattan. Here's what to look for.

Start at 77 Bedford Street, at the corner of Commerce Street, right next to the narrow house, a plain little house with a brick front. It was built in 1799, and it's often called the oldest house in the Village. Its early owners included Harmon Hendricks, a copper merchant who served as Paul Revere's agent in New York. The brick is only skin deep: it was added in 1836 to the older wooden house underneath.

Walk up Bedford to number 102, and you'll see something out of a fairy tale: a half-timbered house with two steep peaks, known as Twin Peaks. It began as a plain house around 1830. In 1925, a designer named Clifford Reed Daily, with money from the banker Otto Kahn, rebuilt it as a whimsical home and studios for artists, a little Alpine fantasy dropped onto a Village street.

Then walk to 39 and 41 Commerce Street, two matching houses with mansard roofs, standing apart with a garden between them. Legend says a sea captain built them for two daughters who couldn't stand each other. It's a good story, with no evidence behind it. They were built in the early 1830s for a milkman, and the mansard roofs were added decades later.

Finally, walk to the corner of Bedford and Grove Streets and look up at the apartment building at number 90. You may recognize it. It's the building shown as the characters' home in the television comedy Friends.

As you go, notice the details that survive on the older houses: low doorways, small windows under the roofline, and iron railings worn smooth by two centuries of hands.
=== DEEPER Thomas Paine's Lost Bones | legends
A short walk from here, at 59 Grove Street, a plaque marks the place where one of the most important writers of the American Revolution died, nearly forgotten.

Thomas Paine's pamphlet Common Sense, published in 1776, persuaded thousands of Americans to support independence. His series The American Crisis, which begins These are the times that try men's souls, was read aloud to Washington's troops. But his later book, The Age of Reason, which attacked organized religion, made him one of the most despised men in America. When he came back from France in 1802, many old friends shunned him.

He spent his last months in Greenwich Village, poor and ill, and died on June 8th, 1809, in a small wooden house where 59 Grove Street now stands. Only a handful of people came to his funeral. The house is long gone. The bar in the building there now is a piano bar called Marie's Crisis, named in honor of his pamphlets.

The Village didn't entirely forget him. A nearby street was named Reason Street, after his book. New Yorkers slurred it into Raisin Street, and it was later renamed Barrow Street.

Now the legend, and it's mostly true. Paine was buried on his farm in New Rochelle, north of the city. In 1819, an English admirer, the radical journalist William Cobbett, dug up the bones and shipped them to England, planning to build a grand memorial. The memorial was never built. After Cobbett died, the bones passed through other hands, and then they vanished. Over the years, people have claimed to own pieces of him, a skull here, a jawbone there, a lock of hair. None of the claims has been proven.

So one of the men who argued America into existence has no known resting place. He has a plaque on a quiet Village street, beside a bar where people gather around the piano and sing show tunes late into the night.
=== STOP Bell Labs at Westbeth
=== DEEPER The Call Across the Continent | companies
Long before Bell Labs got its name, this building was full of engineers working on one enormous problem: how to make a voice travel across a continent.

The building belonged to Western Electric, and in 1907 the engineering staffs of Western Electric and its parent company, AT&T, were brought together here, at what was then 463 West Street. Their task was to stretch the reach of the telephone. Over long distances, the electrical signal of a voice grew weaker and weaker, until nothing was left.

The answer came from a strange new device, the vacuum tube. An inventor named Lee de Forest had built a tube he called the Audion, and AT&T bought the rights to use it. A young physicist here, Harold Arnold, turned it into a reliable amplifier, and engineers placed these amplifiers at stations along the line, boosting the voice as it traveled.

On January 25th, 1915, the transcontinental line opened. In New York, Alexander Graham Bell, the inventor of the telephone, picked up the receiver and repeated the words from his first call, nearly forty years earlier: Mister Watson, come here, I want you. In San Francisco, his old assistant, Thomas Watson, is said to have answered that it would take him five days to get there now.

That October, engineers from this building tried something bolder. From a navy radio station in Arlington, Virginia, they sent a human voice across the Atlantic. Listeners at the Eiffel Tower in Paris heard it, faintly. They had no way to talk back, so they answered by telegraph.

The vacuum tube amplifier became the heart of radio, sound movies and long distance calling for half a century. And when Bell Labs was founded in 1925, it grew out of this same engineering department, in this same building.
=== DEEPER Machines That Talk and Listen | history
The engineers here were fascinated by sound: how we hear it, how we speak, and how a machine might do both.

In the 1920s, a physicist named Harvey Fletcher led research here into human hearing, measuring how loud sounds seem at different pitches, and working on hearing tests and hearing aids. That work led toward stereo. Fletcher's team worked with the Philadelphia Orchestra and its famous conductor, Leopold Stokowski, and in 1933 they carried a concert over telephone lines from Philadelphia to an audience at Constitution Hall in Washington, through three separate channels and three sets of loudspeakers, so that listeners could hear where each instrument was. In 1940, they demonstrated stereo recordings at Carnegie Hall.

Another engineer, Homer Dudley, worked on taking speech apart. Beginning in the late 1920s, he developed the vocoder, which broke a voice into its basic components so that it could be sent more efficiently and rebuilt at the other end. Then he built a machine that could speak with no human voice at all: the Voder, shown at the 1939 World's Fair in Queens. A trained operator played it like an organ, with keys and a foot pedal, and astonished crowds heard it say whole sentences.

The vocoder went to war. During the Second World War, Bell Labs built its principles into a secret system called SIGSALY, which scrambled the telephone conversations of Allied leaders, including Winston Churchill and Franklin Roosevelt, so the enemy couldn't listen in.

After the war, the vocoder found a second life in music, giving robotic voices to electronic and pop records, most famously those of the German band Kraftwerk.

So when you hear a synthesized voice on your phone, or a robot voice in a song, or music that seems to surround you from every side, you're hearing ideas that took shape in this building.
=== DEEPER Discoveries on West Street | people
Some of the most important ideas of the twentieth century were worked out in this building, by people most of us have never heard of.

In 1927, two physicists here, Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer, fired a beam of electrons at a crystal of nickel and found that the electrons scattered in patterns, the way waves do. It was proof that particles of matter can behave like waves, one of the strange central ideas of quantum physics. Davisson shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1937, the first of the many Nobel Prizes for work at Bell Labs.

That same year, an engineer named Harold Black was riding the ferry across the Hudson to work here when an idea came to him for making amplifiers far more stable and faithful. He sketched it on the only paper he had, a blank space in his copy of the New York Times. That idea, the negative feedback amplifier, became a foundation of modern electronics.

Around the same time, John Johnson discovered that every electrical circuit hums with a faint, unavoidable noise, caused by the jiggling of electrons in the warmth of the wire itself, and his colleague Harry Nyquist explained it with an equation. Nyquist's name is also attached to a rule at the root of digital sound: how often a signal has to be measured to capture it completely.

During the Second World War, the British codebreaker Alan Turing came here in 1943 to study the secret system for scrambling the voices of Allied leaders. While he was here, he often ate lunch in the cafeteria with a young Bell Labs mathematician named Claude Shannon, and the two talked about machines, minds and information. Five years later, Shannon published the paper that founded information theory, the mathematics behind every text, photo and video you send.

Look up at these plain industrial walls and imagine the conversations behind them.

=== DEEPER Saving the West Village | politics
You heard that Jane Jacobs helped defeat the plan to designate the West Village for urban renewal. Here's how that fight went.

In early 1961, the people of this neighborhood learned that the city wanted to study it for urban renewal. To planners at the time, that meant declaring an area blighted and clearing it for new housing projects. Blocks like these, with old houses, small factories and corner bars, were exactly what planners considered obsolete.

Neighbors formed the Committee to Save the West Village, and Jacobs became one of its leaders. They knew that once an area was officially labeled a slum, demolition was hard to stop, so they fought the label itself. They gathered evidence that the neighborhood was healthy, busy and loved. They packed public hearings. They pointed out that the people who would be displaced had never been asked.

The fight was fierce. In October 1961, at a stormy hearing, the City Planning Commission voted its approval anyway, while furious residents shouted their protests. But the pressure kept building, and within a few months the city dropped the plan.

The neighbors then went further. They drew up their own plan for new housing that would fit the neighborhood: modest walk-up buildings on vacant lots and old industrial sites, without tearing down homes. It took more than a decade. The result, the West Village Houses, forty two small brick apartment buildings near the river, finished in the 1970s, had a rocky start, squeezed by budget cuts and delays. But it was a rare thing: housing designed with a neighborhood rather than imposed on it.

The episode became a model for neighborhood fights across the country. And today, the blocks that were nearly cleared are among the most sought-after places to live in America.
=== DEEPER Jane Jacobs, Before Death and Life | people
The woman who changed how the world thinks about cities had no degree in planning, and no college degree at all.

Jane Butzner was born in 1916 in Scranton, Pennsylvania. She moved to New York in the mid 1930s, in the depths of the Depression, and took whatever work she could find, as a stenographer and freelance writer. She wrote articles about the city's working districts, the fur district, the flower district, the leather district, learning how neighborhoods actually made a living. She took classes at Columbia for a couple of years, but never finished a degree.

She married an architect, Robert Jacobs, in 1944, and a few years later they bought the modest building here on Hudson Street and fixed it up themselves. In 1952 she joined the magazine Architectural Forum, where she wrote about the great housing projects and urban renewal schemes of the time. The more of them she saw, the less she believed in them. She noticed that the new projects were often empty and unsafe, while old, messy streets like her own were full of life.

In 1958, she wrote an article for Fortune magazine called Downtown Is for People, attacking the planners' visions for city centers. It caught the attention of the Rockefeller Foundation, which gave her a grant to write a book. That book, The Death and Life of Great American Cities, came out in 1961 and opened with a line that announced a fight: This book is an attack on current city planning and rebuilding.

In the late 1960s, opposed to the Vietnam War and determined that her sons would not be drafted, she moved her family to Toronto. She became a Canadian citizen, and she helped stop a highway there too.
=== DEEPER Sir Peter Warren's Greenwich | names
Walk a block north to the little triangle where Hudson Street meets Eighth Avenue, called Abingdon Square, and you'll find a clue to who owned this land before it was a neighborhood.

In the 1740s, much of what's now the West Village belonged to Sir Peter Warren, an Irish-born officer in the British navy who had grown rich on prize money from capturing enemy ships. He married into a prominent New York family, the De Lanceys, and put together a country estate here of around three hundred acres, with a mansion near the river. Greenwich was then quiet countryside, a few miles north of the city.

Warren went back to Britain, became an admiral and a member of Parliament, and died in 1752, leaving his American lands to his daughters. One of them, Charlotte, married an English nobleman, Willoughby Bertie, the Earl of Abingdon, and that's where Abingdon Square got its name. Warren himself is remembered in Warren Street, far downtown in Tribeca.

Over the following decades, the estate was divided and sold off in lots, and some of its old boundaries and lanes live on in the angled streets you've been walking.

There's a nice twist to the name. The Earl of Abingdon was no friend of the British government's war against the colonies. During the Revolution, he spoke and wrote in defense of the Americans' rights. So this little square, in a neighborhood that has always welcomed dissenters, is named for an English lord who took the rebels' side.
