title: Harlem
area: Manhattan
blurb: The Apollo, Hotel Theresa, the Harlem Renaissance, Marcus Garvey's Black Star Line, the Savoy, Strivers' Row, and Hamilton's Grange.
=== OVERVIEW
Harlem is one of the most famous neighborhoods in the world, a name that stands for African American culture, music, politics, and creativity. But Harlem was not always Black, and the story of how it became the capital of Black America is one of the most interesting in New York history.

The Dutch founded the village of Nieuw Haarlem in 1658, named after the city of Haarlem in the Netherlands. For two centuries, Harlem was farmland and country estates, connected to the city by the old Post Road. In the late nineteenth century, elevated trains arrived, and developers built elegant brownstones and apartment buildings for middle- and upper-middle-class white families, many of them German and Jewish immigrants and their children. Harlem became a fashionable suburb within the city.

Then came a crash. Developers, anticipating the arrival of the first subway line along Lenox Avenue in 1904, built far more apartment buildings than the market could fill. Prices collapsed, and landlords were left with empty buildings. Enter Philip A. Payton Junior, a Black real estate entrepreneur, who founded the Afro-American Realty Company in 1904. Payton persuaded white landlords to rent their vacant buildings to Black tenants, who were desperate for decent housing, since they were confined to crowded, run-down neighborhoods like the Tenderloin and San Juan Hill. Payton's company leased and bought buildings, and Black New Yorkers began moving in. White residents resisted, sometimes forming associations to keep Black tenants out, but the movement was unstoppable. The Great Migration, the movement of millions of African Americans out of the rural South starting around 1915, along with immigration from the Caribbean, filled Harlem. By the 1920s, it was the largest Black urban community in the country.

The result was the Harlem Renaissance, a flowering of Black literature, music, art, and political thought in the 1920s and 30s. Writers like Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay, and Countee Cullen; musicians like Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong, Bessie Smith, and Fats Waller; and intellectuals like W. E. B. Du Bois and Alain Locke made Harlem a cultural capital.

Harlem's later history includes the Great Depression, which hit it hard; the political activism of Adam Clayton Powell Junior and Malcolm X; decades of disinvestment, crime, and abandonment in the 1970s and 80s; and, since the 1990s, a dramatic and contested wave of gentrification. Through it all, Harlem has remained a center of Black culture, faith, and politics.

As you walk, notice the remarkable architecture. Harlem has some of the finest rows of brownstones and townhouses in the city, built for wealthy white families who left long ago.
=== STOP Marcus Garvey Park @ 40.8044,-73.9441
You're at Marcus Garvey Park, between 120th and 124th Streets, at Madison Avenue and Fifth Avenue. Originally called Mount Morris Park, it was renamed in 1973 for Marcus Garvey.

Garvey was a Jamaican-born activist who arrived in New York in 1916. He founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association, the UNIA, which became the largest mass movement of people of African descent in history, with chapters around the world and, by some estimates, millions of members and supporters. Garvey preached Black pride, self-reliance, and Pan-Africanism, the idea that people of African descent around the world shared a common destiny, and that Africa should be free of colonial rule. He held huge parades through Harlem, with uniformed marchers, and rallies at the UNIA's Liberty Hall on West 138th Street.

In 1919, Garvey launched the Black Star Line, a shipping company owned by Black shareholders, which would carry passengers and goods between America, the Caribbean, and Africa. It was a bold idea: an international business controlled by Black people, at a time when they were shut out of most industries. Tens of thousands of supporters bought shares, some for as little as five dollars. But the company was badly managed, bought old, unseaworthy ships, and collapsed within a few years. In 1923, Garvey was convicted of mail fraud related to the sale of Black Star Line stock, in a prosecution that many saw as politically motivated, encouraged by J. Edgar Hoover's Bureau of Investigation. He was imprisoned and then deported to Jamaica in 1927. His ideas, though, influenced generations of activists, from Malcolm X to the leaders of African independence movements.

Now look up at the rocky hill in the center of the park. On top stands the Harlem Fire Watchtower, a cast iron tower built in 1857. In the days before telephone alarms, watchmen stood here scanning the area for smoke, and rang the bell to call firefighters. It's the last surviving fire watchtower of its kind in the city. It was dismantled for restoration and reinstalled in 2019.

Around the park, look at the Mount Morris Park Historic District, with some of the most beautiful brownstones and townhouses in Harlem, built in the 1880s and 90s. A few blocks away, at 20 East 127th Street, is the townhouse where the poet Langston Hughes lived for the last twenty years of his life.
=== STOP 125th Street & the Apollo Theater @ 40.8100,-73.9501
Walk west to 125th Street, Harlem's main commercial street, and look for the red and white vertical sign of the Apollo Theater, at 253 West 125th Street.

The building opened in 1914 as Hurtig and Seamon's New Burlesque Theater. Like many theaters in Harlem at the time, it was for white audiences only. In 1934, new owners reopened it as the Apollo Theater, presenting variety shows for Black audiences. It quickly became the most famous venue for Black performers in America.

Its most famous institution is Amateur Night, held on Wednesday nights since 1934. The audience is famously tough. If they like a performer, they cheer; if not, they boo, and a figure called the Executioner sweeps the performer off the stage. In November 1934, a seventeen-year-old girl named Ella Fitzgerald, who had planned to dance, lost her nerve and decided to sing instead. She won. It launched her career. Other performers who were discovered or got early breaks at the Apollo include Billie Holiday, Sarah Vaughan, James Brown, Gladys Knight, the Jackson Five, Stevie Wonder, Lauryn Hill, and many more.

Look for the Tree of Hope, a stump on the stage. It's a piece of an elm that once stood outside on Seventh Avenue, between the Lafayette Theatre and Connie's Inn. Performers rubbed the tree for luck. When it was cut down in the 1930s, a piece was saved and placed on the Apollo stage, and amateur performers still rub it for luck before they go on.

James Brown recorded his album Live at the Apollo here in October 1962. Released in 1963, it became one of the most celebrated live albums ever made.

Look along 125th Street. It's officially named Martin Luther King Junior Boulevard. Throughout the twentieth century, it was the commercial and political heart of Harlem, lined with stores, theaters, and restaurants. For decades, many of its stores were owned by white merchants who refused to hire Black workers. In the 1930s, Harlem activists launched the Don't Buy Where You Can't Work campaign, boycotting and picketing stores until they hired Black employees. The campaign was one of the first major direct-action civil rights campaigns in the North.

Since the 1990s, national chains have moved onto 125th Street, and in 2008, the city rezoned the street for taller buildings, changes that have been both welcomed and resented.
=== STOP The Hotel Theresa @ 40.8090,-73.9481
Walk to the corner of 125th Street and Adam Clayton Powell Junior Boulevard, also known as Seventh Avenue. Look at the tall white terracotta building on the corner. This is the former Hotel Theresa.

When it opened in 1913, the Theresa was a hotel for white guests only, even as the neighborhood around it became predominantly Black. In 1940, under new ownership, it opened to Black guests and quickly became the most important Black hotel in the country. It was nicknamed the Waldorf of Harlem. At a time when most hotels in Midtown Manhattan wouldn't accept Black guests, the Theresa hosted Black celebrities, athletes, and musicians. Joe Louis, the heavyweight boxing champion, celebrated his victories here, with crowds filling the street below. Jimi Hendrix, Little Richard, Duke Ellington, Josephine Baker, and Dinah Washington stayed here.

In September 1960, the Theresa became the center of world attention. Fidel Castro, the new leader of Cuba, came to New York for a meeting of the United Nations General Assembly. After a dispute with his Midtown hotel, he moved his entire delegation to the Theresa in Harlem, a deliberate political statement. He was visited there by Malcolm X and by the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev, who came up to Harlem to meet him. Crowds gathered on the street outside. A few weeks later, the presidential candidate John F. Kennedy campaigned outside the Theresa.

Malcolm X later rented offices in the Theresa for his new Organization of Afro-American Unity, which he founded in 1964 after splitting from the Nation of Islam. In February 1965, Malcolm X was assassinated while giving a speech at the Audubon Ballroom in Washington Heights.

The hotel closed in 1966 and was converted into offices. Its distinctive white facade remains.

Across the street, the tall office tower is the Adam Clayton Powell Junior State Office Building, completed in the 1970s, on the site where Black activists had hoped to build a community center. Its construction led to protests and an occupation of the site in 1969. In front of it, look for the statue of Adam Clayton Powell Junior, whom you'll hear about at Abyssinian Baptist Church.
=== STOP The Schomburg Center @ 40.8143,-73.9409
Walk north on Malcolm X Boulevard, also known as Lenox Avenue, to 135th Street. On the corner is the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, one of the world's leading research libraries devoted to the history and culture of people of African descent.

It began with the collection of Arturo Alfonso Schomburg, an Afro-Puerto Rican scholar and collector who arrived in New York in 1891. As a schoolboy in Puerto Rico, Schomburg had been told by a teacher that Black people had no history. He spent his life proving the teacher wrong, gathering books, manuscripts, art, and artifacts documenting the achievements of people of African descent. In 1926, the New York Public Library bought his collection, with funding from the Carnegie Corporation, and placed it here, in the 135th Street branch library. Schomburg later became the curator.

The library, and its neighborhood, became a hub of the Harlem Renaissance. Writers met, read, and debated at the library. The poet Countee Cullen and the writer Zora Neale Hurston were among the many who used its resources.

Step into the lobby and look down. Under the floor of the atrium, beneath a cosmogram, a circular design inspired by African and African American symbols, lie the ashes of the poet Langston Hughes. The design incorporates the words of his poem The Negro Speaks of Rivers, with rivers radiating out from the center. Hughes's interment here honors his deep connection to this library.

Langston Hughes was the leading poet of the Harlem Renaissance, and his poetry, with its jazz and blues rhythms and its portraits of everyday Black life, made him one of the most important American writers of the twentieth century. His poem Harlem, which asks what happens to a dream deferred, inspired the title of Lorraine Hansberry's play A Raisin in the Sun.

Across Lenox Avenue, look for Harlem Hospital, which has a series of murals painted in the 1930s by Black artists, including Charles Alston, as part of the Works Progress Administration. They were among the first government commissions given to Black artists, and they depict Black life, history, and medicine.
=== STOP Abyssinian Baptist Church @ 40.8167,-73.9404
Walk to West 138th Street, between Lenox Avenue and Adam Clayton Powell Boulevard. Look for the Gothic stone church, Abyssinian Baptist Church, one of the oldest and most influential Black churches in the United States.

Its origins go back to 1808, in lower Manhattan. Black members of the First Baptist Church, along with some Ethiopian merchants and sailors visiting the city, were forced to sit in segregated seating. They walked out and founded their own congregation, naming it Abyssinian, after the old name for Ethiopia. The church moved several times, following its members uptown, and in 1923, it built this church in Harlem.

The church's most famous pastors were a father and son. Adam Clayton Powell Senior led the church from 1908 to 1937 and oversaw its move to Harlem. Under his leadership, it became one of the largest Protestant congregations in the country. His son, Adam Clayton Powell Junior, succeeded him as pastor and became one of the most important Black political figures in American history. In the 1930s, he led protests and boycotts, including the Don't Buy Where You Can't Work campaign. In 1944, he was elected to Congress, the first Black congressman from New York. In Congress, he became chairman of the powerful Education and Labor Committee, where he helped pass major legislation of the 1960s, including laws on minimum wage, education, and anti-poverty programs. He was also flamboyant, defiant, and controversial, and he was eventually expelled from Congress in 1967 over ethics charges, though the Supreme Court later ruled the expulsion unconstitutional.

Another historical connection: the German theologian Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who later became famous for resisting the Nazis, spent a year in New York in 1930 and 1931, studying at Union Theological Seminary. He worshipped at Abyssinian and taught Sunday school here. He said that his experience in the Black church in Harlem shaped his theology, and his commitment to standing with the oppressed.

Today, Abyssinian's Sunday services, with their powerful gospel music, draw so many tourists that the church has had to set rules to make sure members have seats. If you go, dress respectfully and arrive early.
=== STOP The Savoy Ballroom & Lenox Avenue @ 40.8180,-73.9382
Walk to Lenox Avenue between 140th and 141st Streets. Today, there's an apartment complex here. Look for a plaque marking the site of the Savoy Ballroom, which stood here from 1926 to 1958.

The Savoy took up an entire block front, with a dance floor that held thousands of people, and two bandstands at either end, so the music never stopped. It was nicknamed the Home of Happy Feet. Crucially, it was integrated: Black and white dancers danced together, at a time when that was rare and, in many places, illegal.

The Savoy is where the Lindy Hop was born, a fast, acrobatic swing dance that became a national craze. According to a widely told story, in 1928, a dancer named George Shorty Snowden was asked what the dance was called, and, thinking of Charles Lindbergh's recent solo flight across the Atlantic, called it the Lindy Hop. Dancers like Frankie Manning, who invented the first aerial moves, took the dance to new heights, literally.

The Savoy was also famous for its battles of the bands. The house band, led by the drummer Chick Webb, with a young Ella Fitzgerald as his singer, famously took on visiting bands. In a celebrated 1937 battle, Webb's band went up against Benny Goodman's orchestra, and many in the crowd thought Webb won.

A few blocks south, at the corner of Lenox Avenue and 142nd Street, stood the original Cotton Club. It was owned by gangsters, including Owney Madden, and it was a whites-only club, despite being in Harlem, featuring Black performers in shows that often played to racist stereotypes of the jungle and the plantation. It was nonetheless a launching pad for some of the greatest musicians of the era. Duke Ellington's orchestra played there from 1927 to 1931, broadcasting on the radio nationwide, which made Ellington famous. Cab Calloway, Lena Horne, and Ethel Waters also performed there.

Farther south, at West 118th Street, Minton's Playhouse, in the Hotel Cecil, was where in the early 1940s musicians including Thelonious Monk, Dizzy Gillespie, Charlie Parker, and Kenny Clarke jammed late at night and helped invent bebop, the fast, complex style that became the foundation of modern jazz.

And at Lenox Avenue and 126th Street, look for Sylvia's, opened in 1962 by Sylvia Woods, known as the Queen of Soul Food. It became a Harlem institution, visited by presidents, celebrities, and tourists from around the world.
=== STOP Strivers' Row @ 40.8181,-73.9442
Walk west to West 138th and 139th Streets, between Adam Clayton Powell Junior Boulevard and Frederick Douglass Boulevard. These two blocks, officially the St. Nicholas Historic District, are known as Strivers' Row, and they're among the most beautiful residential blocks in New York.

They were built in 1891 to 1893 by a developer named David King, who hired three of the leading architectural firms of the day to design them, each with a distinct style. James Brown Lord designed the red brick Georgian houses on the south side of 138th Street. Bruce Price and Clarence Luce designed the yellow brick and limestone Colonial Revival houses on the north side of 138th and the south side of 139th. And McKim, Mead and White, the most prestigious firm in the country, designed the dark brick Italian Renaissance houses on the north side of 139th Street. Together, they form a rare example of a unified, planned development, with houses sharing rear alleys and service courts.

Look for the signs on the gateposts to the rear alleys, reading Walk your horses. The alleys were used for deliveries and for horses and carriages to reach the stables.

Originally, the houses were built for wealthy white families. But the houses were built just before an economic downturn, and as Harlem's population changed, they didn't sell well to white buyers. By 1919 and 1920, the houses were sold to African American buyers, many of them doctors, lawyers, musicians, and other professionals. The neighborhood got its nickname because it was seen as a place for ambitious, upwardly mobile Black Harlemites, strivers.

Residents over the years included the composer and bandleader W. C. Handy, known as the Father of the Blues; the bandleader Fletcher Henderson; and the surgeon Louis T. Wright, one of the first Black doctors on the staff of a New York City hospital.

Strivers' Row remains one of the most prestigious addresses in Harlem. As you walk along the blocks, notice how carefully the houses have been maintained, with their stoops, ironwork, and doorways. It's a reminder of the Black middle and upper class that has always been part of Harlem's story.
=== STOP Sugar Hill & Hamilton Grange @ 40.8211,-73.9473
End your walk on the hill to the west, near St. Nicholas Park and West 141st Street. This is Hamilton Heights, and just to the north is Sugar Hill.

In St. Nicholas Park, look for a yellow Federal-style house with a porch. This is Hamilton Grange, the only house Alexander Hamilton ever owned. He built it in 1802 as a country retreat, on a thirty-two-acre estate, naming it after his father's ancestral home in Scotland. He only lived here for about two years before he was killed in his duel with Aaron Burr in 1804.

The house has moved twice. In 1889, as the neighborhood was developed, it was moved a short distance to make way for the street grid and squeezed between a church and an apartment building, on Convent Avenue. In 2008, the National Park Service moved it again, lifting the entire house over the church's portico and rolling it down the street to its current location in the park, closer to its original setting. Inside, you can see rooms restored to Hamilton's era. Tours are free.

Up the hill to the west is the campus of the City College of New York, with its striking Gothic buildings, built from dark Manhattan schist excavated during construction of the subway, and trimmed with white terracotta. City College was founded in 1847 as the Free Academy, offering free higher education to the children of the working class. For decades, it was called the Harvard of the proletariat, and it educated generations of immigrants and their children, including many future Nobel Prize winners.

Sugar Hill, the area north of here roughly between 145th and 155th Streets, got its name in the 1920s and 30s because it was where Harlem's sweet life was lived: the most prosperous and successful Black New Yorkers lived here. Two apartment buildings on Edgecombe Avenue became legendary. At 409 Edgecombe Avenue lived W. E. B. Du Bois, the civil rights leader and scholar; Thurgood Marshall, who argued Brown versus Board of Education and became the first Black Supreme Court justice; Walter White and Roy Wilkins, leaders of the NAACP; and many others. At 555 Edgecombe Avenue lived the actor and singer Paul Robeson, the bandleader Count Basie, and the boxer Joe Louis, among others.

Duke Ellington's famous theme song, Take the A Train, written by Billy Strayhorn in 1939, was about the subway line that took you to Sugar Hill.
