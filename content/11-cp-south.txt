title: Central Park: The Southern Park
area: Manhattan
blurb: Olmsted and Vaux's masterpiece, from the Pond and the Dairy to the Mall, Bethesda Fountain, Bow Bridge, and Strawberry Fields.
=== OVERVIEW
Central Park looks natural. It isn't. Almost every rock outcrop, lake, meadow, and hill you see was planned, sculpted, and planted by people. It's one of the largest and most ambitious works of landscape design ever made, and it's been copied in cities all over the world.

By the 1840s, New York was growing fast and had almost no public green space. The 1811 grid plan had allotted very little land to parks, on the theory that the rivers on either side would give residents fresh air. Wealthy New Yorkers, who had seen the great public parks of London and Paris, began to argue that a world-class city needed a great park. The poet and editor William Cullen Bryant and the landscape designer Andrew Jackson Downing were among the leading advocates. In 1853, the state legislature authorized the city to acquire the land, and the park was eventually expanded to its current size of about eight hundred forty acres, stretching from 59th Street to 110th Street, and from Fifth Avenue to Central Park West.

The land wasn't empty. It was rocky, swampy, and considered poor for building, but it was home to about sixteen hundred people, including Irish and German immigrants who raised pigs and goats, and a community of African American property owners called Seneca Village. All were removed through eminent domain. You'll hear more about Seneca Village on the northern Central Park tour.

In 1857, the park's commissioners held a design competition. The winning entry, called the Greensward Plan, came from two men: Frederick Law Olmsted, a journalist, farmer, and failed businessman who had never designed a park, and Calvert Vaux, a young English-born architect. Their vision was a pastoral escape from the city, a sequence of carefully composed scenes, meadows, woods, water, and rocky outcrops, meant to feel like an unspoiled landscape. They believed the park could improve the health and spirit of all New Yorkers, rich and poor alike, and offer a democratic space where all classes could mix.

Building it was a colossal undertaking. Over roughly fifteen years, thousands of workers blasted rock with more gunpowder than was used at the Battle of Gettysburg, according to a frequently repeated claim, moved millions of cubic yards of earth and stone, planted hundreds of thousands of trees and shrubs, and built bridges, arches, and buildings. One of the cleverest features was the design for traffic. Olmsted and Vaux sunk four transverse roads below the level of the park, so that crosstown traffic would pass through without disturbing the landscape. And they separated carriage drives, bridle paths, and footpaths, using bridges and arches so they rarely crossed.

The park declined badly in the mid-twentieth century, and by the 1970s it was covered in graffiti, its lawns were dust bowls, and many considered it dangerous. In 1980, the Central Park Conservancy, a private nonprofit, was founded to restore it, and it now manages the park in partnership with the city.

This tour covers the southern half, the more formal and heavily visited part of the park.
=== STOP The Pond & Gapstow Bridge @ 40.7661,-73.9741
You're at the southeast corner of Central Park, near Fifth Avenue and 60th Street, beside the body of water called the Pond.

Olmsted and Vaux designed the park's entrances carefully. They wanted visitors to leave the city behind within a few steps. Here, the path descends from the busy street to the water's edge, and the noise of the city begins to fade. Notice how the Pond curves out of sight, so you can't see its full extent. This was a classic technique of English landscape design: hide the edges, and the space feels larger and more mysterious.

Look for the arched stone bridge over the northern neck of the Pond. That's Gapstow Bridge. The current stone bridge dates from 1896, replacing an earlier, more ornate wooden and iron bridge by Jacob Wrey Mould. It's one of the most photographed spots in the park, because of the view south, with the towers of Midtown, including the Plaza Hotel and the needle-thin towers of Billionaires' Row, rising above the trees. It's a perfect demonstration of the park's central contrast: wild nature, framed by the city.

On the peninsula jutting into the Pond is the Hallett Nature Sanctuary, a small woodland that was closed to the public for decades to serve as a bird sanctuary. It's now open on a limited schedule. It's named after George Hallett, an amateur birder and conservationist.

In winter, the Pond and the rink beyond it fill with skaters. In the nineteenth century, ice skating was hugely popular in the park, and it was one of the first activities that brought huge crowds to Central Park, even before construction was complete. Skating was also one of the few activities in Victorian New York where men and women could socialize relatively freely.

The park was the first major landscaped public park in America, and when it opened, there was a lot of debate about how people should behave in it. The park's early rules were strict: no walking on the grass in many areas, no picnics, no ball playing for adults, and no commercial vehicles. Over time, as the park became more democratic, those rules loosened.
=== STOP The Arsenal & the Zoo @ 40.7677,-73.9714
Walk north along the eastern side of the park, near 64th Street and Fifth Avenue.

The brick building with the corner towers that looks like a fortress is the Arsenal. It was built between 1847 and 1851, before the park existed, as a storehouse for New York State's munitions. It's one of only two buildings in the park that predate the park itself. Later, it served as a museum, a weather station, and a police precinct, and the American Museum of Natural History got its start here in the 1870s, before its building on the Upper West Side was completed. Today, it's the headquarters of the city's Parks Department. If you peek in, the lobby has murals from the 1930s, painted as part of a Depression-era federal arts program.

The Central Park Zoo, next to the Arsenal, began informally in the 1860s, when New Yorkers started donating animals to the park: a bear, a swan, monkeys, and others. They were kept around the Arsenal in what became known as the menagerie. In 1934, Robert Moses built a formal zoo with brick buildings and cages. By the 1980s, it was outdated, and the Wildlife Conservation Society, which runs the Bronx Zoo, redesigned it with naturalistic habitats. It reopened in 1988. Look for the sea lion pool in the center, and the penguins and snow leopards.

At the north end of the zoo, look up at the Delacorte Musical Clock, an archway topped by a clock and bronze animals: a bear with a tambourine, a hippo with a violin, a goat with pipes, a kangaroo with horns, and others. Every half hour, they rotate around the clock while it plays nursery rhymes. The clock was a gift from the publisher George Delacorte, who founded Dell Publishing, and installed in 1965. Delacorte, a philanthropist, also funded the Delacorte Theater, home of Shakespeare in the Park, and the Alice in Wonderland sculpture near the Conservatory Water.

Nearby is the Tisch Children's Zoo. And a little to the north is the Conservatory Water, where people race model sailboats on summer weekends, a scene immortalized in the children's book Stuart Little by E. B. White.
=== STOP The Dairy & the Carousel @ 40.7683,-73.9750
Walk west into the park to a small Gothic building with a steep roof and a wooden porch with ornate gingerbread trim. This is the Dairy, built in 1870 and designed by Calvert Vaux.

The Dairy was part of Olmsted and Vaux's vision for the Children's District, the southern part of the park that was meant to be especially for families. It served a practical purpose. In the mid-nineteenth century, milk in New York was dangerous. Much of it came from cows kept in filthy city stables next to breweries and fed on distillery waste, called swill. Swill milk was blamed for the deaths of thousands of children every year. At the Dairy, families could buy fresh, safe milk from cows grazing in the country. There were even cows grazing on the lawn nearby. Today the Dairy serves as a visitor center and gift shop.

A short walk west is the Carousel, one of the largest carousels in the United States. There's been a carousel in this spot since 1871. The first one was powered by a horse or mule walking in circles in a pit underneath it. The current carousel, with its colorful hand-carved horses, was built in 1908 by the carvers Solomon Stein and Harry Goldstein, Russian Jewish immigrants who were among the master carvers of Coney Island-style carousels. It originally stood at an amusement park in Coney Island and was moved here in 1951 after an earlier carousel burned.

Nearby, look for the Chess and Checkers House, a small building where you can borrow game pieces and play at outdoor tables. Chess hustlers have long been a fixture in New York parks.

And down the hill to the south is the Wollman Rink, which opened in 1950 with money from the philanthropist Kate Wollman. In winter it's one of the most famous skating rinks in the world, with the skyline of Central Park South rising above it.
=== STOP Sheep Meadow @ 40.7718,-73.9752
You're at the edge of Sheep Meadow, a vast, open lawn of about fifteen acres between roughly 66th and 69th Streets. On a sunny weekend, it's covered with sunbathers, picnickers, and people reading. It's a designated quiet zone: no ball games, no loud music, no dogs.

Olmsted and Vaux designed this meadow as a large open pastoral space, something rare and precious in a dense city. They wanted people to experience the expansiveness of rolling countryside, with a long view toward the trees and the sky. The original plan actually called it the Parade Ground, but Olmsted resisted using it for military drills.

And it really did have sheep. From the 1860s to 1934, a flock of purebred sheep grazed here, tended by a shepherd who lived in a building called the Sheepfold. The sheep kept the grass trimmed and added to the pastoral illusion. Twice a day, the shepherd would stop traffic on the West Drive to lead the flock across. In 1934, Robert Moses removed the sheep, sending them to Prospect Park in Brooklyn, and converted the Sheepfold into a restaurant. That restaurant is Tavern on the Green, at the western edge of the park near 67th Street.

In the 1960s and 70s, Sheep Meadow became a gathering place for the counterculture, with Be-Ins, protests against the Vietnam War, and concerts. Heavy use turned it into a dust bowl. In 1980, it was one of the first major restoration projects of the new Central Park Conservancy, which reseeded it and introduced rules to protect it, including closing it in winter and when the ground is wet.

Look south, over the lawn, at the skyline of Central Park South and Billionaires' Row. The tall, thin towers you see were designed to exploit this view. It's often said that the view of Central Park is the most valuable view in real estate.
=== STOP The Mall & Literary Walk @ 40.7718,-73.9722
You're at the southern end of the Mall, the wide, straight promenade lined with towering elm trees that runs from about 66th to 72nd Streets.

The Mall is the only formal, straight-line feature in Olmsted and Vaux's design. Everything else in the park curves and meanders. But they believed that a great urban park needed one grand promenade, a place where people of all classes could stroll, see and be seen. In the nineteenth century, it was the most fashionable place in the park, where people paraded in their finest clothes on Sunday afternoons.

Look up at the trees. These are American elms, and the Mall has one of the largest and finest remaining stands of American elms in North America. Elms were once the most common street tree in America, until Dutch elm disease, a fungus spread by beetles, killed most of them in the twentieth century. Central Park's elms survived, thanks partly to the park's relative isolation and careful monitoring by the Conservancy. Their branches arch overhead like the vaulted ceiling of a cathedral.

At the southern end of the Mall, look for the statues of writers. This section is called the Literary Walk. You'll find William Shakespeare, the Scottish poets Robert Burns and Sir Walter Scott, and the American poet Fitz-Greene Halleck. There's also a statue of Christopher Columbus. Olmsted and Vaux didn't want statues in their park, which they saw as a landscape, not a museum. But public pressure to honor famous figures was intense, and statues were installed anyway, mostly here in the southern part of the park.

At the northern end of the Mall, look for the Naumburg Bandshell, a limestone bandshell built in 1923, a gift of the banker Elkan Naumburg. Free concerts have been held here for a century, and the area in front of it was the site of political rallies and the funerals of famous figures. Nearby, on summer weekends, you'll often find roller skaters dancing to music, a tradition that dates back to the 1970s.

Before you leave, notice the people around you. The Mall still does what its designers intended: everyone is here, from tourists and joggers to musicians, families, and old men on benches.
=== STOP Bethesda Terrace & Fountain @ 40.7740,-73.9708
At the northern end of the Mall, walk down the grand staircase, or through the arcade beneath the road, to Bethesda Terrace.

Look up as you pass through the arcade. The ceiling is covered in about sixteen thousand ornate encaustic tiles made by the English company Minton. They were designed by Jacob Wrey Mould, Vaux's collaborator, who also designed much of the decorative carving on the terrace. After decades of neglect, the tiles were removed, stored, and restored, and reinstalled in 2007. It's one of the most beautiful spaces in the park, and the acoustics make it a favorite spot for musicians.

On the terrace, look closely at the stone carvings on the staircases. They depict birds, plants, and animals representing the seasons and times of day.

At the center of the terrace is the Bethesda Fountain, topped by a bronze statue called the Angel of the Waters, by the sculptor Emma Stebbins. Unveiled in 1873, it was the first public sculpture in New York City commissioned from a woman. The angel refers to the Gospel of John, in which an angel stirs the waters of the Pool of Bethesda in Jerusalem, giving them healing powers. The symbolism had a local meaning: the fountain celebrated the Croton Aqueduct, which in 1842 had brought clean water to New York for the first time, dramatically reducing diseases like cholera. Pure water, in other words, was New York's healing angel.

There's another layer. Emma Stebbins was in a long-term relationship with the actress Charlotte Cushman, and the fountain has become meaningful to the LGBTQ community. It appears prominently in Tony Kushner's play Angels in America, which ends at this fountain.

Bethesda Terrace was designed as the heart of the park, the place where the formal Mall meets the wild landscape. Stand at the fountain and look north across the Lake toward the Ramble's woods, and you'll see the composition Olmsted and Vaux intended: the architecture gives way to nature. In the 1970s, the fountain was dry, covered in graffiti, and a center of drug dealing. It was among the first places the Conservancy restored, and it's now one of the most beloved spots in the city.
=== STOP Bow Bridge & the Lake @ 40.7757,-73.9718
Walk west along the Lake to Bow Bridge, the graceful cast iron bridge arching across the water.

Completed in 1862, Bow Bridge was designed by Calvert Vaux and Jacob Wrey Mould. Its name comes from its shape, like the curve of an archer's bow. It's about sixty feet long, with a decorative railing of interlocking circles and planters. It's one of the oldest cast iron bridges in America, and probably the most romantic spot in the park. It has appeared in many films, and countless marriage proposals have happened on it.

The bridge connects Cherry Hill and the Bethesda area to the Ramble, the wild woodland on the north shore of the Lake. That contrast is deliberate. Crossing Bow Bridge, you move from the formal, social part of the park to the wild, solitary part.

The Lake itself is entirely artificial, created from a swamp. It covers about twenty acres. In the nineteenth century, it was the main skating spot in the park, and in winter, tens of thousands of skaters would flock here. When the ice was thick enough to be safe, the park raised a red ball on a flagpole, and streetcars would display a similar signal, letting New Yorkers across the city know that skating was open. The Lake is no longer used for skating, now that there are rinks.

In summer, you can rent rowboats at the Loeb Boathouse, on the east side of the Lake, where there's also a restaurant. Rowing on the Lake has been a popular activity since the 1860s. In the nineteenth century, the park also had gondolas, and even a gondolier imported from Venice.

Look around at the trees and the skyline beyond. From the middle of the bridge, the towers of the Upper West Side, including the twin-towered San Remo and the Dakota, rise above the trees, perfectly framed by the park's landscape.

Just west of the bridge is Cherry Hill, a raised plaza with a small fountain. Originally it was a turnaround for carriages, and the fountain was a watering trough for horses.
=== STOP Strawberry Fields @ 40.7757,-73.9750
End your walk at Strawberry Fields, a teardrop-shaped garden on the western edge of the park, near West 72nd Street.

This is a memorial to John Lennon. On the evening of December eighth, 1980, Lennon was shot and killed outside the Dakota, the apartment building where he lived with Yoko Ono, just across Central Park West from here. He was forty years old. The killing devastated fans around the world, and on the following Sunday, hundreds of thousands of people gathered in Central Park for ten minutes of silence.

Yoko Ono, who continued to live at the Dakota, worked with the city to create a memorial in the park. Strawberry Fields, named after the Beatles song Strawberry Fields Forever, was dedicated in 1985, on what would have been Lennon's forty-fifth birthday. It was designed by the landscape architect Bruce Kelly. Ono invited countries around the world to contribute plants, and many did.

At the center of the garden, look for the black and white mosaic with a single word: Imagine. The mosaic was a gift from the city of Naples, Italy, and it's modeled on ancient Roman mosaics. On any given day, you'll find flowers left on it, often arranged into a peace sign, and musicians playing Beatles songs. On December eighth and on Lennon's birthday, October ninth, crowds gather here to sing.

The area is designated a quiet zone, meant for contemplation, though it's often busy with visitors.

Look across the street at the Dakota, the dark, gabled building with its own fortress-like entrance. You'll hear more about it on the Upper West Side tour. It's one of the most famous apartment buildings in the world.

This is a good place to reflect on the park as a whole. Olmsted and Vaux designed Central Park as a place of escape and renewal. More than a century later, it's become something they couldn't have foreseen: the city's collective memory, a place where New Yorkers come to celebrate, protest, mourn, and remember.
