=== TOUR
id: fidi
title: Financial District & the Battery
area: Manhattan
blurb: Where New York began: Dutch streets, Steamship Row, Wall Street's canyons, and the World Trade Center.
=== OVERVIEW
Welcome to the southern tip of Manhattan, the oldest part of New York City and, for most of its history, the whole of it.

Before any Europeans arrived, this was Lenape country. The Lenape called the island something close to Manahatta, often translated as the island of many hills. The hills are mostly gone now, shaved down and dumped into the rivers, but the street plan you are walking on still remembers the people who came next. In 1624 and 1625 the Dutch West India Company established a trading post here called New Amsterdam. It was a company town in the most literal sense: a commercial venture built to ship beaver pelts back to Europe. In that respect, almost nothing has changed. Lower Manhattan was founded as a business, and it has been a business ever since.

Notice how the streets down here twist and wander. North of about Houston Street, Manhattan is famous for its rigid grid, laid down by the Commissioners' Plan of 1811. But down here, the streets predate the grid. They follow old cow paths, property lines, a canal, and the original shoreline. Pearl Street was once literally the edge of the East River. Water Street, Front Street, and South Street were each added, one after another, as merchants dumped fill into the river to create more valuable waterfront land. If you are standing anywhere east of Pearl Street, you are standing on what used to be river. The same is true on the west side, where the entire neighborhood of Battery Park City was built on fill excavated from the original World Trade Center site.

Wall Street itself takes its name from a wooden palisade the Dutch built in 1653 to protect against attack from the north, by the English or by the Lenape, depending on which year's anxieties you consult. The English took the colony in 1664 without a fight and renamed it New York, after the Duke of York. The wall came down in 1699. The name stayed.

In the nineteenth century, this district became the counting house of the nation. The Erie Canal opened in 1825 and made New York the gateway between the Atlantic and the interior of the continent. Grain, cotton, and capital flowed through these blocks. Banks, insurers, and shipping lines crowded together, because in an age before telephones, physical proximity was information. The Great Fire of 1835 burned hundreds of buildings in the area, which is one reason so little of colonial New York survives, and much of what replaced it was rebuilt bigger, in stone.

By the early twentieth century, the district had become the birthplace of the skyscraper race. Land was so expensive and so scarce that the only direction left was up. Walk these streets and you will notice that they feel like canyons. Many are barely wider than they were in the 1600s, but the buildings lining them are forty, fifty, sixty stories tall. That tension, a medieval street plan with modern towers stacked on top, is the signature experience of the Financial District.

Here is something to look for as you walk. Along lower Broadway, near Bowling Green, stood what was called Steamship Row. In the early 1900s, the great transatlantic shipping lines kept their ticket offices and headquarters here, close to the harbor and the customs house. The Cunard Line, the White Star Line's parent company, and others sold passage to Europe from grand halls along this stretch. Some of those buildings survive, with nautical carvings of ships, shells, and sea creatures still on their facades.

On this tour we will start at the Battery, where the harbor opens up, and work our way north past Bowling Green, the old Dutch streets, Wall Street, Trinity Church, the Federal Reserve, and finally the World Trade Center site. Along the way, keep looking up. The best details in this neighborhood are often thirty feet above your head.
=== STOP Castle Clinton & Battery Park @ 40.7036,-74.0168
You are in Battery Park, named for the artillery batteries that once defended the harbor. The round sandstone fort in front of you is Castle Clinton, and it may be the most reinvented building in New York.

It was built between 1808 and 1811, when tensions with Britain were rising toward what became the War of 1812. When it was built, it did not sit in a park. It stood on a small artificial island about two hundred feet offshore, connected to Manhattan by a wooden bridge. Everything between here and the old shoreline is fill. The fort was armed with heavy cannon, but it never fired a shot in anger. In 1815 it was renamed for DeWitt Clinton, the mayor and later governor who would go on to champion the Erie Canal.

The army gave the fort to the city in the 1820s, and it became Castle Garden, an entertainment venue with a roof added over the open center. This was one of the most fashionable places in New York. In 1850, the showman P. T. Barnum brought the Swedish soprano Jenny Lind here for her American debut, and the publicity frenzy he built around her is often described as one of the first modern celebrity marketing campaigns. Tickets were auctioned off. The demand was extraordinary.

Then the building changed purpose entirely. From 1855 to 1890, Castle Garden served as New York State's immigrant landing depot. Roughly eight million people entered America through this building, before Ellis Island existed. If your ancestors arrived in New York in the mid-nineteenth century, many of them from Ireland and Germany, there is a good chance they walked through here. The operation was meant to protect new arrivals from the swindlers, fake boarding house agents, and con artists who worked the docks.

After Ellis Island took over federal immigration processing, the building was reinvented again, this time as the New York Aquarium, which opened in 1896 and became one of the most popular attractions in the city. In the 1940s, the parks commissioner Robert Moses moved the aquarium to Coney Island and wanted to tear the fort down entirely, partly to make way for his plans for a bridge to Brooklyn. Preservationists fought him, and the fort was eventually designated a national monument. Today it is where you buy tickets for the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island ferries.

Look out across the harbor. The Statue of Liberty stands on Liberty Island, a gift from France dedicated in 1886. To its right, Ellis Island. Farther left, you can see Governors Island, a former army and Coast Guard base that is now a public park, and beyond it, Brooklyn.

Somewhere in this park you will also find The Sphere, a large, damaged bronze sculpture by the German artist Fritz Koenig. It originally stood in the plaza between the Twin Towers. It survived the collapse on September eleventh, battered and torn, and was moved here, and later to Liberty Park near the World Trade Center site. Its scars were deliberately left in place.
=== STOP Bowling Green & Steamship Row @ 40.7049,-74.0138
This small oval is Bowling Green, the oldest public park in New York City. In 1733, the city leased it to three neighbors for the token rent of one peppercorn a year, on the condition that they turn it into a bowling green for the recreation of the inhabitants. The Dutch had used this spot earlier as a cattle market and parade ground.

Look at the iron fence surrounding the green. It dates to 1771, which makes it one of the oldest surviving fences in the city. It once had small crowns ornamenting its posts. In 1770, the city had installed a gilded lead equestrian statue of King George the Third in the center of the green. On July ninth, 1776, after the Declaration of Independence was read to George Washington's troops in New York, a crowd marched here, pulled the statue down, and sawed off the crowns from the fence posts. Much of the lead from the statue was reportedly shipped to Connecticut and melted into musket balls for the Continental Army. Pieces of it still turn up in museum collections.

At the north tip of the green is the Charging Bull, the bronze sculpture by Arturo Di Modica. He installed it without permission in December 1989, dropping it overnight in front of the New York Stock Exchange as a gift to the city after the 1987 market crash. The police removed it, public outcry followed, and the city found it a new home here.

Now turn and look at the buildings around you. This stretch of lower Broadway was Steamship Row. In the age of the great ocean liners, a trip to Europe began with a visit to one of these offices. Look for 25 Broadway, the Cunard Building, completed in 1921. Cunard was the British line that operated famous ships like the Lusitania and, later, the Queen Mary. Its ground floor booking hall was one of the most lavish interiors in the city, with a vaulted, painted ceiling meant to evoke the great domed halls of the Renaissance. The building's ornament is full of maritime references.

Across the way, at One Broadway, stood the offices of the International Mercantile Marine Company, J. P. Morgan's shipping trust, which controlled the White Star Line, owner of the Titanic. Look at the entrances: one door is marked First Class and another Cabin Class, a leftover from when you bought your ticket according to your station in life.

Now look at 26 Broadway, the long, curved building that follows the bend of the street. This was the headquarters of John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil Company, which at its peak controlled the overwhelming majority of oil refining in the United States. The company was broken up by the Supreme Court in 1911, and its pieces became, among others, the ancestors of ExxonMobil and Chevron. The top of the tower is designed to suggest an oil lamp, a quiet reminder that Standard Oil's first fortune came from kerosene for lighting, not gasoline for cars.

Finally, the massive building facing the south end of the green is the Alexander Hamilton United States Custom House, designed by Cass Gilbert and completed in 1907. Before the federal income tax arrived in 1913, customs duties were a principal source of federal revenue, and the Port of New York collected a large share of them. The building looks like a temple to commerce because it was one. The four large sculptures in front, by Daniel Chester French, represent four continents, and the twelve figures along the cornice represent great seafaring powers of history. Today it houses a branch of the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, which is free, and its oval rotunda, with murals of ships entering the harbor, is worth a look if it's open.
=== STOP Stone Street, Fraunces Tavern & Delmonico's @ 40.7043,-74.0105
You are now in the oldest surviving fabric of the street plan. Stone Street, the narrow lane lined with nineteenth century buildings and outdoor tables, takes its name from the fact that it was reportedly one of the first paved streets in New Amsterdam, cobbled in the 1650s. The Dutch knew it as Brouwer Straat, Brewers Street, because breweries lined it. The buildings you see today mostly went up right after the Great Fire of 1835 destroyed the neighborhood. They were warehouses and counting rooms for merchants. Today it is one of the few places downtown where you can feel the scale of the old city.

A short walk away, at the corner of Pearl and Broad Streets, is Fraunces Tavern, the brick building with the hipped roof. In 1762, Samuel Fraunces bought the building and opened a tavern called the Queen's Head. It became a meeting place for the Sons of Liberty before the Revolution. Its most famous moment came on December fourth, 1783. The British had just evacuated New York, the war was over, and George Washington gathered his officers in the tavern's Long Room to say goodbye. Accounts describe an emotional scene, with Washington asking each officer to come and take him by the hand. He then walked to the waterfront and left the city, expecting to retire to private life.

A fair caution: the building you see is substantially a 1907 reconstruction, based on careful guesswork about what the colonial original looked like. But it occupies the same spot, and it houses a small museum upstairs.

Notice Pearl Street itself. It probably takes its name from the oyster shells that littered the original shoreline. When New Amsterdam was founded, Pearl Street was the waterfront. Everything to the east of it was water.

Now walk toward the triangle where Beaver Street and South William Street meet. That building, with the columns at its rounded corner entrance, is Delmonico's. The Delmonico brothers, immigrants from the Italian speaking part of Switzerland, opened a small café downtown in 1827, and their family went on to create what is often called the first fine dining restaurant in America: a place where you sat at your own table and ordered from a printed menu, rather than eating whatever the tavern served at a common table. The current building dates from 1837. Delmonico's is credited, with varying degrees of certainty, with popularizing or inventing Lobster Newberg, Eggs Benedict, Baked Alaska, and of course the Delmonico steak. It was the place where Wall Street celebrated its deals.

A block away is Hanover Square, a little triangular park. The handsome brownstone Italianate building on its south side is India House, built in the early 1850s for Hanover Bank. It later housed the New York Cotton Exchange, a reminder that the profits of the slave economy of the American South flowed directly through these streets. New York's banks financed cotton crops, its insurers insured them, and its ships carried them to England. Today it is a private club.
=== STOP Wall Street, Federal Hall & the Stock Exchange @ 40.7071,-74.0104
You are at the intersection of Wall and Broad, arguably the most symbolically loaded street corner in the world.

Start with the building with the Greek temple front and the large bronze statue of George Washington on its steps. This is Federal Hall National Memorial. The building itself dates from 1842 and was built as the United States Custom House, later becoming a branch of the Treasury. But the site is what matters. An earlier building here served as New York's City Hall, and in 1735, it hosted the trial of the printer John Peter Zenger, who was charged with seditious libel for criticizing the colonial governor. His acquittal became an early landmark in the idea of a free press.

After the Revolution, New York was briefly the capital of the United States, and the old City Hall was remodeled as Federal Hall to house the new Congress. On April thirtieth, 1789, George Washington stood on a balcony here and took the oath of office as the first president. The statue, by John Quincy Adams Ward, marks roughly the spot. The first Congress met here, and it was here that it drafted the amendments that became the Bill of Rights.

Now face the building with the six giant Corinthian columns across Broad Street. That's the New York Stock Exchange, completed in 1903 to a design by George B. Post. Look at the sculpture in the pediment above the columns. It is called Integrity Protecting the Works of Man, and it shows a central figure of Integrity with figures representing agriculture, mining, industry, and science. The exchange traces its origins to the Buttonwood Agreement of 1792, when twenty four brokers agreed to trade with each other under fixed commissions, supposedly beneath a buttonwood tree that stood a short distance from here on Wall Street.

Now look at the low, plain, marble building on the southeast corner of Wall and Broad, at 23 Wall Street. It has no name on it. It didn't need one. This was the headquarters of J. P. Morgan and Company, the most powerful private bank in the country, known on the street simply as The Corner. In the Panic of 1907, J. Pierpont Morgan personally organized the rescue of the banking system, a crisis that led directly to the creation of the Federal Reserve a few years later.

Walk up to the Wall Street side of the building and look closely at the stone. You will see pockmarks and gouges. On September sixteenth, 1920, just after noon, a horse-drawn wagon packed with dynamite and iron weights exploded right here. Thirty eight people were killed and hundreds were injured. No one was ever convicted, though anarchists were widely suspected. Morgan's firm chose not to repair the damage. It is still there, a century later.

One more building to notice. Look up along Wall Street for a tower topped by a stepped pyramid. That is 14 Wall Street, built for Bankers Trust, and its pyramid was modeled on the ancient Mausoleum at Halicarnassus. Bankers Trust later made the pyramid its corporate logo. And farther down, at 40 Wall Street, is the tower with the green pyramidal roof and spire, built in 1930 for the Bank of Manhattan Company. It briefly raced the Chrysler Building to be the world's tallest, and lost when the Chrysler's builders secretly assembled a spire inside their dome and raised it into place. The Bank of Manhattan has an interesting origin of its own: it began as the Manhattan Company, chartered in 1799 supposedly to supply the city with clean water. Its real purpose, engineered by Aaron Burr, was to slip a bank charter past Alexander Hamilton's Federalists. Through a long chain of mergers, it is an ancestor of today's JPMorgan Chase.
=== STOP Trinity Church @ 40.7081,-74.0120
At the head of Wall Street, framed perfectly by the canyon of buildings, stands Trinity Church. Its location is no accident. Wall Street was laid out as the church's front walk, in effect. Stand on Wall Street looking west and the church closes the view, like an altar at the end of a nave.

The congregation dates to 1697, chartered by King William the Third, which makes it one of the oldest Anglican, now Episcopal, parishes in the country. The current building is the third on the site. The first burned in the Great Fire of 1776, during the British occupation. The second was weakened by heavy snow. The building you see now was designed by Richard Upjohn and completed in 1846. It's one of the most important early Gothic Revival churches in America, and its brownstone was meant to look like a church from medieval England.

For decades, its spire, about two hundred eighty feet tall, was the tallest structure in New York. Sailors approaching the harbor used it as a landmark. Imagine that: a whole skyline dominated by a church steeple, until the commercial towers overtook it at the end of the nineteenth century. Today it looks small, but in its time, it was the skyline.

Trinity also has one of the great fortunes in New York real estate history. In 1705, Queen Anne granted the church a large tract of farmland on the west side of lower Manhattan. Over three centuries, the church sold much of it, and gave away parcels to found other institutions, but it still owns substantial property, especially in the Hudson Square neighborhood to the north, and its real estate operation remains a major landlord.

Step into the churchyard, which is open most days. Here you'll find some notable graves. Alexander Hamilton is buried here, under a white marble pyramid-topped monument near the south side, with his wife Eliza nearby. Hamilton founded the Bank of New York, which still exists today as part of BNY, and as first Treasury Secretary he designed much of the financial architecture of the young country. He was killed in 1804 in a duel with Aaron Burr, the same Aaron Burr behind the Manhattan Company you just heard about. The two men are, in a sense, still competing on Wall Street.

Also here: Robert Fulton, the inventor and engineer whose steamboat, the Clermont, made its famous run up the Hudson to Albany in 1807 and launched commercial steam navigation in America. Albert Gallatin, Treasury secretary under Jefferson and Madison, is also buried here. So is William Bradford, the colonial printer.

As you stand here, notice how the old gravestones, some dating from the late 1600s, sit in the shadow of glass towers. Trinity's churchyard is one of the only pieces of open ground in this part of the city that has stayed open continuously since the seventeenth century.
=== STOP The Federal Reserve & Chase Manhattan Plaza @ 40.7084,-74.0089
You are now on Liberty Street, in front of a building that looks like a fortified Italian Renaissance palace, which is exactly the effect its architects intended. This is the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, completed in 1924 and designed by the firm York and Sawyer. They modeled it on the palazzos of fifteenth century Florence, like the Strozzi Palace, built for banking families who wanted their wealth to look secure, imposing, and untouchable.

Look at the ironwork on the windows and lanterns. It was made by Samuel Yellin, a Philadelphia metalsmith considered the greatest American ironworker of his era. It is functional security disguised as craft.

The New York Fed is the most important of the twelve regional Federal Reserve banks. It carries out the Fed's open market operations, the buying and selling of securities that steers interest rates. And underneath your feet, roughly eighty feet below street level, sitting directly on Manhattan's bedrock, is a vault holding one of the largest stores of gold in the world. Most of it doesn't belong to the United States. It belongs to foreign governments and central banks, which keep it here for safekeeping. When one country pays another in gold, sometimes the bars are simply moved from one locked compartment to another inside this vault. The New York Fed offers public tours, booked in advance, if you want to see it for yourself.

Now look across the street at the plain, glass-and-aluminum tower set back on a broad open plaza. That's 28 Liberty Street, originally One Chase Manhattan Plaza, finished in 1961 and designed by Gordon Bunshaft of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill. Its story is about confidence. In the 1950s, many companies were fleeing lower Manhattan for Midtown. David Rockefeller, then a rising executive at Chase Manhattan Bank, pushed for the bank to build a giant new headquarters here instead, and his commitment is often credited with helping keep the Financial District alive. He and his brother Nelson later pushed the plans that led to the World Trade Center.

Two pieces of art on the plaza are worth finding. The tall, black-and-white sculpture that looks like a cluster of cartoon trees is Group of Four Trees by Jean Dubuffet, installed in 1972. And set into the plaza is a sunken circular garden by Isamu Noguchi, with stones and patterns meant to evoke a Japanese dry garden. You can look down into it from the plaza level.

This combination, a Renaissance fortress for the nation's money on one side and a sleek modernist tower for private banking on the other, captures the two eras of the Financial District in a single glance.
=== STOP 9/11 Memorial & the Oculus @ 40.7115,-74.0134
You are now at the World Trade Center site.

The original World Trade Center was conceived in the late 1950s and 1960s, championed by David and Nelson Rockefeller and built by the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey. To make room, the city cleared an entire neighborhood of small streets, including Radio Row, a cluster of electronics shops where New Yorkers once bought radios, parts, and gadgets. The architect Minoru Yamasaki designed the two towers, which opened in the early 1970s and were, briefly, the tallest buildings in the world. The soil and rock excavated to build them were dumped into the Hudson River, creating the landfill on which Battery Park City now stands.

In August 1974, a French high-wire artist named Philippe Petit strung a cable between the two towers and walked across it, more than thirteen hundred feet above the street, for nearly an hour. He was arrested, and then celebrated.

On the morning of September eleventh, 2001, terrorists hijacked four airliners. Two were flown into the towers, which collapsed within two hours. In all, two thousand nine hundred seventy seven people were killed in the attacks in New York, at the Pentagon, and in Pennsylvania, the large majority of them here.

The memorial, called Reflecting Absence, was designed by Michael Arad with the landscape architect Peter Walker, and opened in 2011. The two pools sit in the footprints of the original towers. Water falls thirty feet down all four sides and then disappears into a smaller square void in the center whose bottom you cannot see. Around the edges, the names of the victims, including those killed in the 1993 bombing here, are cut into bronze panels. The names are not alphabetical. They are arranged according to what the designers called meaningful adjacencies: coworkers next to coworkers, friends next to friends, family members together, based on requests from the families. At night, light shines up through the cut letters. On victims' birthdays, staff place a single white rose in their names.

Look for the Survivor Tree, a Callery pear found in the rubble badly burned, with snapped roots and just one living branch. It was nursed back to health in a Bronx nursery and replanted here. It flowers every spring.

The tallest tower on the site is One World Trade Center, opened in 2014. Its height to the tip of its spire is one thousand seven hundred seventy six feet, a deliberate reference to the year of the Declaration of Independence.

Finally, the white, ribbed structure that looks like a bird with outstretched wings, or a skeleton, depending on whom you ask, is the Oculus, the transit hub designed by the Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava. It opened in 2016 and became one of the most expensive train stations ever built. Its central skylight is aligned so that each year on the morning of September eleventh, the sun shines down the length of the opening, at the time the second tower fell. Step inside if you can. The interior, all white ribs and open space, is one of the great rooms of modern New York.
=== STOP St. Paul's Chapel @ 40.7113,-74.0091
End your walk at St. Paul's Chapel, the stone church with the tall steeple facing Broadway. Completed in 1766, it is the oldest surviving church building in Manhattan, and one of the few buildings in the city that predates the American Revolution.

The chapel was built as an outpost of Trinity Church, for the growing population moving north of Wall Street. Its design was inspired by St. Martin-in-the-Fields in London. Notice that the church seems to face the wrong way: its grand portico, with the columns, faces Broadway, but the main entrance was originally on the other side, facing the Hudson River, which back then came up close to the churchyard. The steeple was added in the 1790s.

During the Great Fire of 1776, when much of the city burned during the British occupation, St. Paul's survived, reportedly thanks to a bucket brigade on its roof. On April thirtieth, 1789, after taking the oath of office at Federal Hall, George Washington walked here with members of Congress for a service of prayer. His pew is preserved inside, along with a painting of the Great Seal of the United States.

The chapel had a second historic moment in 2001. Even though it stood just across the street from the World Trade Center, it was not seriously damaged. Not even a window broke, according to the parish. A large sycamore tree in the churchyard is often credited with shielding it from debris. In the months after the attacks, St. Paul's became a relief center for rescue and recovery workers. Volunteers served meals, provided cots, offered massages and counseling, and kept the doors open around the clock. Firefighters and police slept in the pews. Their boots scuffed the pews, and some of those marks were deliberately preserved. Inside you will find a small exhibit about that period.

Behind the chapel, look for a bronze sculpture made from the root system of that sycamore tree, a reminder of how the old and the new are fused together in this neighborhood.

As you leave, consider the arc of this walk: from a Dutch trading post on the edge of the water, to the steamship lines and Standard Oil, to Morgan's bank and the Stock Exchange, to the towers that defined the modern skyline, and the memorial that stands in their place. Lower Manhattan has been destroyed and rebuilt many times, by fire, by speculation, and by attack. Each time, it has rebuilt itself in the same place, along the same crooked streets.
