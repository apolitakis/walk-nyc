=== OVERVIEW
Brooklyn Heights is often called America's first suburb. In the early nineteenth century, it was a bluff of farmland and estates above the East River, directly across from lower Manhattan. When steam ferry service began at Fulton Ferry in 1814, a merchant could live up here, in a quiet, leafy neighborhood with fresh air and a stunning view, and commute to his counting house on Wall Street in minutes. Real estate promoters advertised the neighborhood exactly that way, and the idea of commuting from a residential district to a business district was born.

The key figure was Hezekiah Pierrepont, a merchant who made his fortune in shipping and, notably, in gin. He owned the Anchor Gin distillery on the Brooklyn waterfront. Pierrepont bought up much of the land on the Heights, invested in the steam ferry, and divided his property into lots for townhouses. Other landowners followed. Several of the streets are named after the families who owned the land: Pierrepont, Remsen, Joralemon, Hicks, Middagh. Others are named for fruits: Orange, Pineapple, and Cranberry Streets. According to a long-told local story, an early resident, Lady Middagh, was annoyed by her neighbors naming streets after themselves, tore down their street signs, and replaced them with the names of fruits. The city eventually accepted her names.

Brooklyn Heights became one of the most beautiful residential neighborhoods in the country, with streets of Federal, Greek Revival, Gothic Revival, Italianate, and Romanesque townhouses. It was the home of prosperous merchants, ministers, and, later, writers and artists. In the twentieth century, as the wealthy moved elsewhere, many houses were split into rooming houses and apartments, and the neighborhood attracted writers drawn by cheap rents, beautiful streets, and views of Manhattan.

In the 1950s, the neighborhood fought to protect itself from Robert Moses's highway plans and from urban renewal, and in 1965, it became the first neighborhood protected as a historic district under New York City's new landmarks law. The south end of this walk takes you down to Atlantic Avenue, with its Middle Eastern shops and a remarkable secret beneath the street, and to Borough Hall, the old City Hall of the independent City of Brooklyn.
=== STOP The Brooklyn Heights Promenade @ 40.6968,-73.9966
You're on the Brooklyn Heights Promenade, a walkway running about a third of a mile along the edge of the bluff, from Remsen Street to Orange Street. The view across the East River to lower Manhattan, the Brooklyn Bridge, and the Statue of Liberty is one of the most famous in the world.

The Promenade exists because of a highway. In the 1940s, Robert Moses planned the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway, and his original route ran right through the middle of Brooklyn Heights, which would have destroyed blocks of historic houses. The residents fought back. The compromise was ingenious. Instead of cutting through the neighborhood, the highway was built along the edge of the bluff, in a stack of three cantilevered levels. Two levels of highway traffic, one in each direction, are stacked on top of each other, and on top of the highway is the Promenade, which opened in 1950. It's a rare example of a highway built to fit a neighborhood, rather than the other way around.

That structure, known as the triple cantilever, is now around seventy-five years old and has been deteriorating, and its future has been the subject of a long, contentious debate over how to repair or replace it.

During the Revolutionary War, this bluff was strategically important. In August 1776, as the British closed in after the Battle of Brooklyn, George Washington held a council of war in a house that stood near here, the Four Chimneys, the home of Philip Livingston, a signer of the Declaration of Independence. It was there that Washington decided to retreat across the East River to Manhattan, the escape you can hear about at Fulton Ferry Landing.

Look across the river at the skyline. From here, you can trace the history of lower Manhattan, from the old church steeples to the skyscrapers of Wall Street, to One World Trade Center. Many New Yorkers watched the Twin Towers burn from this Promenade on September eleventh, 2001. Some residents of the Heights were among those killed, and the Promenade became a gathering place for vigils.
=== STOP Columbia Heights & Willow Street: Writers' Row @ 40.6982,-73.9952
Walk north from the Promenade to Columbia Heights and Willow Street, where some of the most famous writers in American history have lived.

At 110 Columbia Heights, which no longer stands, Washington Roebling lived while he supervised the building of the Brooklyn Bridge from his window. In a remarkable coincidence, more than forty years later, the poet Hart Crane rented a room in the same building, and he discovered that it was the very room from which Roebling had watched the bridge. Crane, inspired, wrote his epic poem The Bridge, published in 1930, which uses the Brooklyn Bridge as a symbol of America.

Now walk to 70 Willow Street, a large yellow Greek Revival house built in the 1830s. In the 1950s and 60s, Truman Capote rented a basement apartment here, from a friend who owned the house. It was here that he wrote Breakfast at Tiffany's, published in 1958, and much of In Cold Blood, published in 1966. He described the neighborhood in an essay, A House on the Heights, which captures its atmosphere in the 1950s. The house sold in 2012 for a then-record price for a Brooklyn single-family home.

Other writers who lived in the Heights include Arthur Miller, who lived here in the years around Death of a Salesman; Norman Mailer, who wrote much of The Naked and the Dead while living in the Heights and later lived on Columbia Heights for decades; W. H. Auden and Carson McCullers, who shared a famous house on Middagh Street in the early 1940s with other artists, including the burlesque star Gypsy Rose Lee and the composer Benjamin Britten; and Marianne Moore, who lived in Fort Greene nearby. Thomas Wolfe lived here too.

Look around at the houses on Willow Street and the side streets. Notice the variety of styles, from early nineteenth century Federal houses, with their small dormers and simple doorways, to grand Italianate brownstones from the mid-1800s. At 155 to 159 Willow Street, look for three small Federal-style houses, among the oldest in the neighborhood. And on the corner of Willow and Middagh Streets, look for 24 Middagh Street, a charming wooden Federal house built in 1824, one of the oldest houses in the Heights.
=== STOP Plymouth Church @ 40.6995,-73.9933
Walk to Orange Street, between Hicks and Henry Streets, to Plymouth Church, a plain brick church with a courtyard and a bronze statue.

Plymouth Church was founded in 1847, and its first minister was Henry Ward Beecher, the most famous preacher in America in the mid-nineteenth century. Beecher came from a remarkable family. His father, Lyman Beecher, was a renowned preacher, and his sister was Harriet Beecher Stowe, the author of Uncle Tom's Cabin. Henry Ward Beecher was a passionate, theatrical speaker and an outspoken opponent of slavery. People came by ferry from Manhattan in such large crowds to hear him that the ferries were nicknamed Beecher Boats.

Beecher dramatized the evils of slavery by staging mock slave auctions in the church. Most famously, in 1860, he brought a young enslaved girl, nicknamed Pinky, before the congregation and asked them to contribute money to buy her freedom. The congregation filled the collection plates, and she was freed. The church is believed to have been a stop on the Underground Railroad, sheltering people escaping slavery in its basement, though documentation is limited.

Beecher also helped raise money to send rifles to anti-slavery settlers in Kansas, which were shipped in boxes labeled Bibles, and they were nicknamed Beecher's Bibles.

In February 1860, Abraham Lincoln attended a service here, just before his Cooper Union speech in Manhattan. The pew where he sat is marked.

Beecher's career was later tarnished by scandal. In 1874, his friend Theodore Tilton accused him of having an affair with Tilton's wife, Elizabeth. Tilton sued Beecher, and the trial in 1875 was a national sensation, covered by newspapers across the country. The jury couldn't reach a verdict, and Beecher remained in his pulpit, but his reputation never fully recovered.

In the courtyard, look for the bronze statue of Beecher by Gutzon Borglum, the sculptor who carved Mount Rushmore. It includes figures of two enslaved people at its base. On the wall nearby is a relief of Lincoln. Inside the church, there's a stained glass window commemorating Beecher, and a piece of Plymouth Rock in Massachusetts.
=== STOP Montague Street & Jackie Robinson @ 40.6944,-73.9915
Walk south to Montague Street, the main commercial street of Brooklyn Heights, lined with shops, restaurants, and grand bank buildings.

Look for the building at 215 Montague Street. In the 1940s, this was the headquarters of the Brooklyn Dodgers baseball club. On August twenty-eighth, 1945, the Dodgers' president, Branch Rickey, met here with a young Black baseball player named Jackie Robinson. For three hours, Rickey questioned him and warned him about the abuse he would face as the first Black player in the major leagues. Rickey famously told him he was looking for a player with the guts not to fight back. Robinson agreed. He signed with the Dodgers organization, and on April fifteenth, 1947, he took the field at Ebbets Field, breaking baseball's color barrier. A plaque on the building marks the meeting.

Montague Street was also the financial center of Brooklyn. Look for the grand bank buildings near the corner of Clinton Street, like the former Brooklyn Trust Company building at 177 Montague Street, designed by York and Sawyer, the architects of the Federal Reserve Bank in Manhattan, and modeled on a Renaissance palazzo. Today, it's a Chase branch.

At the corner of Pierrepont and Clinton Streets, look for the elaborate red brick and terracotta building with busts on the facade. This is the Center for Brooklyn History, originally built in 1881 for the Long Island Historical Society, designed by George B. Post, the architect of the New York Stock Exchange. The busts on the facade depict famous figures, including Christopher Columbus, Benjamin Franklin, Shakespeare, Beethoven, and Michelangelo, and there's a Viking and a Native American representing Long Island's history. Inside, the library is one of the most beautiful historic reading rooms in New York.

Nearby, on Montague Street, the church of St. Ann and the Holy Trinity has some of the earliest figural stained glass windows made in America, from the 1840s.

The Heights has had many prominent residents in business, too. Charles Pratt, of the Astral Oil Company, whose story you'll hear on the Williamsburg and Greenpoint tour, built mansions nearby, on Clinton Avenue in Clinton Hill. And the neighborhood's banks, insurance companies, and law firms made Montague Street the Wall Street of Brooklyn.
=== STOP Borough Hall @ 40.6929,-73.9902
Walk to Joralemon and Court Streets, to the white marble building with a columned portico and a cupola. This is Brooklyn Borough Hall, completed in 1848 as the City Hall of the City of Brooklyn.

Brooklyn was an independent city from 1834 to 1898. It grew rapidly, fed by ferries, the Navy Yard, the port, and industry, and by the 1890s it was the fourth largest city in the United States, with nearly a million people. Brooklyn had its own newspapers, its own museums, its own parks, and a strong civic identity.

In 1894, voters in New York, Brooklyn, and the surrounding towns voted on whether to consolidate into one city. In Brooklyn, the vote was extremely close: consolidation passed by only a few hundred votes, out of more than a hundred and twenty thousand. On January first, 1898, Greater New York was born, with five boroughs: Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens, and Staten Island. This building became Borough Hall. Many Brooklynites resented the merger, which they called the Great Mistake. Some still do.

The building's design is Greek Revival. The cupola was added later, after a fire, in 1898. Today, it's the office of the Brooklyn Borough President. Notice the statue of Justice that was planned for the top but only installed in the 1980s, over a century after it was first designed.

Look around the plaza. Columbus Park, in front of Borough Hall, has a statue of Henry Ward Beecher, whom you may have heard about at Plymouth Church. To the north, the massive building with columns is the Brooklyn Municipal Building, and the large building to the east is the New York State Supreme Court. Downtown Brooklyn has been transformed since a rezoning in 2004, with dozens of residential towers, including the first skyscraper in Brooklyn to exceed one thousand feet, completed in the early 2020s.

Court Street, running south from here, leads into the neighborhoods of Cobble Hill and Carroll Gardens. A farmers' market is held in front of Borough Hall several days a week.
=== STOP Atlantic Avenue & the Secret Tunnel @ 40.6905,-73.9934
End your walk on Atlantic Avenue, near Court Street.

Look along the street for the Middle Eastern shops, restaurants, and grocery stores. Starting in the late nineteenth century, immigrants from the Ottoman province of Syria, including what is now Lebanon, settled in lower Manhattan, in a neighborhood called Little Syria, on Washington Street. Most were Christians, and many were merchants, selling textiles and lace. When the Brooklyn-Battery Tunnel construction and other projects destroyed much of Little Syria in the 1940s, many of these businesses moved here, to Atlantic Avenue. The most famous is Sahadi's, a grocery store that opened in Manhattan in 1895 and moved here in 1948. It's still run by the Sahadi family, selling dried fruits, nuts, spices, olives, and other foods.

Now here's a secret: beneath your feet is the oldest subway tunnel in the world. In 1844, the Long Island Rail Road built a tunnel under Atlantic Avenue, about half a mile long, from near the waterfront to about Boerum Place. It was the first railroad tunnel under a city street in the world. It was used for less than twenty years, closed in 1861, and sealed off. Over time, it was forgotten. Legends grew up around it: that it held the body of a murdered man, or an old steam locomotive, or the lost pages of John Wilkes Booth's diary. In 1980, a young engineering student named Bob Diamond, who had heard the stories, tracked down the tunnel, found a manhole under the street, crawled through it, and broke through a wall to discover it. He ran tours of the tunnel for years, until the city closed it in 2010. The tunnel is still there, sealed under the pavement.

A few blocks east, at Boerum Place and Schermerhorn Street, is the New York Transit Museum, housed in a decommissioned subway station from 1936. On its platforms, you can walk through a collection of historic subway cars, from early wooden cars to sleek cars from the 1960s. It's a fun place to end this walk, and a reminder of how transportation, from ferries to bridges to railroads and subways, shaped every part of Brooklyn.
