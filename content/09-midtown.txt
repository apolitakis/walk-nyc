=== TOUR
id: midtown
title: Midtown: Empire State to Grand Central
area: Manhattan
blurb: The Empire State, Macy's, the Garment District, Bryant Park, Times Square, Grand Central, and the Chrysler Building.
=== OVERVIEW
Midtown Manhattan is the densest concentration of office space in the United States, and arguably the most recognizable urban landscape on Earth. Hundreds of thousands of people commute into these few square miles every workday. But for much of the nineteenth century, this area was on the outskirts of the city, a patchwork of farms, rocky outcrops, squatters' shanties, and institutions that wanted to be out of the way, like reservoirs, cattle yards, and rail yards.

The great transformation came at the turn of the twentieth century, driven by transportation. In 1871, Cornelius Vanderbilt opened the first Grand Central at 42nd Street, consolidating his railroads in one terminal. In 1904, the first subway line opened, running up the east side to Grand Central, across 42nd Street to Times Square, and up the west side. In 1910, the Pennsylvania Railroad opened Penn Station at 34th Street, connecting Manhattan by tunnel to New Jersey and Long Island. In 1913, the new Grand Central Terminal opened. Suddenly, Midtown was the most accessible place in the region.

Business followed. Department stores moved to Herald Square and Fifth Avenue. Theaters moved to Times Square. The garment industry moved to the West Thirties. Corporations built headquarters along 42nd Street and Park Avenue. In the late 1920s, during an extraordinary boom, Midtown became the site of a race for the sky, producing the Chrysler Building and the Empire State Building within a year of each other.

The look of Midtown was shaped by one law in particular. In 1916, after the massive Equitable Building downtown blocked light and air from its neighbors, New York passed the first comprehensive zoning law in the country. It required buildings to step back as they rose, so that light could reach the street. This produced the wedding-cake setbacks that define Art Deco skyscrapers, including the Empire State Building. As you walk, look up at the towers and you'll notice those setbacks everywhere.

On this walk, you'll see icons of American commerce, the department store, the corporate skyscraper, the advertising sign, and the train terminal, and you'll hear about the people who built them, the companies that paid for them, and the controversies that shaped them.
=== STOP The Empire State Building @ 40.7484,-73.9857
You're at Fifth Avenue and 34th Street, at the base of the Empire State Building.

Before the skyscraper, this site held the Waldorf-Astoria Hotel, born of a family feud. In the 1890s, William Waldorf Astor built the Waldorf Hotel here, next to the mansion of his aunt, Caroline Astor, the queen of New York society. It was partly an act of spite. Her son, John Jacob Astor the Fourth, responded by building an even taller hotel, the Astoria, next door. The two hotels were then joined into the Waldorf-Astoria, the most luxurious hotel in the city. When the hotel moved to Park Avenue in 1931, the old one was demolished. By the way, John Jacob Astor the Fourth died on the Titanic in 1912. He was among the wealthiest people aboard.

The Empire State Building was the project of John J. Raskob, a financier and former executive of General Motors and DuPont, and Al Smith, the former governor of New York who had just lost the 1928 presidential election. Raskob was reportedly in a competition with Walter Chrysler, of the rival automaker, who was building the Chrysler Building a few blocks away.

The design, by the firm Shreve, Lamb and Harmon, was simple and efficient, and construction was astonishingly fast. Steel went up at a rate of about four and a half stories per week. The building opened on May first, 1931, after about thirteen and a half months of construction. President Herbert Hoover pressed a button in Washington to turn on the lights.

At one thousand two hundred fifty feet to its roof, it was the tallest building in the world, a title it held for nearly forty years. But it opened at the worst possible moment, in the depths of the Great Depression. For years, much of its office space was empty, and New Yorkers nicknamed it the Empty State Building. The observation deck's ticket sales helped keep it afloat.

The spire on top was originally meant to be a mooring mast for dirigibles, the airships. The idea was that passengers would disembark onto a gangplank eighty-six floors up. A couple of attempts were made, but high winds made it impractical and dangerous, and the idea was abandoned.

In 1933, the building became a movie star, when King Kong climbed to its top. On July twenty-eighth, 1945, an Army B-25 bomber, flying in heavy fog, crashed into the 79th floor, killing fourteen people. An elevator operator, Betty Lou Oliver, survived a fall of about seventy-five stories in an elevator whose cables had been damaged, which remains one of the most famous survival stories in the building's history.

The Art Deco lobby is one of the city's finest, with a ceiling mural of gears and planets in gold leaf and an image of the building itself in aluminum relief.
=== STOP Herald Square & Macy's @ 40.7506,-73.9878
You're at Herald Square, where Broadway crosses Sixth Avenue at 34th Street. The square takes its name from the New York Herald, a newspaper whose headquarters, designed by Stanford White, once stood here. Look for the bronze monument at the north end of the square, with a figure of Minerva and two bell ringers with hammers who strike a bell every hour. The ringers are nicknamed Stuff and Guff. The monument honors James Gordon Bennett Senior and Junior, the publishers of the Herald. It was the younger Bennett who sent the reporter Henry Morton Stanley to Africa to find the missionary David Livingstone, producing the famous line, Doctor Livingstone, I presume?

The giant store on the south side of 34th Street is Macy's. Rowland Hussey Macy, a former whaler from Nantucket, opened a dry goods store on Sixth Avenue at 14th Street in 1858, after several failed retail ventures. The company's red star logo is said to come from a tattoo Macy got while at sea. Macy's was innovative: it used fixed prices, sold goods for cash only, and advertised aggressively.

Later, Macy's was taken over by the Straus family, Isidor and Nathan Straus, who had run its china and glassware department. Isidor Straus and his wife Ida died together on the Titanic in 1912, after Ida reportedly refused to get into a lifeboat without him. Under the Strauses, Macy's moved here in 1902 and built what it advertised as the World's Largest Store.

Now look at the corner of Broadway and 34th Street. You'll notice a small, narrow building, covered in giant advertising signs, wedged into the corner of the Macy's block. Macy's doesn't own it. When Macy's was assembling the land for its store, an agent working for a rival, Henry Siegel of the Siegel-Cooper store, bought this corner lot first, hoping to force Macy's to buy it at an outrageous price, or to block its access to the corner. Macy's refused, and built around it. The holdout building still stands, and Macy's has rented its exterior for its own advertising.

Macy's had a fierce rivalry with Gimbels, a department store that stood a block away. The rivalry was so famous that it appears in the 1947 film Miracle on 34th Street, in which the Macy's Santa Claus sends customers to Gimbels if Macy's doesn't have what they want. Gimbels closed in 1987.

Macy's also gave the city one of its biggest traditions: the Thanksgiving Day Parade, first held in 1924. Its famous giant balloons were introduced in 1927. The parade ends right here, in front of the store.
=== STOP The Garment District @ 40.7545,-73.9881
You're on Seventh Avenue, around 39th Street. Look at the street signs: this stretch of Seventh Avenue is also called Fashion Avenue. Look down: in the sidewalk, you may find bronze plaques of the Fashion Walk of Fame, honoring American designers. And look at the corner of Seventh Avenue and 39th Street, where a giant sculpture of a needle threading a button stands, along with an information kiosk.

You're in the Garment District, roughly between Fifth and Ninth Avenues, from about 34th to 42nd Streets. For much of the twentieth century, this was the center of the American clothing industry. In the early 1900s, the industry was concentrated in the tenement sweatshops of the Lower East Side. After the Triangle fire and new safety laws, and as the industry grew, manufacturers moved into new loft buildings here, built in the 1920s. Those tall, setback buildings were designed for garment production, with big windows for light and large open floors for rows of sewing machines.

By the 1950s, this district was producing a large majority of the women's clothing made in the United States, and the industry employed hundreds of thousands of workers in New York. The streets were jammed with trucks and with workers pushing rolling racks of clothing from factories to showrooms. Many of the workers were Jewish and Italian immigrants and their children, and later Puerto Rican, Chinese, Dominican, and other immigrants. The industry was heavily unionized, and the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union was one of the most powerful unions in the city.

Look for the statue of a garment worker at a sewing machine, a bronze sculpture by Judith Weller, at 555 Seventh Avenue. It honors the many immigrant workers, especially Jewish workers, who built the industry.

Beginning in the 1960s and accelerating in the 1980s and 90s, most garment manufacturing moved overseas, to Asia and Latin America, where labor was cheaper. The number of garment jobs in New York collapsed. The district's buildings were gradually converted to offices, showrooms, hotels, and tech companies.

Still, New York remains a fashion capital, the home of major designers, fashion magazines, and New York Fashion Week. Nearby schools, including the Fashion Institute of Technology on 27th Street and Parsons School of Design, train designers from around the world. And you'll still find a few fabric stores, button shops, and trimming suppliers on the side streets, especially around 38th and 39th Streets, where designers come to buy materials.
=== STOP Bryant Park & the New York Public Library @ 40.7532,-73.9823
You're in Bryant Park, behind the New York Public Library, between Fifth and Sixth Avenues and 40th and 42nd Streets.

In the nineteenth century, the site of the library held the Croton Distributing Reservoir, completed in 1842. It was part of the Croton Aqueduct system, which brought clean water to the city from the Croton River, forty miles north, transforming public health in New York. The reservoir was surrounded by massive Egyptian-style stone walls, and New Yorkers strolled along a promenade on top of them, looking down at the water.

Next to the reservoir, on the site of today's park, New York hosted its first world's fair in 1853, housed in a giant iron-and-glass building called the Crystal Palace, inspired by the one in London. It was here, in 1854, that Elisha Otis demonstrated his safety elevator by cutting the rope. The Crystal Palace burned down in 1858.

The library, built over the old reservoir, opened in 1911. It was designed by Carrère and Hastings, and it's a masterpiece of Beaux-Arts architecture. The library was created by combining two private libraries, those of John Jacob Astor and James Lenox, with a large bequest from Samuel Tilden, the former governor you heard about at Gramercy Park. In front, on Fifth Avenue, are the famous marble lions. In the 1930s, Mayor Fiorello La Guardia named them Patience and Fortitude, the qualities he said New Yorkers would need to survive the Depression.

If you can, go inside and see the Rose Main Reading Room on the third floor, nearly two blocks long, with a painted ceiling of clouds.

As for the park, it's named for William Cullen Bryant, a poet and longtime editor of the New York Evening Post, who campaigned for the creation of Central Park. By the 1970s, Bryant Park was a notorious haven for drug dealers and was considered dangerous. In the 1980s, a private nonprofit, the Bryant Park Restoration Corporation, led by Daniel Biederman, took over its management. It was redesigned with open sightlines, movable chairs, and food kiosks, and reopened in 1992. Its turnaround became a model for business improvement districts and public-private park management around the world. Today, it hosts an ice rink in winter, outdoor movies in summer, and some of the best people watching in the city.

Before you leave, look at the black and gold tower on 40th Street, facing the park. That's the American Radiator Building, completed in 1924 and designed by Raymond Hood. It was built for the American Radiator Company, which made heating equipment, and the black brick and gold top were meant to evoke glowing coals. Georgia O'Keeffe painted it at night. It's now a hotel.
=== STOP Times Square @ 40.7580,-73.9855
You're in Times Square, at the junction of Broadway and Seventh Avenue, between 42nd and 47th Streets.

Until 1904, this was Longacre Square, a district of carriage makers and horse stables. Then the New York Times moved into a new tower at 42nd Street, the narrow building at the south end of the square, now called One Times Square. The Times's publisher, Adolph Ochs, persuaded the city to rename the square after his newspaper. The same year, the first subway line opened, with a station right here.

To celebrate the new building, the Times hosted a New Year's Eve celebration with fireworks on December thirty-first, 1904. A few years later, when fireworks were banned, the paper lowered an illuminated ball from the building's flagpole at midnight. The first ball drop was on New Year's Eve, 1907, and it's happened almost every year since. The Times itself moved out of the tower in 1913, but the name stuck. In 1928, the building installed a news ticker that ran headlines around the building in lights, the famous zipper.

By the 1920s, the area was the center of the American theater, with dozens of Broadway theaters on the side streets. Electric advertising signs turned Broadway into the Great White Way. Advertisers competed to create the most spectacular displays. Look up at the giant screens around you. Zoning rules actually require buildings in Times Square to have large, illuminated signs. It's one of the only places in the world where flashy advertising is legally mandatory.

After World War Two, Times Square declined. By the 1970s, 42nd Street was lined with pornographic theaters, peep shows, and drug dealers, and it became a national symbol of urban decay. In the 1990s, the city and state launched an aggressive redevelopment. A key moment came in 1997, when the Walt Disney Company restored the historic New Amsterdam Theatre on 42nd Street, signaling that big corporations were willing to invest here. Within a few years, the area was transformed into a family-friendly district of chain stores, restaurants, and tourist attractions. Some New Yorkers mourn the old grit; most are glad to be rid of it.

In 2009, under Mayor Michael Bloomberg and his transportation commissioner, Janette Sadik-Khan, the city closed parts of Broadway to cars and created pedestrian plazas. The experiment was so popular it was made permanent.

At the north end of the square, look for the red glass steps, which sit atop the TKTS booth, where you can buy discounted same-day theater tickets, and the statue of Father Francis Duffy, the chaplain of the Fighting 69th regiment in World War One. Behind him is a statue of George M. Cohan, the composer of Give My Regards to Broadway.
=== STOP Shubert Alley & the Theater District @ 40.7584,-73.9872
Walk west to Shubert Alley, a private pedestrian alley running between 44th and 45th Streets, just west of Broadway.

The alley is named for the Shubert brothers, Lee, Sam, and Jacob, three immigrants from Lithuania who grew up poor in Syracuse, New York, and built the largest theater empire in America. At their peak, the Shuberts owned, operated, or booked a huge number of theaters across the country. The Shubert Organization, headquartered in the Shubert Theatre building next to the alley, still owns more Broadway theaters than anyone else.

Shubert Alley was created as a fire exit for the theaters on either side. It became a gathering place for actors, who would wait here hoping to be noticed by producers, since casting offices were nearby. Today, the walls are covered with posters for current shows.

The theaters around you, on 44th, 45th, and nearby streets, are the heart of Broadway. Broadway is technically not the street, but a designation for about forty professional theaters, each with at least five hundred seats, mostly located between 41st and 54th Streets. Many date from the 1910s and 1920s, the golden age of theater construction. The Shubert Theatre, next to the alley, opened in 1913, and was home to A Chorus Line for its fifteen-year run. The Booth Theatre, on the other side, is named after Edwin Booth, the actor whose club you may have visited at Gramercy Park.

On West 44th Street, look for Sardi's, the restaurant famous for the caricatures of Broadway stars covering its walls. It's been a gathering place for theater people since the 1920s, and for decades, opening night casts would gather there to wait for the reviews in the morning papers.

Broadway is a big business. In a typical recent season, it has drawn more than twelve million attendees and grossed over a billion and a half dollars, with tourists making up the majority of the audience. Shows are high-risk investments: most lose money, but a hit, like The Phantom of the Opera, which ran for thirty-five years at the Majestic Theatre on 44th Street, or The Lion King, can earn billions over its life.

If you have time, the stretch of West 44th and 45th Streets between Broadway and Eighth Avenue is the best place to see a cluster of historic theater facades, marquees, and stage doors, where crowds gather after shows to get autographs.
=== STOP Grand Central Terminal @ 40.7527,-73.9772
You're at Grand Central Terminal, at 42nd Street and Park Avenue. Go inside, if you can, and stand in the Main Concourse.

The terminal was built by the New York Central Railroad, the Vanderbilt family's railroad empire. The first Grand Central opened here in 1871. In those days, steam locomotives ran down Park Avenue, through an open trench and a smoky tunnel. In January 1902, two trains collided in the smoke-filled tunnel, killing seventeen people. Public outrage led to a law banning steam locomotives in Manhattan.

The railroad's chief engineer, William Wilgus, came up with a brilliant plan. He proposed electrifying the trains, and since electric trains don't produce smoke, the tracks could be put underground and covered over. Then the railroad could sell the air rights above the tracks to developers. The new terminal, with two levels of underground tracks, opened in 1913. It was designed by the architects Reed and Stem and Warren and Wetmore. The covered tracks became Park Avenue, and the blocks above them were developed into hotels, apartments, and office buildings, a district called Terminal City. The sale of air rights helped pay for the whole project. It was one of the great real estate deals in history.

Look up at the ceiling of the Main Concourse: a turquoise sky with gold constellations of the zodiac. The constellations are painted backward, as if you're looking down at the sky from outside. Some say it was a mistake; others say it's a view from the heavens. Look also for a small dark patch near one corner. During restoration in the 1990s, workers left one area uncleaned, to show how dark the ceiling had become, mostly from decades of tobacco smoke.

At the center of the concourse is the four-faced clock on top of the information booth. Its faces are made of opal, and it's often said to be worth millions of dollars.

Grand Central's survival is a landmark story. In 1963, the Pennsylvania Railroad demolished the original Penn Station, a magnificent Beaux-Arts building, to build Madison Square Garden. The loss shocked the city and led to the creation of the New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission in 1965. In the late 1960s, Grand Central's owners planned to build an office tower on top of the terminal. The city refused, and with support from Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis and other prominent New Yorkers, the case went to the Supreme Court. In 1978, in Penn Central Transportation Company versus New York City, the court upheld the city's right to protect landmarks, a decision that has shaped historic preservation across the country.

Downstairs, near the Oyster Bar, look for the Whispering Gallery, a low tiled archway where you can whisper into one corner and someone standing diagonally opposite can hear you clearly.
=== STOP The Chrysler Building @ 40.7516,-73.9754
End your walk at Lexington Avenue and 42nd Street, looking up at the Chrysler Building, which many people consider the most beautiful skyscraper in the world.

It was built for Walter Chrysler, a former railroad mechanic who had founded the Chrysler Corporation in 1925, which quickly became one of the big three American automakers. Chrysler paid for the building himself, rather than with company money, so that his children would inherit it. The architect was William Van Alen.

The building was at the center of the great race for the sky in 1929 and 1930. Van Alen's former partner, H. Craig Severance, was designing 40 Wall Street, and the two buildings were competing to be the tallest in the world. When 40 Wall Street seemed to have won, Van Alen revealed his secret. A steel spire, called the vertex, had been assembled in pieces inside the building's dome. In October 1929, it was raised through the top and bolted into place in about ninety minutes. The Chrysler Building rose to one thousand forty-six feet, the tallest in the world, and the first structure to exceed one thousand feet. It held the title for less than a year, until the Empire State Building opened.

Look at the details. The stainless steel crown has rows of triangular windows in a sunburst pattern. Near the top, at the setbacks, are gleaming steel gargoyles shaped like eagle heads, and lower down, ornaments modeled on Chrysler radiator caps and hubcaps, along with a frieze of stylized car wheels. It's a temple to the automobile.

If it's open, step into the lobby, which is clad in red Moroccan marble, with a ceiling mural by Edward Trumbull showing transportation and human endeavor, and elevator doors with elaborate wood inlays.

Before you go, look east along 42nd Street. At 220 East 42nd Street is the Daily News Building, designed by Raymond Hood and completed in 1930, home of the tabloid New York Daily News, once the highest-circulation newspaper in America. Its lobby has a giant rotating globe. In the 1978 film Superman, the building appeared as the Daily Planet.

At the end of 42nd Street, along the East River, is the United Nations headquarters, built on land that once held slaughterhouses. John D. Rockefeller Junior bought the land and donated it to the United Nations in 1946. The Secretariat Building, completed in 1952, was among the first glass curtain wall skyscrapers in New York.

From railroads and department stores, to newspapers, cars, and the world's diplomats, the businesses that built Midtown are all written into its skyline.
