title: Washington Heights & Inwood
area: Manhattan
blurb: Washington's headquarters, the Audubon Ballroom, the High Bridge, the George Washington Bridge, the Cloisters, and Manhattan's last forest.
=== OVERVIEW
North of Harlem, Manhattan narrows and rises. The island's spine becomes a ridge of rock, with cliffs dropping to the Hudson River on the west and the Harlem River on the east. This is Washington Heights, and at the very top of the island, Inwood. Many New Yorkers who live downtown have never been up here, which is a shame, because this part of Manhattan has some of the city's most dramatic landscapes and some of its deepest history.

The neighborhood's name comes from Fort Washington, a Revolutionary War fortification built on the highest ground in Manhattan in 1776. That November, the fort fell to the British in one of the worst American defeats of the war, and almost three thousand American soldiers were captured. Many died in the horrific conditions of British prison ships in Wallabout Bay, in Brooklyn. For the rest of the war, the British held all of Manhattan.

For most of the nineteenth century, the area remained rural, with farms and grand country estates. The naturalist John James Audubon had his estate here. Development came with the subway in the early 1900s, and apartment buildings filled the ridge. The neighborhood became home to successive waves of immigrants: Irish, then Greek, and in the 1930s and 40s, so many German Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi persecution that the area was nicknamed Frankfurt on the Hudson. The future Secretary of State Henry Kissinger grew up here, after his family fled Germany in 1938.

From the 1960s onward, Washington Heights became the center of the Dominican community in the United States. Today, it's often called Little Dominican Republic, and you'll hear Spanish and bachata everywhere, especially along St. Nicholas Avenue and 181st Street. Lin-Manuel Miranda, who grew up in nearby Inwood, set his musical In the Heights here, and he wrote parts of Hamilton in a historic house you'll visit on this walk.

This tour covers a long stretch, from 160th Street to the northern tip of Manhattan at about 218th Street. It's best done in pieces, using the A train or the 1 train, which run up the length of the neighborhood.
=== STOP Morris-Jumel Mansion @ 40.8345,-73.9387
You're at the Morris-Jumel Mansion, at West 160th Street and Edgecombe Avenue, the oldest surviving house in Manhattan.

It was built in 1765 as a summer villa for Roger Morris, a British army officer, and his wife Mary Philipse, from one of the wealthiest families in the colony. It's in the Palladian style, with a grand columned portico, and it sat on a hilltop with views of both the Hudson and Harlem Rivers and all the way down to the harbor. When the Revolution came, the Morrises, who were loyalists, fled.

In September and October 1776, George Washington made the house his headquarters, during the weeks around the Battle of Harlem Heights. From here, he could watch British movements. After Washington left, the British and their Hessian allies used it as a headquarters too. After the war, in 1790, Washington returned for a dinner here with members of his cabinet, including John Adams, Thomas Jefferson, and Alexander Hamilton.

In 1810, the house was bought by Stephen Jumel, a wealthy French-born wine merchant, and his wife Eliza. Eliza Jumel was born into poverty in Rhode Island and rose to become one of the richest women in America. After Stephen died in 1832, Eliza married Aaron Burr, the former vice president who had killed Alexander Hamilton, in this house in 1833. She was in her late fifties; he was seventy-seven. The marriage quickly fell apart when Burr started speculating with her money. She filed for divorce, and the divorce was finalized on the very day Burr died, in 1836. Her divorce lawyer was Alexander Hamilton Junior, the son of the man Burr had killed.

Lin-Manuel Miranda was given permission to write here while working on Hamilton, and he reportedly wrote parts of the musical in Aaron Burr's bedroom.

As you leave, look across the street at Sylvan Terrace, a short cobblestone lane lined with two rows of small yellow wooden houses, built in 1882 as housing for working people. It looks like a village street from another century, because it is.
=== STOP The Audubon Ballroom @ 40.8372,-73.9399
Walk to Broadway and West 165th Street, where you'll see a building with an ornate terracotta facade, with figures of Neptune and sea creatures, attached to a modern glass research building. This is the former Audubon Ballroom.

It was built in 1912 by William Fox, a Hungarian Jewish immigrant who had started out in the garment business and moved into the new business of movies. Fox built a chain of theaters, and later founded the Fox Film Corporation, which merged in 1935 to become Twentieth Century-Fox, one of the great Hollywood studios. The Audubon was a theater and ballroom, used for vaudeville, movies, dances, and meetings. Labor unions, political groups, and community organizations rented its ballroom.

On February twenty-first, 1965, Malcolm X was speaking to a meeting of his Organization of Afro-American Unity in the ballroom when he was shot and killed by several gunmen in front of his wife, Betty Shabazz, and their children. Three members of the Nation of Islam were convicted of the murder. In 2021, after a reinvestigation, two of the three men's convictions were vacated, after it emerged that authorities had withheld evidence. The case remains a subject of investigation and debate.

In the 1980s and 90s, Columbia University, which owned the building, planned to demolish it for a biomedical research center. Preservationists and activists protested, and in a compromise, part of the facade and ballroom were preserved and incorporated into the new research building. Today, the building houses the Malcolm X and Dr. Betty Shabazz Memorial and Educational Center, with a mural depicting Malcolm X's life.

The research building is part of the vast Columbia University Irving Medical Center and NewYork-Presbyterian Hospital complex, which opened here in 1928, as the first academic medical center in the world, combining a hospital, medical school, and research in one place.

A few blocks south, at 155th Street and Broadway, is the Trinity Church Cemetery and Mausoleum, the only active cemetery in Manhattan. John James Audubon, the naturalist and painter of The Birds of America, is buried there. He owned an estate, called Minnie's Land, along the Hudson in this neighborhood, which is why the Audubon name appears all over the area.
=== STOP The High Bridge @ 40.8423,-73.9301
Walk east to Highbridge Park, near West 173rd Street and Amsterdam Avenue, at the edge of the cliff overlooking the Harlem River. Here you'll find the High Bridge, the oldest standing bridge in New York City.

The High Bridge was completed in 1848 as part of the Croton Aqueduct, the great water system you may have heard about at Bryant Park and Bethesda Fountain. Before the aqueduct, New Yorkers relied on wells and polluted cisterns, and epidemics of cholera and other diseases ravaged the city, while fires burned unchecked because there wasn't enough water. The Croton Aqueduct brought clean water from the Croton River in Westchester County, forty-one miles to the north, flowing entirely by gravity. The aqueduct had to cross the Harlem River, and engineers built this bridge, modeled on the arched aqueducts of ancient Rome, to carry the water pipes over it.

When it opened, the bridge was a sensation. A promenade on top became a favorite destination for New Yorkers on Sunday outings. It was the Victorian equivalent of the High Line.

In the 1920s, several of the original stone arches over the river were replaced with a single steel arch, to allow bigger ships to pass underneath. The bridge stopped carrying water in the mid-twentieth century, and it was closed to the public in the 1970s. After a long restoration, it reopened to pedestrians and cyclists in 2015, connecting Manhattan to the Bronx.

Next to the bridge on the Manhattan side stands the High Bridge Water Tower, an octagonal granite tower completed in 1872. It held water in a tank at the top to provide pressure to supply the growing neighborhoods on the high ground of northern Manhattan. Its design, with its bell tower, makes it look more like a church tower than a piece of infrastructure.

The Croton system set New York on the path to having one of the best municipal water supplies in the world. Today, the city's water comes mainly from reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains and the Delaware River watershed, upstate, and it's so clean that it's one of the few big city water systems in the United States that doesn't need to be filtered.
=== STOP The George Washington Bridge & the Little Red Lighthouse @ 40.8501,-73.9469
Walk west to Fort Washington Park, along the Hudson, beneath the George Washington Bridge, near West 178th Street. Here you'll find a small red lighthouse right at the foot of the bridge.

Look up at the bridge. The George Washington Bridge opened in 1931, connecting Manhattan with Fort Lee, New Jersey. Its chief engineer was Othmar Ammann, a Swiss immigrant, who later designed the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge. When it opened, its main span, about thirty-five hundred feet, was nearly double the length of the previous record holder, an astonishing leap. The towers were originally meant to be covered in concrete and granite, but the Depression put a stop to that, and they were left as bare steel latticework. The Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier called it the most beautiful bridge in the world, praising the honesty of its exposed steel. A second, lower deck was added in 1962. Today, it's one of the busiest motor vehicle bridges in the world, carrying hundreds of thousands of vehicles a day.

Now look at the lighthouse. It's officially called the Jeffrey's Hook Lighthouse. It was built in 1880 at Sandy Hook, New Jersey, and moved here in 1921 to warn ships away from the rocky point. When the bridge was built, its bright lights made the lighthouse obsolete, and it was decommissioned in 1948. The Coast Guard planned to auction it off.

But the lighthouse had become famous. In 1942, a children's book called The Little Red Lighthouse and the Great Gray Bridge, by Hildegarde Swift and Lynd Ward, told the story of a little lighthouse that felt useless next to the giant bridge, until one foggy night, it saved a ship. Children who loved the book wrote letters and protested to save it, and in 1951, the Coast Guard gave the lighthouse to the city. It was restored and is occasionally open for tours.

High on the ridge above you, near 183rd Street and Fort Washington Avenue, is Bennett Park, the highest natural point in Manhattan, at about two hundred sixty-five feet above sea level. It's also where Fort Washington stood. You can still see the outline of the fort marked in the park. On November sixteenth, 1776, about eight thousand British and Hessian troops attacked the fort from several directions. It fell within hours. It was a devastating blow to the American cause, and it forced Washington into a long retreat across New Jersey.
=== STOP Fort Tryon Park & the Cloisters @ 40.8649,-73.9318
Take the A train to 190th Street, or walk north, to Fort Tryon Park, one of the most beautiful parks in New York, perched on cliffs high above the Hudson. In the park is The Met Cloisters, a branch of the Metropolitan Museum of Art devoted to the art and architecture of medieval Europe.

The park and the museum were gifts of John D. Rockefeller Junior. In the 1910s and 20s, he bought up the land here, including the former estate of the industrialist C. K. G. Billings. He hired the Olmsted Brothers firm, run by the sons of Frederick Law Olmsted, to design a park, and gave it to the city, which opened it in 1935. Rockefeller also bought land across the Hudson, along the Palisades in New Jersey, and donated it to the public, to ensure that the view from the park would never be spoiled by development. It still hasn't been.

The museum began with the collection of an American sculptor named George Grey Barnard, who had gathered medieval sculptures and architectural fragments from ruined abbeys and churches in France. Rockefeller bought the collection in 1925 for the Met and funded a new building to house it. The Cloisters, which opened in 1938, was designed by the architect Charles Collens. It incorporates parts of five medieval cloisters, the covered walkways around a monastery's garden, from France and Spain, along with chapels, doorways, and windows, reassembled into a building that evokes a medieval monastery.

Inside, you'll find some of the greatest treasures of medieval art, including the Unicorn Tapestries, seven tapestries woven in the Netherlands around 1500, depicting the hunt for a unicorn. The gardens, planted with species that were grown in the Middle Ages, are especially beautiful in spring and summer.

The park takes its name from Fort Tryon, an American outpost that was captured by Hessian troops during the Battle of Fort Washington in 1776, and renamed by the British for William Tryon, the last British governor of New York. Near the park's entrance, look for Margaret Corbin Circle. Margaret Corbin fought here in that battle. Her husband was a gunner, and when he was killed, she took his place at the cannon and kept firing until she was seriously wounded. She later became the first woman in the United States to receive a military pension.
=== STOP Dyckman Farmhouse @ 40.8673,-73.9226
Walk to Broadway and West 204th Street, where you'll find a small white clapboard and fieldstone farmhouse with a gambrel roof and a front porch, sitting on a little hill above the street. This is the Dyckman Farmhouse Museum, the last surviving Dutch colonial farmhouse in Manhattan.

The Dyckman family were among the early Dutch settlers of upper Manhattan. Jan Dyckman arrived in the 1660s, and the family farmed land here for generations, eventually owning hundreds of acres. During the Revolutionary War, British and Hessian troops occupied the area and burned the original Dyckman house. After the war, around 1784, William Dyckman built this farmhouse, in the Dutch colonial style, with fieldstone and brick walls, a steep roof, and a porch called a stoep, from which the New York word stoop comes.

The family lived here and farmed until the mid-nineteenth century. By the early twentieth century, the house was in poor condition, surrounded by new apartment buildings. In 1915, two Dyckman descendants, Mary Alice Dyckman Dean and Fannie Fredericka Dyckman Welch, bought the house, restored it, filled it with family furniture and heirlooms, and donated it to the city as a museum in 1916.

Look around the garden behind the house. There's a reconstruction of a hut used by Hessian soldiers during the British occupation, and a smokehouse. In this neighborhood of busy streets and apartment buildings, the farmhouse is a startling reminder that all of Manhattan was once countryside.

Inwood, the neighborhood around you, is one of the most residential and affordable parts of Manhattan, with a large Dominican population, and a mix of Irish and Jewish families who have been here for generations. The area along Dyckman Street, with its restaurants, bars, and music, comes alive on summer nights.
=== STOP Inwood Hill Park @ 40.8722,-73.9264
End your walk in Inwood Hill Park, at the northern tip of Manhattan. Walk in from the entrance at Seaman Avenue and 207th Street, past the ballfields, to the salt marsh.

Inwood Hill Park contains the last natural forest and the last natural salt marsh in Manhattan. Unlike Central Park, which was entirely designed and rebuilt, much of the woods here were never cleared for farming or development. Some of the trees are very old. Walking the trails up the hill, among large tulip trees, oaks, and rock outcrops, you can get a sense of what the island looked like before Europeans arrived.

The Lenape lived and fished here for thousands of years. Archaeologists have found evidence of their camps, and on the hillside, you'll find rock shelters, natural caves formed by fallen boulders, which were used by the Lenape.

Near the salt marsh, look for a boulder with a plaque. This is Shorakkopoch Rock. It marks the spot where a giant tulip tree stood until 1938, when it died. Local tradition holds that this was where, in 1626, Peter Minuit of the Dutch West India Company bought Manhattan from the Lenape for trade goods worth sixty guilders, an amount that later writers calculated as twenty-four dollars. Historians doubt the story's details, including the location. And the Lenape likely understood the transaction very differently from the Dutch, as an agreement to share the land rather than a permanent sale. But it's the closest thing to a founding site Manhattan has.

Walk to the water's edge. The water to the north is Spuyten Duyvil Creek, which separates Manhattan from the Bronx. The name is Dutch, and its meaning is debated: it may mean in spite of the devil or spouting devil, referring to its strong currents. Look across the creek at the cliff on the Bronx side. You'll see a giant blue C painted on the rock. It was first painted in the 1950s by members of the Columbia University rowing team, who train on the river nearby, and it's been repainted many times since. Columbia's athletic facilities, including its football stadium, are just to the east.

Spanning the creek is the Henry Hudson Bridge, a steel arch bridge built by Robert Moses in 1936. According to tradition, somewhere near here in 1609, the explorer Henry Hudson, sailing for the Dutch East India Company, anchored his ship, the Half Moon, on his voyage up the river that now bears his name, looking for a passage to Asia.

You're standing about as far as you can get from Wall Street and still be in Manhattan, in the place where the island's story began.
