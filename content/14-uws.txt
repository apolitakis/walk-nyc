title: Upper West Side
area: Manhattan
blurb: Columbus Circle, the lost neighborhood of San Juan Hill, the Dakota, the Ansonia, the Natural History Museum, Zabar's, and Riverside Park.
=== OVERVIEW
The Upper West Side developed later than the Upper East Side, and it has always had a different personality. If the East Side was old money and Society with a capital S, the West Side was intellectual, artistic, liberal, and a little bohemian. It's the neighborhood of musicians, writers, academics, psychoanalysts, and serious eaters, and the setting for countless novels and films, from West Side Story to When Harry Met Sally to You've Got Mail.

In the mid-nineteenth century, this area was mostly countryside, called Bloomingdale, from the Dutch Bloemendael, or valley of flowers. The old Bloomingdale Road, which followed a Native American trail, ran through it; it later became upper Broadway. There were country estates along the Hudson, farms, villages, and shantytowns, as well as institutions like the Bloomingdale Insane Asylum, which stood on the site of today's Columbia University.

Development was slow until transportation arrived. The Ninth Avenue elevated train reached the area in 1879. Developers began building rowhouses on the side streets, and a few ambitious apartment buildings, like the Dakota. But the real boom came with the first subway, the IRT, which opened in 1904 and ran up Broadway. Almost overnight, the West Side became accessible to the middle class, and giant apartment buildings rose along Broadway, Central Park West, West End Avenue, and Riverside Drive. Many of them were built for a new population: upwardly mobile, often Jewish families moving up from the Lower East Side and other parts of the city.

The neighborhood went through hard times in the mid-twentieth century. Many large apartments were cut up into single-room-occupancy hotels, and parts of the neighborhood were considered dangerous by the 1970s. Urban renewal projects demolished whole neighborhoods, most notably San Juan Hill, for Lincoln Center. Then, starting in the 1980s, the West Side gentrified dramatically. Today, it's one of the most expensive neighborhoods in the country, though it retains its reputation as a place of books, music, and lively street life.

On this walk, you'll explore some of the most famous apartment buildings in the world, great cultural institutions, and a few beloved food shops, and you'll end on the banks of the Hudson in Riverside Park.
=== STOP Columbus Circle @ 40.7681,-73.9819
You're at Columbus Circle, at the southwest corner of Central Park, where Broadway, Eighth Avenue, Central Park West, and 59th Street converge.

In the center of the circle, atop a tall granite column, stands a marble statue of Christopher Columbus by the Italian sculptor Gaetano Russo. It was erected in 1892, for the four hundredth anniversary of Columbus's first voyage, and was paid for by Italian Americans through a fundraising campaign led by the Italian-language newspaper Il Progresso. At the time, Italian immigrants faced intense discrimination, and Columbus was embraced as a symbol of Italian contributions to America. In recent years, the statue has been the subject of debate over how Columbus should be remembered, given his treatment of Indigenous people. A fun fact: Columbus Circle is the official point from which distances to and from New York City are measured on highway signs.

Look at the twin glass towers on the west side of the circle. This is the Deutsche Bank Center, formerly the Time Warner Center, completed in 2003, on the site of the old New York Coliseum, a convention hall built by Robert Moses. It houses offices, luxury condos, a hotel, restaurants, and Jazz at Lincoln Center's concert halls, with a window behind the stage overlooking Central Park.

On the south side of the circle, at 2 Columbus Circle, is a curious white marble building with round porthole-like openings at its base. It was designed by Edward Durell Stone and completed in 1964 as a museum for the collection of the A&P supermarket heir Huntington Hartford. Critics mocked it; the architecture critic Ada Louise Huxtable called it a die-cut Venetian palazzo on lollipops. It was controversially remodeled in the 2000s, and now houses the Museum of Arts and Design.

A block south, at Eighth Avenue and 57th Street, look for the Hearst Tower, a glass skyscraper with a distinctive diagonal grid pattern, rising from a six-story Art Deco stone base. William Randolph Hearst, the newspaper and magazine baron who built a media empire on sensational yellow journalism, commissioned the base in 1928 as the foundation for a skyscraper. The Depression halted the plan. Nearly eighty years later, in 2006, the Hearst Corporation finally completed the tower, designed by Norman Foster, rising from the old base. The company, publisher of magazines like Cosmopolitan and Esquire, still has its headquarters there.
=== STOP Lincoln Center & the Lost San Juan Hill @ 40.7725,-73.9835
You're at Lincoln Center for the Performing Arts, between 62nd and 66th Streets, Columbus and Amsterdam Avenues. Walk into the main plaza and stand near the Revson Fountain.

Around you are three great halls. Straight ahead is the Metropolitan Opera House, with its five tall arched windows. Inside, visible through the windows, hang two enormous murals by Marc Chagall. To the left is the David H. Koch Theater, home of the New York City Ballet. To the right is David Geffen Hall, home of the New York Philharmonic. Nearby are the Juilliard School, the Vivian Beaumont Theater, the New York Public Library for the Performing Arts, and other institutions.

Lincoln Center was built between 1959 and the late 1960s, championed by John D. Rockefeller the Third and Robert Moses. It was the first major center of its kind in the world, bringing together opera, symphony, ballet, theater, and education in one complex. President Dwight Eisenhower broke ground in 1959.

But there's a story underneath the plaza. Before Lincoln Center, this area was a neighborhood called San Juan Hill. In the early twentieth century, it was one of the largest African American neighborhoods in Manhattan, before Harlem became the center of Black New York. Its name may refer to the Battle of San Juan Hill in the Spanish-American War, in which Black soldiers fought. San Juan Hill was a crowded neighborhood of tenements, with a vibrant musical culture. The stride pianist James P. Johnson lived and played here, and he said the dance the Charleston was inspired by the dances of Gullah dockworkers from South Carolina he saw in the neighborhood. The jazz pianist Thelonious Monk lived on West 63rd Street for decades. By the 1940s and 50s, the neighborhood was largely Black and Puerto Rican.

In the 1950s, the city declared the area a slum and cleared it as part of the Lincoln Square urban renewal project. Thousands of families were displaced. Before the buildings were demolished, the film crew for the 1961 movie version of West Side Story shot its opening scenes in the neighborhood's streets, a story about Puerto Rican and white gangs fighting over the very neighborhood that was about to be destroyed.

Lincoln Center has acknowledged this history in recent years with exhibitions and programs. As you look around this elegant plaza, it's worth remembering the neighborhood that was erased to make room for it.
=== STOP The Dakota @ 40.7765,-73.9762
At Central Park West and 72nd Street stands the Dakota, one of the most famous apartment buildings in the world. Look at its steep roofs, gables, dormers, and towers, its yellow brick and sandstone, and its moat-like areaway surrounded by an iron fence.

The Dakota was completed in 1884, designed by Henry Janeway Hardenbergh, who later designed the Plaza Hotel. It was built by Edward Clark, the lawyer who was president of the Singer Manufacturing Company, the sewing machine giant. Clark had made a fortune from Singer and invested heavily in West Side real estate.

At the time, wealthy New Yorkers lived in private houses, and apartments were associated with tenements. Clark set out to create apartments luxurious enough to attract the rich. The Dakota had large apartments, some with as many as twenty rooms, with high ceilings, elaborate woodwork, and modern amenities, including central heating, its own electrical generating plant, and hydraulic elevators. Its interior courtyard was large enough for carriages to drive in.

A popular story says it was called the Dakota because it was so far uptown, so remote from the city, that it might as well have been in the Dakota Territory. Historians think it was more likely just part of Clark's fondness for western names; he also proposed renaming the West Side avenues after western states. But the story stuck.

The Dakota became a home for artists, musicians, and actors, including Leonard Bernstein, Lauren Bacall, Boris Karloff, Judy Garland, and Rudolf Nureyev. The building's co-op board is famously selective, and it reportedly turned down several celebrities who wanted to buy in. Its gloomy, Gothic exterior made it the setting for Roman Polanski's 1968 horror film Rosemary's Baby.

And, most famously, it was the home of John Lennon and Yoko Ono. On December eighth, 1980, Lennon was shot and killed just outside the building's entrance, the arched carriage gate on 72nd Street. Yoko Ono continued to live in the building for many years. The Strawberry Fields memorial in Central Park is just across the street.

Look up and down Central Park West. The avenue is lined with grand apartment buildings, many of them built in the 1920s and 30s, including the twin-towered San Remo at 74th Street and the Majestic and the Century, with their Art Deco towers. Their twin towers create one of the most recognizable skylines in the city, especially viewed from across the park.
=== STOP The Ansonia & Verdi Square @ 40.7806,-73.9816
Walk west to Broadway and 73rd Street. Look up at the huge, ornate Beaux-Arts building with rounded corner towers, iron balconies, and a mansard roof. This is the Ansonia, completed in 1904, one of the grandest and strangest apartment hotels ever built.

Its developer was William Earl Dodge Stokes, a wealthy and eccentric heir to a copper and real estate fortune. He named it after his grandfather, Anson Greene Phelps, whose company, Phelps Dodge, became a mining giant. Stokes's ambitions were enormous. The Ansonia had hundreds of apartments, a grand lobby, Turkish baths, and one of the world's largest indoor swimming pools at the time. And on the roof, Stokes kept a small farm, with chickens, goats, ducks, and reportedly a small bear. He sold fresh eggs to residents at a discount. In the lobby, there was a fountain with live seals. The city's health department eventually shut down the farm.

The Ansonia's thick walls made it nearly soundproof, which attracted musicians and singers. The opera star Enrico Caruso, the composer Igor Stravinsky, the conductor Arturo Toscanini, and the singer Lily Pons all lived here at various times.

Baseball fans have a reason to visit too. Babe Ruth lived here in the early 1920s, after joining the Yankees. And in 1919, members of the Chicago White Sox reportedly met in a room at the Ansonia with gamblers to plan the fixing of the World Series, the infamous Black Sox scandal, in which eight players were banned from baseball for life.

In the 1970s, the building's basement was home to the Continental Baths, a gay bathhouse where Bette Midler got her start, singing for patrons with Barry Manilow accompanying her on piano. Later, the space became Plato's Retreat, a notorious swingers' club.

Just to the north is Verdi Square, a small triangular park with a statue of the Italian composer Giuseppe Verdi, surrounded by characters from his operas. In the 1960s and 70s, this park became notorious as Needle Park because of open drug use, and was the setting for the 1971 film The Panic in Needle Park, an early starring role for Al Pacino. Today, the park has been restored, and the neighborhood is a world away from those days.

Across Broadway, the subway station at 72nd Street has its original 1904 control house, a small Beaux-Arts building, the entrance to one of the first subway stations in the city.
=== STOP The American Museum of Natural History @ 40.7813,-73.9740
You're on Central Park West, between 77th and 81st Streets, at the American Museum of Natural History, one of the largest natural history museums in the world.

It was founded in 1869 by the naturalist Albert Bickmore and a group of wealthy New Yorkers, including Theodore Roosevelt Senior. Its first home was in the Arsenal in Central Park. In 1877, it moved into its first building here, on what was then a rocky, rural site called Manhattan Square. Today, the museum complex is a patchwork of more than two dozen interconnected buildings, built over a century and a half, in different styles.

The main entrance on Central Park West is the Theodore Roosevelt Memorial, completed in 1936, honoring Theodore Roosevelt Junior, the former president, naturalist, and conservationist, who was a lifelong supporter of the museum. For decades, a bronze equestrian statue of Roosevelt flanked by an African man and a Native American man stood on the front steps. Critics argued that it portrayed the two men as subordinate, and it was removed in 2022. It's been relocated to North Dakota.

Inside, the museum is famous for its dinosaur halls, which hold one of the greatest collections of fossils in the world, including a Tyrannosaurus rex. Its dioramas of animals in their habitats, created in the early twentieth century, are masterpieces of naturalistic art. The Milstein Hall of Ocean Life features a life-size model of a blue whale, ninety-four feet long, suspended from the ceiling.

On the north side, on 81st Street, look at the giant glass cube enclosing a sphere. This is the Rose Center for Earth and Space, which opened in 2000, and houses the Hayden Planetarium. Shortly after it opened, its exhibits grouped Pluto with the Kuiper Belt objects, not with the planets, a decision that caused a public uproar, years before astronomers officially reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet in 2006. The planetarium's director, Neil deGrasse Tyson, became a celebrity partly through that controversy.

On the Columbus Avenue side, look for the newest addition, the Richard Gilder Center for Science, Education, and Innovation, which opened in 2023. Designed by Jeanne Gang's firm, Studio Gang, its flowing, cave-like atrium is inspired by natural canyons and geological formations.

Behind the museum, on Columbus Avenue, there's a popular Sunday flea and farmers' market.
=== STOP Zabar's & Upper Broadway @ 40.7848,-73.9797
Walk to Broadway and 80th Street. Here, you'll find Zabar's, the most famous food store on the Upper West Side.

Zabar's was founded in 1934 by Louis and Lillian Zabar, Jewish immigrants from Ukraine, who started with a small counter selling smoked fish. They grew it into a sprawling gourmet store, now spread across a full block front. Inside, you'll find smoked fish, coffee, cheese, breads, and cooking equipment upstairs. The store is still run by the Zabar family. Its bright orange signs and shopping bags are an icon of the neighborhood.

Zabar's is part of the larger story of the Upper West Side's Jewish culture. After the subway opened in 1904, many Jewish families moved here from the Lower East Side and Harlem, and the West Side became one of the most important centers of Jewish life in America. The neighborhood is home to many synagogues, delis, and bakeries. Nearby on Broadway, H and H Bagels was a famous bagel shop for decades; Barney Greengrass, on Amsterdam Avenue at 86th Street, has been selling smoked fish and appetizing since 1908, calling itself The Sturgeon King.

Look up and down Broadway. Notice how wide it is, with a planted median down the middle. That's because the old Bloomingdale Road was widened into a grand boulevard in the 1860s and 70s, called the Boulevard, before it was renamed Broadway in 1899. The subway runs right underneath it.

At Broadway and 78th to 79th Streets, look for the Apthorp, a giant Italian Renaissance apartment building, completed in 1908, built by William Waldorf Astor, the same Astor who built the Waldorf Hotel. It has a large interior courtyard with gardens and fountains, and grand iron gates at its entrances. It was among the first and largest courtyard apartment buildings in the city, and it was the model for a style of luxury living that defined the Upper West Side: big, solid buildings with plenty of light and air.

The neighborhood was also home to the Thalia, a repertory cinema on West 95th Street, which appears in Annie Hall, and Symphony Space, which still hosts readings and concerts.
=== STOP Riverside Park & the Soldiers' and Sailors' Monument @ 40.7930,-73.9776
End your walk in Riverside Park, at Riverside Drive and 89th Street, at the Soldiers' and Sailors' Monument.

Riverside Park stretches for about four miles along the Hudson, from 59th Street up to 158th Street. Frederick Law Olmsted, one of the designers of Central Park, designed the original park and Riverside Drive in the 1870s. The drive curves along the contours of the land, unlike the rigid street grid, and was designed for carriages, with views of the river and the New Jersey Palisades.

There was one big problem: the New York Central Railroad's tracks ran along the shore at the bottom of the park, with smoke, noise, and soot. In the 1930s, Robert Moses covered the tracks with a deck, extended the park on landfill, and built the Henry Hudson Parkway along the water, as part of the West Side Improvement Project. The rail line is still there, in a tunnel under the park, and is used today by Amtrak trains. Moses's project also created the 79th Street Rotunda and Boat Basin, where people still live year-round on houseboats.

The white marble monument in front of you, a circular temple of Corinthian columns, is the Soldiers' and Sailors' Memorial, completed in 1902 to honor the Union soldiers and sailors who fought in the Civil War. It was modeled on the ancient Choragic Monument of Lysicrates in Athens.

Look out over the river from the monument's terrace. The views of the Hudson and the New Jersey Palisades are among the best in the city, especially at sunset.

Riverside Drive is lined with handsome apartment buildings and a few surviving mansions. Some of its most famous residents included the newspaper publisher William Randolph Hearst, who lived in a vast apartment at the Clarendon, at Riverside Drive and 86th Street, which he expanded until it took up several floors, and the composer George Gershwin, who lived on Riverside Drive in the 1920s.

Up and down the park, look for other monuments: a statue of Joan of Arc at 93rd Street, a statue of Eleanor Roosevelt at 72nd Street, and a garden at 91st Street that featured prominently in the final scene of the 1998 film You've Got Mail. Riverside Park is less famous than Central Park, but many West Siders love it more: quieter, more local, and with a river view.
