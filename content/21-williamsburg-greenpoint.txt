blurb: The Sugar Trust's refinery, Brewers' Row, Peter Luger, the hipster boom, Eberhard Faber pencils, Pratt's Astral Oil, and the USS Monitor.
=== OVERVIEW
Williamsburg and Greenpoint, along the East River in North Brooklyn, were once among the most intensely industrial places in America. In the second half of the nineteenth century, this waterfront was lined with sugar refineries, oil refineries, breweries, shipyards, ironworks, glass and porcelain factories, and pencil makers. Greenpoint was famous for what were called the five black arts: printing, pottery, gas, glass, and iron, all industries that involved fire and smoke.

Some of the most powerful companies in American history had major operations here. The sugar refinery of the Havemeyer family became the heart of the Sugar Trust, which controlled almost all of the sugar refining in the country. Charles Pratt's Astral Oil Works became part of John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil. Brewers made Williamsburg and neighboring Bushwick one of the beer capitals of America. And in Greenpoint, the shipyards built the USS Monitor, the ironclad warship that changed naval warfare.

Williamsburg was a separate city until 1855, when it merged with Brooklyn. The opening of the Williamsburg Bridge in 1903 brought a flood of new residents from the crowded Lower East Side, especially Jewish families. After World War Two, Hasidic Jews, many of them Holocaust survivors from Hungary and Romania, settled in South Williamsburg, which remains one of the largest Hasidic communities in the world. Puerto Rican and Dominican families settled in the area known as Los Sures, on the South Side. Greenpoint became the heart of the Polish community in New York, often called Little Poland.

As factories closed in the second half of the twentieth century, the waterfront emptied. In the 1990s, artists, musicians, and young people priced out of Manhattan discovered the cheap lofts of Williamsburg, a stop or two from Manhattan on the L train. By the 2000s, Williamsburg had become a global symbol of hipster culture, of indie rock, artisanal food, and fixed-gear bicycles. In 2005, the city rezoned the waterfront, and luxury towers rose where factories had stood. Today, the neighborhood is one of the most expensive in Brooklyn, and the transformation has been a subject of fierce debate.

This tour runs south to north, from the Domino Sugar refinery to Newtown Creek, the border between Brooklyn and Queens.
=== STOP Domino Sugar & the Sugar Trust @ 40.7144,-73.9682
You're at Domino Park, on the waterfront at Kent Avenue and South 3rd Street. Look at the massive brick building along the water, with the giant illuminated sign that reads Domino Sugar. This is the old Domino Sugar refinery.

The story starts with the Havemeyer family, German immigrants who began refining sugar in Manhattan in the early nineteenth century. In 1856, they built a refinery here in Williamsburg, on the waterfront, where ships could unload raw sugar from the Caribbean. After a fire, the main building was rebuilt in 1882, and it was then one of the largest sugar refineries in the world. By the late nineteenth century, the Williamsburg refineries were producing more than half of the sugar consumed in the United States.

Henry Osborne Havemeyer was the driving force behind one of the most powerful monopolies of the Gilded Age. In 1887, he organized the Sugar Refineries Company, combining most of the major refiners into a trust, which was later reorganized as the American Sugar Refining Company. By the early 1890s, it controlled the vast majority of the country's sugar refining. When the federal government sued, in a case known as United States versus E. C. Knight Company, the Supreme Court ruled in 1895 that the Sherman Antitrust Act didn't apply, because refining was manufacturing, not interstate commerce. The decision greatly weakened antitrust enforcement for years, until later cases, including the breakup of Standard Oil, restored it. In 1900, the company introduced the Domino brand, and it eventually renamed itself Domino Sugar.

The work inside was grueling and dangerous: workers labored in intense heat, and many were immigrants, first German and Irish, later Polish, Puerto Rican, and others. The refinery closed in 2004.

In 2014, before redevelopment began, the artist Kara Walker filled the building's vast, sticky interior with a giant sculpture made of sugar: a sphinx-like woman with the features of a stereotypical mammy figure, called A Subtlety, or the Marvelous Sugar Baby. It confronted the history of slavery in the Caribbean sugar plantations that produced the raw sugar refined here. More than a hundred thousand people came to see it.

The site has since been redeveloped by Two Trees Management, the same company that redeveloped DUMBO. The waterfront became Domino Park, which opened in 2018, designed by James Corner Field Operations, the firm behind the High Line. Look for the old cranes, gantries, and syrup tanks that were incorporated into the park's design, and for the walkway built from salvaged industrial pieces.
=== STOP Peter Luger & South Williamsburg @ 40.7099,-73.9625
Walk to Broadway and Driggs Avenue, near the foot of the Williamsburg Bridge. Look for a plain brick building with a sign reading Peter Luger Steak House.

Peter Luger has been serving steaks here since 1887. It was founded as Carl Luger's Café, Billiards and Bowling Alley, in what was then a German neighborhood, near the ferry to Manhattan. Peter Luger was the owner; his nephew Carl was the chef. After Peter Luger's death, the restaurant declined. In 1950, it was bought at auction by Sol Forman, a businessman whose metalware factory was across the street, and who had eaten lunch there for years. His wife, Marsha, learned to select beef, and the family turned it into the most famous steakhouse in New York. Its porterhouse steak, dry-aged on the premises and served sizzling, is legendary. For many years it accepted only cash or its own house card.

Look up at the Williamsburg Bridge. As you may have heard on the Lower East Side tour, when it opened in 1903 it was the longest suspension bridge in the world. It transformed Williamsburg from a German neighborhood into a crowded, largely Jewish and Eastern European neighborhood almost overnight.

South of Broadway is South Williamsburg, home of a large Hasidic Jewish community, centered on the Satmar sect. The Satmar Hasidim came from the town of Satu Mare, in present-day Romania. Their leader, Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, survived the Holocaust and arrived in New York in 1946. He built a community here that has grown into tens of thousands of people. The community is very traditional, speaking Yiddish as its primary language, with distinctive dress: men in black coats and hats, with sidelocks, and women in modest dresses with head coverings. Walk along Lee Avenue, the main commercial street, and you'll see kosher shops, Yiddish signs, and bustling families.

The area to the east, along Broadway and the South Side, known as Los Sures, has been the heart of the Puerto Rican and Dominican community in Williamsburg since the 1950s. Community organizations here fought for affordable housing in the 1970s and 80s, when many buildings were abandoned.
=== STOP Brewers' Row & the Brooklyn Brewery @ 40.7217,-73.9575
Walk north to North 11th Street, near Wythe Avenue, to the Brooklyn Brewery.

In the late nineteenth century, Brooklyn was one of the largest beer-producing places in America. German immigrants brought lager brewing with them, and by the 1890s, Brooklyn had several dozen breweries, concentrated in Williamsburg and Bushwick, in an area nicknamed Brewers' Row. Brands like Schaefer, Rheingold, Piels, and Trommer's were brewed here. The Schaefer brewery, on the Williamsburg waterfront along Kent Avenue, was one of the largest in the country. Its slogan, Schaefer is the one beer to have when you're having more than one, was famous in the mid-twentieth century.

Prohibition, from 1920 to 1933, closed many breweries. Those that survived faced competition after World War Two from national brewers like Anheuser-Busch, which could produce beer more cheaply in giant, modern breweries and advertise on national television. Brooklyn's breweries closed one after another. Schaefer and Rheingold, the last big ones, closed in 1976. For about a decade, there was no commercial brewing in Brooklyn.

Then, in 1988, two men founded the Brooklyn Brewery: Steve Hindy, a former Associated Press foreign correspondent who had learned home brewing from diplomats in the Middle East, and Tom Potter, a banker. They hired the brewmaster Bill Moeller, whose family had brewed beer in Germany, and they hired the famous graphic designer Milton Glaser, who created the I heart NY logo, to design their logo. The first beers were contract brewed upstate. In 1996, they opened their own brewery here, in a former ironworks. In 1994, they hired Garrett Oliver as brewmaster, and he became one of the most influential figures in American craft brewing.

The Brooklyn Brewery was one of the pioneers of the craft beer movement, and it helped revive Williamsburg's industrial waterfront. It has offered tours and tastings to visitors for years, and its connection to Williamsburg defines the brand.

Look around at the old brick factory and warehouse buildings nearby. Many of them have been converted to offices, shops, music venues, and condominiums.
=== STOP Bedford Avenue & Hipster Williamsburg @ 40.7172,-73.9566
Walk to Bedford Avenue and North 7th Street, at the Bedford Avenue station on the L train, the first stop in Brooklyn coming from Manhattan.

In the 1970s and 80s, this area was a struggling industrial neighborhood, with Polish, Italian, and Puerto Rican working-class families and many empty factories. In the late 1980s and 1990s, artists began moving into the lofts, priced out of the East Village and SoHo. The L train put them just one stop from Manhattan. By the late 1990s, Williamsburg had a thriving underground art and music scene, with galleries, performance spaces, and illegal warehouse parties.

In the 2000s, Williamsburg became internationally famous. Bands like Yeah Yeah Yeahs, TV on the Radio, and Vampire Weekend were associated with the Brooklyn indie rock scene. Coffee shops, vintage clothing stores, and restaurants serving artisanal food opened along Bedford Avenue. The word hipster became almost synonymous with Williamsburg. Magazines and newspapers around the world wrote about it, and the neighborhood's aesthetic, beards, tattoos, flannel, craft beer, and pickles, was exported to cities everywhere.

Then the money came. In 2005, the city rezoned the Williamsburg and Greenpoint waterfront, allowing towers of up to forty stories on land that had been zoned for manufacturing. Glass towers rose along the river. Rents soared. Many of the artists who had made the neighborhood famous were priced out, moving farther east to Bushwick and beyond. Big brands arrived: Apple, Whole Foods, and luxury hotels opened stores and locations near Bedford Avenue.

Walk toward the river, to Marsha P. Johnson State Park, formerly East River State Park, named in 2020 for the transgender activist who was a central figure in the gay liberation movement. On weekends in warm months, it's the site of Smorgasburg, a giant outdoor food market, launched in 2011, with dozens of vendors.

From the waterfront, look across the river at the Manhattan skyline, from the Williamsburg Bridge to the Empire State Building. Every new tower on this side of the river was built to capture this view.
=== STOP McCarren Park & the Pool @ 40.7203,-73.9512
Walk east to McCarren Park, at the border between Williamsburg and Greenpoint, around Bedford Avenue and Lorimer Street.

On the west side of the park, look for the large building with a grand arched entrance. This is McCarren Park Pool, one of eleven huge public swimming pools that opened in New York City in the summer of 1936, built by Robert Moses with funding from the federal Works Progress Administration during the Great Depression. The eleven pools were designed to bring recreation and relief to working-class neighborhoods during the heat of summer. McCarren Pool could hold nearly sixty-eight hundred swimmers at a time.

The pool was a beloved neighborhood institution for decades, but by the 1980s, it had deteriorated, and the city closed it in 1984. It sat empty for more than twenty years, a ruin surrounded by a fence. In the mid-2000s, while plans for its restoration were made, the empty pool became a concert venue, hosting free summer concerts by indie rock bands, as well as dodgeball leagues and other events, part of Williamsburg's emergence as a cultural destination. After a major restoration, the pool reopened for swimming in 2012. In winter, part of it becomes an ice skating rink.

Just east of the park, at North 12th Street and Driggs Avenue, look for the Russian Orthodox Cathedral of the Transfiguration of Our Lord, with its five green onion domes. It was built in the 1920s, and it's a reminder of the many Eastern European communities in North Brooklyn.

The park is named for Patrick McCarren, a state senator and Democratic political boss of Brooklyn in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Today, it's the backyard of the neighborhood, filled with soccer games, softball, runners, and people lounging on the grass.
=== STOP Greenpoint & the Eberhard Faber Pencil Factory @ 40.7295,-73.9580
Walk north into Greenpoint, to the corner of Kent Street and Franklin Street, near the Greenpoint waterfront. Look at the buildings at Greenpoint Avenue and West Street, and on Kent Street. On the facade of one of them, look for decorative ornaments shaped like giant yellow pencils, along with stars.

These were the buildings of the Eberhard Faber Pencil Company. Eberhard Faber came from a German family that had been making pencils in Bavaria since the eighteenth century, and he came to New York in 1848 to represent the family business. In 1861, he opened a pencil factory in Manhattan. After it burned down in 1872, he moved the factory here to Greenpoint, and over the following decades the company expanded into a complex of buildings. Eberhard Faber became one of the largest pencil manufacturers in the country. Its Mongol pencil was one of the most popular pencils in America. The company also made erasers, rubber bands, and pens. The factory moved to Pennsylvania in 1956, and the buildings were converted to other uses. They're now protected as a historic district.

The pencil company was one of many factories in Greenpoint. Along with the five black arts, Greenpoint had shipbuilding, rope making, and sugar refining. The Union Porcelain Works, in Greenpoint, produced fine porcelain tableware in the nineteenth century, some of which is now in the collections of the Metropolitan Museum and the Brooklyn Museum.

Walk along Manhattan Avenue, Greenpoint's main shopping street, a few blocks to the east. For more than a century, Greenpoint has been the heart of the Polish community in New York, with Polish bakeries, butcher shops selling kielbasa, delis, churches, and restaurants. Polish immigrants came in waves, in the late nineteenth century, after World War Two, and again in the 1980s, when many fled the communist government's crackdown on the Solidarity movement. The Polish population has declined as younger generations have moved and new residents have arrived, but you'll still hear Polish spoken on the street.

Look also at the beautiful churches of Greenpoint, including St. Anthony of Padua, on Manhattan Avenue, with its tall spire, and St. Stanislaus Kostka, the Polish Catholic parish, which Pope John Paul the Second visited as a cardinal in 1969 and again in 1976.
=== STOP Charles Pratt's Astral Oil & the Monitor @ 40.7316,-73.9585
Walk north along Franklin Street, to Franklin and India Streets.

In the nineteenth century, Greenpoint and the shore of Newtown Creek, just to the north, became one of the largest oil-refining centers in America. In 1867, Charles Pratt, a businessman who had made money in paint and oil, opened the Astral Oil Works on the creek. He produced kerosene, a fuel refined from petroleum, used for lighting lamps. Pratt's brand, Astral Oil, was known for its quality and safety, at a time when cheap kerosene often exploded. Its advertising slogan claimed that the holy lamps of Tibet were primed with Astral Oil.

In 1874, Pratt sold his company to John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, becoming one of its leading partners and making a vast fortune. He used part of it to found the Pratt Institute, an art and engineering school in Clinton Hill, which opened in 1887 to train working people in practical skills. He also built the Astral Apartments, at 184 Franklin Street, completed in 1886, as model housing for his workers, with better light, air, and sanitation than typical tenements of the time. Look for it, a large, handsome brick building in the Queen Anne style.

The oil industry left a toxic legacy. In 1978, a Coast Guard helicopter patrol spotted an oil slick on Newtown Creek. Investigators discovered that over many decades, oil from refineries and storage facilities had leaked into the ground under Greenpoint. Estimates of the total ranged from seventeen to thirty million gallons, making it one of the largest oil spills in American history, larger than the Exxon Valdez spill. Cleanup has continued for decades, and Newtown Creek is now a federal Superfund site.

Now walk to the waterfront at the end of India Street, near the ferry pier. Just north of here, at the Continental Iron Works on the Greenpoint shore, the USS Monitor was built. The Monitor was a revolutionary ironclad warship, designed by the Swedish-born engineer John Ericsson, with a low, armored hull and a rotating gun turret, something no ship had used in battle before. It was built in about one hundred days and launched on January thirtieth, 1862. On March ninth, 1862, at the Battle of Hampton Roads, in Virginia, the Monitor fought the Confederate ironclad CSS Virginia, formerly the Merrimack, to a standoff. It was the first battle between ironclad warships, and it signaled the end of the age of wooden warships. In McGolrick Park, a few blocks east, look for a monument to the Monitor and its crew.
=== STOP Newtown Creek & the Digester Eggs @ 40.7360,-73.9500
End your walk at the Newtown Creek Nature Walk, at the end of Paidge Avenue, on the shore of Newtown Creek, the waterway that separates Brooklyn from Queens.

Look at the giant, shining, egg-shaped structures rising beside the creek. These are the digester eggs of the Newtown Creek Wastewater Treatment Plant, the largest of the city's wastewater treatment plants. There are eight of them, each about one hundred forty feet tall, clad in stainless steel. Inside, bacteria break down sludge from the city's sewage, producing methane gas. The egg shape helps the sludge mix and the process run efficiently. Completed in the 2010s, they've become an unlikely architectural landmark, and they glow blue at night. The plant offers tours a few times a year, including at sunrise or sunset, and the view from the top of the eggs is said to be remarkable.

The Nature Walk was designed by the artist George Trakas, and opened in 2007, as part of the plant's commitment to the community. It's a winding path along the water, with granite steps, plantings, and references to the creek's history. It includes a timeline of the area carved into stone, and features that recall the creek's natural past, before industry transformed it.

Newtown Creek was once a tidal estuary, with marshes and wildlife. The Lenape fished here, and Dutch and English settlers farmed nearby. In the nineteenth century, it became one of the busiest industrial waterways in the country, lined with refineries, chemical plants, fertilizer works, and factories. At one time, the creek carried more cargo than the Mississippi River, by some accounts. It also became one of the most polluted waterways in the nation, absorbing oil, chemicals, and raw sewage.

Today, community groups and the federal government are working on a long, slow cleanup. Kayakers and canoeists now paddle the creek, and scientists monitor its recovery.

It's a fitting place to end a tour of North Brooklyn: a place where industry made fortunes, left scars, and is now being slowly transformed into something new.
