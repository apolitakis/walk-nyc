=== TOUR
id: morningside
title: Morningside Heights
area: Manhattan
blurb: The unfinished cathedral, Columbia and the birth of the atomic age, Riverside Church, and the man in Grant's Tomb.
=== OVERVIEW
Morningside Heights is a plateau high above Harlem, bounded by the cliffs of Morningside Park on the east and the Hudson River on the west. Around the turn of the twentieth century, it became home to so many major institutions, a university, a cathedral, seminaries, a hospital, and a monumental tomb, that it was nicknamed the Acropolis of America.

Before that, it was rural and isolated. In the colonial era, it was farmland. In September 1776, it was the site of the Battle of Harlem Heights, one of George Washington's few early successes in the New York campaign. After a string of defeats on Long Island and in lower Manhattan, Washington's troops fought the British to a standstill in the fields around what is now 120th Street and Broadway, near the Columbia campus. The victory was small, but it boosted the morale of an army that badly needed it.

In the nineteenth century, the plateau was dominated by the Bloomingdale Insane Asylum, a psychiatric hospital surrounded by gardens, and the Leake and Watts Orphan House. The asylum was considered progressive for its time, offering patients fresh air and landscaped grounds. When the city grew northward and land became valuable, those institutions moved out and new ones moved in. In the 1890s, Columbia University bought the asylum's land, the Episcopal Church began building a massive cathedral nearby, and General Grant's Tomb rose on Riverside Drive. The subway arrived in 1904, and the neighborhood filled with apartment buildings for professors, students, and middle-class families.

Morningside Heights has also been a place of conflict. Columbia's relationship with its Harlem neighbors has often been tense, as the university expanded, bought up buildings, and, in 1968, proposed building a gymnasium in Morningside Park, which triggered one of the most famous student uprisings in American history.

On this walk, you'll see one of the largest cathedrals in the world, which is still unfinished more than a century after construction began; the campus where the first nuclear fission experiment in the United States was carried out; a soaring Gothic church built by John D. Rockefeller Junior; and the tomb of Ulysses S. Grant, whose story ends with a financial scandal you won't expect.
=== STOP Morningside Park @ 40.8063,-73.9584
You're at Morningside Park, the long, narrow park that runs along a steep cliff from 110th Street to 123rd Street, separating Morningside Heights above from Harlem below.

The park was designed by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, the designers of Central Park, in the 1870s and 80s. The cliff was too steep for buildings, so the city turned it into a park. Olmsted and Vaux designed it with a walkway along the top of the cliff, offering sweeping views east across Harlem, and paths and stairs winding down through rocky outcrops to the flatter area at the bottom.

For much of the twentieth century, the park was neglected, and it became known as dangerous. It also became a symbol of the divide between Columbia, at the top of the cliff, and Harlem, at the bottom.

That divide exploded in 1968. Columbia planned to build a new gymnasium in the park, on land leased from the city. The plan included a separate, smaller entrance on the Harlem side, at the bottom of the cliff, for community residents, while Columbia students would enter from the top. Critics called it Gym Crow, comparing it to the Jim Crow segregation laws of the South.

In April 1968, student protesters, led by groups including the Students for a Democratic Society and the Student Afro-American Society, occupied several campus buildings, including the president's office in Low Library. They protested the gym, and the university's ties to the Institute for Defense Analyses, a think tank doing research for the Vietnam War. After a week, the police stormed the buildings, arresting more than seven hundred people, with violence that injured many students and turned much of the campus against the administration. The university canceled the gym, and the protests became a turning point in the student movement of the 1960s.

Look for the pond and waterfall at the bottom of the park, near 113th Street. They were built on the site where gym construction had begun, as a kind of healing gesture. The park has been substantially restored since the 1990s and is now well used by both Columbia and Harlem residents.
=== STOP The Cathedral of St. John the Divine @ 40.8038,-73.9619
At Amsterdam Avenue and 112th Street stands the Cathedral of St. John the Divine, the mother church of the Episcopal Diocese of New York, and one of the largest cathedrals in the world.

The cornerstone was laid on December twenty-seventh, 1892, the feast day of St. John. The original design, by the architects George Heins and Christopher Grant LaFarge, was in a Romanesque and Byzantine style. After Heins died in 1907, the church hired Ralph Adams Cram, the leading Gothic Revival architect in America, who redesigned the nave in a French Gothic style. That's why the building looks like a mix of styles. The eastern end, with its rounded arches, is from the original design. The long nave, with its pointed arches and soaring vault, is Cram's.

Construction has stopped and started many times, interrupted by two world wars, the Depression, and financial troubles. The cathedral has never been finished. Its towers on the west front are incomplete stumps, and the planned central tower was never built. New Yorkers nicknamed it St. John the Unfinished. In the 1980s, the cathedral ran a program training young people from Harlem in medieval stone carving, and they carved some of the statues on the west front portal. Look closely and you'll find some surprising details, including an apocalyptic scene with a recognizable depiction of the Manhattan skyline being destroyed.

Step inside, if you can. The nave is about six hundred feet long, longer than two football fields, and its vaulted ceiling rises over one hundred twenty feet. The cathedral regularly hosts concerts, art installations, and events, and every October, it holds a Blessing of the Animals for the Feast of St. Francis, in which people bring dogs, cats, birds, and even camels and elephants to be blessed.

In 2001, a fire badly damaged the north transept, which was home to the cathedral's gift shop. It took years to clean and restore.

Look for the peacocks. The cathedral keeps a few of them, and they roam the grounds. In the garden on the south side, look for the Peace Fountain, a large bronze sculpture by Greg Wyatt, depicting the struggle between good and evil, with the archangel Michael and a giant crab.

Across Amsterdam Avenue is the Hungarian Pastry Shop, a beloved café for Columbia students and faculty for decades, where writers have worked on dissertations, novels, and screenplays over endless cups of coffee.
=== STOP Columbia University @ 40.8077,-73.9626
Walk to the main gate of Columbia University, at Broadway and 116th Street, and walk onto the College Walk.

Columbia was founded in 1754 as King's College, by royal charter from King George the Second. It's the oldest institution of higher learning in New York, and the fifth oldest in the country. Among its early students were Alexander Hamilton; John Jay, the first chief justice of the United States; Gouverneur Morris, who wrote the final draft of the Constitution; and Robert Livingston, who helped draft the Declaration of Independence. After the Revolution, it was renamed Columbia.

The college's first home was near City Hall Park. In 1857, it moved to Midtown, at 49th Street and Madison Avenue. In 1897, it moved here, to the former grounds of the Bloomingdale Asylum. The president of the university, Seth Low, hired the firm McKim, Mead and White to design the new campus, which was conceived as an academic city, inspired by Greek and Roman architecture.

At the center of the campus is Low Memorial Library, with its great dome and columned portico, completed in 1897 and named in honor of Seth Low's father, a merchant in the China trade. Low himself paid for it. It was the university's main library until 1934, when the larger Butler Library, across the quadrangle, opened. Look at Butler's facade: carved across the top are the names of great writers and thinkers from antiquity, including Homer, Herodotus, Sophocles, Plato, Aristotle, Demosthenes, Cicero, and Virgil.

On the steps of Low Library sits the bronze statue of Alma Mater, by Daniel Chester French, the sculptor of the Lincoln Memorial. Look closely in the folds of her robe for a small owl, a symbol of wisdom. Tradition says that the first freshman of each class to find it will become class valedictorian. During the 1968 protests, the statue was damaged by a bomb.

Columbia is also tied to modern finance. The Columbia Business School was where Benjamin Graham, the father of value investing, taught for decades, starting in the 1920s. His most famous student was Warren Buffett, who enrolled in 1950 after reading Graham's book The Intelligent Investor. Graham's classes, and his book Security Analysis, written with David Dodd, have shaped generations of investors.
=== STOP Pupin Hall & the Atomic Age @ 40.8100,-73.9614
Walk to the northwest corner of the campus, near Broadway and 120th Street, and look for Pupin Hall, the physics building, a tall brick and limestone tower with a small observatory dome on the roof.

It's named for Michael Pupin, a Serbian immigrant who arrived in New York in 1874 with almost nothing, worked as a laborer, put himself through Columbia, and became a renowned physicist and inventor. His inventions, including loading coils that dramatically improved long-distance telephone lines, made him wealthy. AT&T bought the rights to his patent. His autobiography, From Immigrant to Inventor, won the Pulitzer Prize in 1924.

But Pupin Hall is famous for what happened in its basement in January 1939. Just weeks earlier, scientists in Germany had discovered that uranium atoms could be split, releasing energy, a process called nuclear fission. When the news reached the United States, physicists at Columbia, led by John Dunning, set out to confirm it. On January twenty-fifth, 1939, using a cyclotron, a particle accelerator, in the basement of Pupin Hall, Dunning's team observed fission, among the first such experiments carried out in the United States. The Italian physicist Enrico Fermi, who had just fled fascist Italy, came to Columbia and worked here on nuclear chain reactions.

Fermi and Leo Szilard, also at Columbia, conducted early research on the possibility of a nuclear chain reaction. Szilard drafted the famous letter that Albert Einstein signed in August 1939, warning President Franklin Roosevelt that Germany might build an atomic bomb, and urging the United States to start its own research.

When the United States launched its effort to build the bomb, the Army Corps of Engineers set up the Manhattan Engineer District, headquartered at first in an office building at 270 Broadway, near City Hall. That's why the program became known as the Manhattan Project. Much of the early research took place here at Columbia, before the work shifted to Chicago, Oak Ridge, Hanford, and Los Alamos.

Pupin Hall was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1965. The cyclotron was dismantled in 2008. Standing outside this ordinary-looking academic building, it's sobering to think that the atomic age began, in part, in its basement.
=== STOP Riverside Church @ 40.8122,-73.9631
Walk west to Riverside Drive and 120th Street, to Riverside Church, with its massive Gothic tower rising over the Hudson.

The church was built by John D. Rockefeller Junior, the son of the Standard Oil founder, and completed in 1930. Rockefeller was a devout Baptist, and he wanted to create a great interdenominational church, open to all Christians. He chose as its minister Harry Emerson Fosdick, one of the most famous preachers of the era, a liberal Protestant who had been forced out of his previous pulpit for challenging fundamentalist doctrine.

The architecture was modeled largely on Chartres Cathedral in France, but the building has a steel frame, like a skyscraper. The tower is about three hundred ninety feet tall, and contains offices, classrooms, and meeting rooms, as well as the Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial Carillon, named for Rockefeller's mother. The carillon has seventy-four bells. Its largest bell, the bourdon, weighs about twenty tons, and is said to be the largest tuned carillon bell in the world. On Sundays, you can hear the bells ring across the neighborhood. You can sometimes take an elevator up the tower, for a tremendous view of the Hudson and upper Manhattan.

Riverside Church has been a center of progressive activism. On April fourth, 1967, exactly one year before his assassination, the Reverend Martin Luther King Junior delivered his speech Beyond Vietnam: A Time to Break Silence, from the pulpit here. In it, King publicly denounced the Vietnam War, calling the United States government the greatest purveyor of violence in the world today. The speech was condemned by much of the press and many of his allies at the time, and it marked a decisive shift in his public stance. In 1990, Nelson Mandela spoke here, just months after his release from prison in South Africa.

Many presidents, foreign leaders, and religious leaders have spoken here. The church has also been active in the anti-apartheid movement, the sanctuary movement for refugees, and LGBTQ rights.

Look at the west portal, which is modeled on the portal at Chartres, with carved figures of saints and prophets. Mixed in with the traditional figures are carvings of modern figures, including scientists and philosophers, including Albert Einstein. Einstein is said to be the only living person included when the carvings were made.
=== STOP Grant's Tomb @ 40.8133,-73.9631
End your walk across the street, at Riverside Drive and 122nd Street, at the General Grant National Memorial, better known as Grant's Tomb.

Here's a trivia question made famous by Groucho Marx on his quiz show You Bet Your Life, who used it to make sure every contestant went home with a prize: Who is buried in Grant's Tomb? The technical answer is: nobody. Ulysses S. Grant and his wife Julia are not buried underground. Their sarcophagi rest in a crypt, above ground, inside the monument. They're entombed, not buried.

Ulysses S. Grant was the commanding general of the Union Army who won the Civil War, and he served two terms as president, from 1869 to 1877. After his presidency, he and Julia settled in New York, in a house on East 66th Street. And here's the part of Grant's story that many people don't know.

Grant's son, Ulysses Junior, known as Buck, had gone into business with a charming young Wall Street financier named Ferdinand Ward. Their firm, Grant and Ward, seemed to be wildly successful, and the former president invested nearly all his money in it and lent the firm his name. But Ward was running a fraud, borrowing against the same securities multiple times and paying old investors with new investors' money, a scheme that would today be called a Ponzi scheme. In May 1884, the firm collapsed, and the Grants were ruined. Grant was left nearly penniless. Ward went to prison.

That same year, Grant was diagnosed with throat cancer. Desperate to provide for his family, he began writing his memoirs. His friend Mark Twain offered him a generous publishing deal through Twain's own publishing company. Grant raced against death to finish, writing in great pain, and completed the manuscript just days before he died in July 1885. The Personal Memoirs of U. S. Grant became a massive bestseller, earning his widow a fortune, and are still regarded as among the finest military memoirs ever written.

Grant had requested to be buried in New York, and around ninety thousand people contributed to build this tomb, which was completed in 1897. It was designed by John Duncan, and inspired by the ancient Mausoleum at Halicarnassus. It's the largest mausoleum in North America. More than a million people attended the dedication parade.

Look around the back and sides of the tomb, at the colorful, wavy mosaic benches. They were created in the 1970s by the artist Pedro Silva, with community volunteers, inspired by the work of Antoni Gaudí in Barcelona. The benches caused controversy, since some felt they didn't match the solemn monument, but they've become a beloved part of the site.

Look north along Riverside Drive. Nearby is a small, easily missed monument, the Amiable Child memorial, marking the grave of a five-year-old boy named St. Claire Pollock, who died in 1797 when this land was a private estate. It's one of the only private graves on public land in New York City.
