title: Rockefeller Center, Park Avenue & Fifth Avenue
area: Manhattan
blurb: Rockefeller Center, St. Patrick's, MoMA, Park Avenue's glass towers, Gilded Age Fifth Avenue, the Plaza, and Carnegie Hall.
=== OVERVIEW
This walk covers the stretch of Midtown that has long been the showroom of American wealth and corporate power: Fifth Avenue, Park Avenue, and 57th Street.

In the second half of the nineteenth century, as the rich moved north from Madison Square, Fifth Avenue between about 50th and 70th Streets became a canyon of mansions. The Vanderbilts alone built a string of enormous houses along the avenue, each trying to outdo the others, in French château and Italian Renaissance styles. This was the Gilded Age, the era of railroad, oil, and steel fortunes, and Fifth Avenue was its main street.

Within a few decades, the mansions were gone. Rising property taxes, the income tax introduced in 1913, the cost of servants, and the relentless pressure of commerce made them impractical. Families sold them to developers and moved into apartments on the Upper East Side. Department stores and luxury retailers took over Fifth Avenue: Saks, Bergdorf Goodman, Tiffany, Cartier. Office buildings rose around them.

Then came Rockefeller Center, built in the 1930s during the Great Depression, a city within a city that became the model for the modern mixed-use urban development. After World War Two, Park Avenue, which had been lined with elegant apartment buildings, was transformed into a corridor of glass corporate headquarters, starting with Lever House and the Seagram Building, two of the most influential skyscrapers ever built. In recent years, the newest form of wealth display has appeared along 57th Street: super-tall, super-thin residential towers for the global ultra-rich, known as Billionaires' Row.

As you walk, notice how each era expressed wealth differently: Gilded Age mansions with marble and ornament; the Art Deco optimism of Rockefeller Center; the cool, minimalist glass of mid-century corporate modernism; and the needle-thin towers of today. Every one of them was built to impress you.
=== STOP Rockefeller Center @ 40.7587,-73.9787
You're at Rockefeller Plaza, between Fifth and Sixth Avenues and 48th to 51st Streets, the heart of Rockefeller Center.

The story begins in 1928. John D. Rockefeller Junior, the son of the Standard Oil founder, agreed to lease land here from Columbia University to build a new home for the Metropolitan Opera. Then the stock market crashed in 1929, and the opera pulled out. Rockefeller was stuck with a long-term lease on a large swath of Midtown real estate. Rather than walk away, he decided to build a commercial development on his own, with his own money. Construction began in 1930 and continued through the Depression, employing tens of thousands of workers when jobs were scarce.

The result, completed in the late 1930s, was fourteen Art Deco buildings, organized around plazas, gardens, and a private street, Rockefeller Plaza. It was one of the first developments to combine offices, shops, restaurants, theaters, and public space in a coherent design, and it's been a model for urban developments around the world ever since.

The centerpiece is 30 Rockefeller Plaza, the tall, slender limestone tower. It was originally the RCA Building, headquarters of the Radio Corporation of America, which owned NBC. NBC has broadcast from here since 1933, and shows like Saturday Night Live and The Tonight Show have been produced inside. The building was later renamed for General Electric, and then for Comcast, which now owns NBC.

During construction, in 1932, a photographer captured eleven ironworkers eating lunch on a steel beam high above the city, their legs dangling over the void. The image, Lunch atop a Skyscraper, became one of the most famous photographs of the twentieth century. It was taken on this building.

The center has a famous art controversy. Rockefeller commissioned the Mexican muralist Diego Rivera to paint a mural in the lobby of the RCA Building. Rivera, a communist, included a portrait of Vladimir Lenin. Rockefeller asked him to remove it. Rivera refused. The mural was covered and, in 1934, destroyed. Rivera later recreated it in Mexico City.

Look down into the sunken plaza, where the ice rink is in winter, and the gilded statue of Prometheus by Paul Manship, bringing fire to humanity. The famous Christmas tree tradition began in 1931, when construction workers set up a small tree on the site. It became official in 1933.

On Fifth Avenue, in front of the International Building, is the giant bronze statue of Atlas holding the heavens, by Lee Lawrie and Rene Chambellan. And at Sixth Avenue and 50th Street is Radio City Music Hall, opened in 1932, the largest indoor theater in the world at the time and home of the Rockettes.
=== STOP St. Patrick's Cathedral @ 40.7585,-73.9760
Across Fifth Avenue from Atlas is St. Patrick's Cathedral, the largest Gothic Revival Catholic cathedral in the United States.

When construction began in 1858, this site was far north of the city's center, and critics mocked the decision to build a cathedral out in the countryside. They called it Hughes's Folly, after Archbishop John Hughes, who pushed the project forward. Hughes was a formidable figure, an Irish immigrant nicknamed Dagger John because of the cross he drew next to his signature and because of his combative defense of Catholics against nativist attacks. Building a grand cathedral on Fifth Avenue was a statement that Irish Catholics, despised by many Protestant New Yorkers, had arrived. The project was funded by wealthy donors and by the small contributions of tens of thousands of working-class Irish immigrants.

Construction was interrupted by the Civil War, and the cathedral was finally dedicated in 1879. Its twin spires, which rise about three hundred thirty feet, were added in 1888. The architect was James Renwick Junior, who also designed Grace Church downtown and the Smithsonian Castle in Washington.

Inside, look for the great rose window, the bronze doors, and the Lady Chapel behind the altar. Beneath the altar is a crypt where past archbishops of New York are buried, along with Pierre Toussaint, the formerly enslaved Haitian philanthropist you may have heard about at Old St. Patrick's on the Lower East Side, who is the only layperson buried there.

The cathedral underwent a major restoration in the 2010s that cleaned the marble exterior, which had been blackened by pollution, back to its bright white.

Notice the dialogue between the cathedral and Rockefeller Center across the street. The Atlas statue faces the cathedral directly, holding up the heavens. Whether that was meant as a symbol of human ambition facing faith, or simply a coincidence of planning, it's become one of the most photographed juxtapositions in New York.

Next to the cathedral, on Fifth Avenue between 49th and 50th Streets, is Saks Fifth Avenue, which opened its flagship here in 1924. It was one of the first major department stores on upper Fifth Avenue, helping to cement the avenue's transformation from mansions to luxury retail.
=== STOP MoMA & Paley Park @ 40.7613,-73.9777
Walk to West 53rd Street, between Fifth and Sixth Avenues. Here you'll find the Museum of Modern Art.

MoMA was founded in 1929 by three wealthy women: Abby Aldrich Rockefeller, the wife of John D. Rockefeller Junior, and her friends Lillie P. Bliss and Mary Quinn Sullivan. At the time, established museums like the Met were reluctant to collect contemporary art. The founders wanted a museum dedicated to the art of their own time. It opened nine days after the stock market crash, in rented rooms in an office building. Its first director, Alfred Barr, was just twenty-seven years old. His vision, that a museum should show modern painting alongside photography, film, industrial design, and architecture, was revolutionary.

The museum moved to 53rd Street in 1939, into a modernist building designed by Philip Goodwin and Edward Durell Stone. It's been expanded many times since, most recently in 2004 by the Japanese architect Yoshio Taniguchi and in 2019 by Diller Scofidio and Renfro, the firm behind the High Line. Its collection includes Vincent van Gogh's The Starry Night, Pablo Picasso's Les Demoiselles d'Avignon, Claude Monet's Water Lilies, Andy Warhol's Campbell's Soup Cans, and much more. Its design collection includes everything from a helicopter to the original emoji set.

Now walk east, to East 53rd Street between Fifth and Madison Avenues, and look for a small park with a waterfall. This is Paley Park, a tiny vest-pocket park opened in 1967. It was created by William S. Paley, the founder of CBS, in memory of his father, on the site of the famous Stork Club, a nightclub where celebrities and gossip columnists gathered in the mid-century.

Paley Park is only about forty-two by one hundred feet, but it's considered one of the most perfect small urban spaces in the world. The twenty-foot waterfall at the back drowns out traffic noise. Honey locust trees provide dappled shade. Movable chairs let people sit wherever they like. The urbanist William H. Whyte studied this park and others like it in the 1970s, filming how people used public spaces, and found that small, well-designed spaces with seating, water, food, and trees could attract heavy use. His findings shaped the city's plaza rules and later the redesign of Bryant Park.

CBS's own headquarters, a dark granite tower nicknamed Black Rock, designed by Eero Saarinen and completed in 1965, stands a block west, at Sixth Avenue and 52nd Street.
=== STOP Park Avenue: Lever House & the Seagram Building @ 40.7584,-73.9721
You're on Park Avenue, around 52nd and 53rd Streets. Remember that Park Avenue exists because the railroad tracks going into Grand Central were covered over. In the 1910s and 20s, this stretch was lined with elegant apartment buildings. After World War Two, corporations replaced them with glass towers. Two buildings, across the avenue from each other, started that transformation.

On the west side, at 390 Park Avenue, look for a green glass tower that rises from a low horizontal base lifted on columns. This is Lever House, completed in 1952. It was designed by Gordon Bunshaft of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill for the Lever Brothers company, the British soap maker that produced brands like Lifebuoy and Lux, and is now part of Unilever. The company wanted a building that looked clean, like its products. Lever House was among the first glass curtain-wall office buildings in New York, and it was revolutionary: instead of filling its lot, it left most of the ground open, with a courtyard and a slim tower. Its glass was even cleaned by a gondola that moved along the facade, which was itself a marketing statement.

Across the avenue, at 375 Park Avenue, is the Seagram Building, completed in 1958. It was the headquarters of Joseph E. Seagram and Sons, the Canadian whiskey distiller. The founder's daughter, Phyllis Lambert, a young architecture student, persuaded her father to hire Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, the German modernist master. Mies designed a dark bronze and glass tower set back from the street behind a granite plaza with two reflecting pools. He used bronze I-beams on the exterior, purely for visual effect, to express the steel structure inside. It was enormously expensive, and enormously influential. The Seagram Building became the template for countless corporate towers around the world.

Its plaza inspired a change in the city's zoning law in 1961, which gave developers extra height if they provided public plazas. Not all of those plazas turned out as well as Seagram's.

Inside the Seagram Building, the restaurant space designed by Philip Johnson housed the Four Seasons, where the power lunch is said to have been invented.

Now look south along Park Avenue, toward 47th Street, at the massive new tower at 270 Park Avenue. This is the new global headquarters of JPMorgan Chase, completed in the mid-2020s. To build it, the bank demolished the previous fifty-two-story tower on the site, the former Union Carbide Building, making it the tallest building ever intentionally demolished. JPMorgan Chase's history, as you may have heard on the Financial District tour, includes J. P. Morgan's bank and Aaron Burr's Manhattan Company. Its new tower is a sign that, for the biggest banks, Midtown, not Wall Street, is now the center of gravity.

Also on Park Avenue, at 49th Street, is the Waldorf Astoria, the Art Deco hotel that opened in 1931 after the old one was demolished for the Empire State Building. For decades, it was where presidents stayed in New York.
=== STOP Gilded Age Fifth Avenue @ 40.7627,-73.9738
You're at Fifth Avenue and 57th Street, the most famous luxury shopping intersection in America.

Imagine this corner in the 1890s. On the west side of Fifth Avenue, from 57th to 58th Street, stood the largest private house ever built in New York City, the mansion of Cornelius Vanderbilt the Second, the grandson of the Commodore. It was a French château with well over one hundred rooms, and it took up the whole block front. Vanderbilt family mansions lined Fifth Avenue for blocks to the south. This was the heart of the Gilded Age.

By 1926, the Vanderbilt mansion was demolished. Its site became the department store Bergdorf Goodman, founded by Herman Bergdorf, a tailor, and Edwin Goodman, which moved here in 1928. Bergdorf's is still one of the most exclusive department stores in the world. The mansion's grand iron gates were saved, and today they stand at the entrance to the Conservatory Garden in Central Park.

On the southeast corner of 57th Street is Tiffany and Company's flagship store, which opened here in 1940. Above the entrance, look for the statue of Atlas holding a clock, which was moved here from the company's earlier stores. In the 1961 film Breakfast at Tiffany's, Audrey Hepburn ate a pastry while gazing into these windows. The store underwent a major renovation in the early 2020s.

Walk back a few blocks south, to Fifth Avenue and 52nd Street, and look for an Italian Renaissance mansion of pale stone, home to the Cartier jewelry store. Here's one of the best stories in New York real estate. The mansion was built for Morton Plant, a railroad and steamship magnate. By 1917, the neighborhood was going commercial, and Plant wanted out. Meanwhile, Pierre Cartier, the French jeweler, wanted a Fifth Avenue store. Plant's wife, Maisie, admired a double strand of natural pearls Cartier had. At the time, natural pearls were extraordinarily valuable, before cultured pearls became widespread. So they made a trade: the mansion, plus a small sum of cash, for the pearl necklace. Cartier got its New York flagship, which it still occupies. A few decades later, after cultured pearls had flooded the market, the necklace sold at auction for a small fraction of its former value. The house turned out to be the better deal.

Up and down this stretch, flagship stores for luxury brands occupy some of the most expensive retail space in the world. Upper Fifth Avenue's rents regularly rank among the highest anywhere.
=== STOP The Plaza Hotel & Grand Army Plaza @ 40.7645,-73.9743
You're at Grand Army Plaza, at Fifth Avenue and 59th Street, the southeast corner of Central Park.

The grand, château-style building facing the park is the Plaza Hotel. It opened in 1907, designed by Henry Janeway Hardenbergh, who also designed the Dakota apartment building on the Upper West Side. It was built to be the most luxurious hotel in the world, and the Vanderbilts and other wealthy families took up residence there.

The Plaza has played a starring role in countless stories. Kay Thompson's Eloise books, published starting in 1955, featured a mischievous six-year-old girl who lives at the Plaza; a portrait of Eloise hangs in the hotel. In 1964, the Beatles stayed here on their first visit to America. In 1966, the writer Truman Capote held his Black and White Ball in the hotel's ballroom, a masked party considered by many to be the social event of the century.

The Plaza has a place in financial history too. In September 1985, finance ministers and central bankers from the United States, Japan, West Germany, France, and the United Kingdom met at the Plaza and agreed to coordinate interventions in currency markets to bring down the value of the US dollar, which had risen sharply. The agreement, the Plaza Accord, worked. The dollar fell dramatically over the following two years. Many economists link the resulting rise of the Japanese yen to Japan's asset bubble in the late 1980s and its collapse into a long stagnation.

In the plaza in front of the hotel is the Pulitzer Fountain, with a statue of Pomona, the Roman goddess of abundance, funded by a bequest from the newspaper publisher Joseph Pulitzer. North of it, at the park's corner, is a gleaming gilded statue of General William Tecumseh Sherman on horseback, led by a winged figure of Victory, by Augustus Saint-Gaudens, completed in 1903. It's considered one of the finest equestrian statues in America.

Across Fifth Avenue is the General Motors Building, a white marble tower completed in 1968. In front of it is a glass cube, the entrance to Apple's Fifth Avenue store, opened in 2006. The store is underground, open around the clock, and the cube has become one of the most photographed retail designs in the world.

For decades, the GM Building was also home to the toy store FAO Schwarz, founded in 1862 and famous for the giant floor piano played in the 1988 film Big. The store closed here in 2015 and has since reopened at Rockefeller Center.
=== STOP Carnegie Hall & Billionaires' Row @ 40.7651,-73.9799
End your walk at Seventh Avenue and 57th Street, outside Carnegie Hall.

Carnegie Hall was built by Andrew Carnegie, the Scottish-born steel magnate who was one of the richest men in history, and who later gave away most of his fortune, funding more than two thousand five hundred public libraries around the world. He built the hall at the urging of a young conductor, Walter Damrosch, and his wife Louise, who was a member of the Oratorio Society. It opened in 1891, with the Russian composer Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky conducting on opening night. Its acoustics are considered among the best in the world. Everyone from Gustav Mahler to Benny Goodman, Judy Garland, and the Beatles has performed here.

In the 1950s, Carnegie Hall faced demolition after the New York Philharmonic decided to move to the new Lincoln Center. The violinist Isaac Stern led a campaign to save it, and in 1960, the city bought it. The main auditorium is now named for Stern.

There's an old joke: a tourist stops a musician on 57th Street and asks, How do you get to Carnegie Hall? The musician answers, Practice, practice, practice.

Now look up and around you, along 57th Street. You're on Billionaires' Row, a cluster of super-tall, super-slender luxury residential towers built since the 2010s, mostly along 57th Street at the south edge of Central Park. They include One57, Central Park Tower, the tallest residential building in the world, and, just east of here, 111 West 57th Street, often called the Steinway Tower. It's one of the most slender skyscrapers in the world, with a width-to-height ratio of about one to twenty-four. It incorporates the historic Steinway Hall, built in 1925 as the showroom of Steinway and Sons, the piano company founded by the German immigrant Henry Steinway in 1853, whose pianos are used by most of the world's concert pianists.

These towers were made possible by new engineering techniques and by developers buying up the unused air rights of neighboring buildings, allowing them to build much taller than would otherwise be allowed. Their apartments have sold for tens of millions of dollars, and some for over one hundred million. Critics point out that many are owned by international buyers as investments and sit empty much of the year, and that the towers cast long shadows across Central Park.

It's a fitting place to end: a nineteenth-century concert hall built by a steel baron, a twentieth-century piano showroom, and twenty-first-century towers for the global rich, side by side on the same block. On 57th Street, wealth keeps finding new ways to show itself.
