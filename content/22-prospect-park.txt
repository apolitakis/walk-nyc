title: Prospect Park, Park Slope & Gowanus
area: Brooklyn
blurb: Olmsted and Vaux's favorite park, Brooklyn's triumphal arch, the Long Meadow, the Battle of Brooklyn, brownstone Park Slope, and the Gowanus Canal.
=== OVERVIEW
If you ask Brooklynites which park is better, Central Park or Prospect Park, many will tell you Prospect Park. And they have a strong ally: Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, who designed both, reportedly considered Prospect Park their finer work.

In the 1860s, the City of Brooklyn was booming, and its leaders wanted a great park to rival Manhattan's new Central Park. The driving force was James S. T. Stranahan, a businessman and civic leader who was also a key promoter of the Brooklyn Bridge. An initial plan for the park would have split it in two with a major road. Vaux, who had been consulted, argued for a different site and a unified design, and in 1865, he and Olmsted were hired. Unlike in Manhattan, they didn't have to work around a reservoir or a rigid rectangle. They had a chance to design a park closer to their ideal: a huge, sweeping meadow, a deep forested ravine, and a large lake, each arranged as a distinct experience, flowing into one another.

The park opened in stages starting in 1867. It covers about five hundred twenty-six acres. Around it, the neighborhoods developed quickly. Park Slope, on the hill to the west, filled with grand mansions along Prospect Park West, and with block after block of brownstones on the side streets. The neighborhood declined in the mid-twentieth century, but in the 1960s and 70s, young families, many of them professionals, discovered its brownstones, bought them cheaply, and restored them. They called themselves brownstoners, and they helped launch the brownstone revival movement that transformed many Brooklyn neighborhoods. Today, Park Slope is one of the most desirable and expensive neighborhoods in New York, famous for its strollers, its food co-op, and its progressive politics.

The land here also holds the story of the largest battle of the American Revolution. On August twenty-seventh, 1776, the Battle of Brooklyn was fought across this terrain, along the ridge that now runs through Prospect Park, and down in the lowlands near the Gowanus Creek. The Americans were routed, and only a heroic rear-guard action and a foggy night escape saved Washington's army.

This walk starts at Grand Army Plaza, explores the park, climbs into Park Slope, and ends at the Old Stone House and the Gowanus Canal.
=== STOP Grand Army Plaza & the Arch @ 40.6731,-73.9701
You're at Grand Army Plaza, the great oval plaza at the northern entrance to Prospect Park, where Flatbush Avenue, Eastern Parkway, and Prospect Park West meet.

Olmsted and Vaux designed this plaza as a grand entrance, a kind of buffer between the city and the park, with an oval shape to direct traffic around it. At its center rises the Soldiers' and Sailors' Memorial Arch, completed in 1892, honoring the Union forces in the Civil War. It was designed by John Duncan, the architect of Grant's Tomb, and modeled on Roman triumphal arches. The sculptures were added later. On top is a dramatic bronze quadriga, a chariot pulled by four horses, with a figure of Columbia, representing the United States, by the sculptor Frederick MacMonnies. On the sides are groups representing the Army and the Navy. Inside the arch, look for bronze reliefs of Abraham Lincoln and Ulysses S. Grant on horseback, by Thomas Eakins and William O'Donovan. The arch is sometimes open for climbing, offering a great view.

North of the arch is the Bailey Fountain, with figures representing Wisdom and Felicity and Neptune with tritons. And on the southwest side of the plaza, there's a bust of President John F. Kennedy, the only official memorial to him in New York City.

On the east side of the plaza, look for the Central Library of the Brooklyn Public Library, completed in 1941. Its striking Art Deco facade is shaped to follow the curve of the plaza, like the spine of an open book. Its entrance is framed by tall bronze screens with gilded figures from American literature, including Moby Dick, Tom Sawyer, Rip Van Winkle, and characters from Edgar Allan Poe. Brooklyn's library system is independent from the New York Public Library, a legacy of Brooklyn's days as a separate city.

On Saturdays, the plaza hosts one of the city's largest greenmarkets.

Eastern Parkway, running east from here, was designed by Olmsted and Vaux as the first parkway in the world, a broad, tree-lined boulevard for carriages, with separate lanes for local traffic and walkways. It was meant to connect Prospect Park with the rest of the city and served as the model for parkways everywhere.
=== STOP The Brooklyn Museum & Botanic Garden @ 40.6712,-73.9636
Walk east along Eastern Parkway to the Brooklyn Museum, the grand Beaux-Arts building with a columned portico.

The Brooklyn Museum was designed by McKim, Mead and White, and the first section opened in 1897. It was planned to be the largest art museum in the world, larger even than the Louvre. Brooklyn was then an independent city, and its leaders wanted it to have cultural institutions to rival anything in Manhattan. But in 1898, Brooklyn merged into New York, and the political will and funding for the giant museum faded. Only about one-sixth of the planned building was ever completed. The museum is still one of the largest in the United States.

Look up at the attic of the building, the top of the facade. You'll see a row of statues representing great figures and cultures of the ancient world, created by many sculptors, including Daniel Chester French. Notice that the grand front staircase is missing: it was removed in the 1930s, a decision later regretted. A modern glass entrance pavilion was added in 2004.

Inside, the museum is known for its Egyptian collection, one of the finest in the world, and for its American art, its African art, and its feminist art center, which houses Judy Chicago's famous installation The Dinner Party, a triangular banquet table with place settings for thirty-nine women from history.

Next to the museum is the Brooklyn Botanic Garden, founded in 1910 on the site of a former ash dump. It covers fifty-two acres, with themed gardens, conservatories, and collections. Its Japanese Hill-and-Pond Garden, designed by the Japanese landscape architect Takeo Shiota and completed in 1915, is among the oldest Japanese-inspired gardens in an American public garden. In spring, the garden's cherry trees burst into bloom along the Cherry Esplanade, and the garden holds a festival to celebrate them.

Across the street, the Brooklyn Public Library's Central Library and the museum and garden together form a cultural complex that reflects Brooklyn's ambitions in its days as a great independent city.
=== STOP The Long Meadow @ 40.6684,-73.9711
Walk back into Prospect Park through the entrance at Grand Army Plaza, under the Endale Arch, a dark, low stone tunnel. As you walk through it, the park opens up in front of you, and you see the Long Meadow.

This was exactly what Olmsted and Vaux intended. They used the dark, enclosed arch as a kind of threshold, a compression, so that when you emerged, the vast open meadow would feel even more expansive, like stepping from the city into the countryside. It's a classic technique of landscape design, and it's one of the most dramatic moments in any park in the world.

The Long Meadow stretches almost a mile, about ninety acres, making it one of the longest continuous meadows in any urban park in the United States. Olmsted and Vaux shaped it carefully. Notice how its edges curve, and how groups of trees are planted to hide its full length, so you never see the whole thing at once. It invites you to keep walking to see what's around the next bend.

It's the heart of the park: on a sunny weekend, it's filled with picnics, soccer games, dogs, frisbees, and families. Before nine in the morning and after nine at night, dogs are allowed off leash on the meadow, and it becomes one of the best dog parks in the city.

Just like Central Park's Sheep Meadow, the Long Meadow had sheep. A flock grazed here from the park's early years, and in 1934, when Robert Moses removed the sheep from Central Park, he sent that flock here too. Not long afterward, Prospect Park's sheep were retired as well, and the meadow's grass has been mowed by machine ever since.

Look along the western edge of the meadow, near Prospect Park West, for Litchfield Villa, an Italianate mansion completed in 1857, before the park existed. It was built for Edwin Clark Litchfield, a railroad developer and lawyer who owned much of the land that became Park Slope. His property was taken for the park, but the villa survived, and it now houses the offices of the Prospect Park Alliance, the nonprofit that manages the park.
=== STOP The Battle of Brooklyn & Lefferts House @ 40.6622,-73.9643
Walk to the east side of the park, near Flatbush Avenue and the zoo, close to the carousel and the Lefferts Historic House.

You're in the area where the Battle of Brooklyn was fought, on August twenty-seventh, 1776. Here's what happened. After the British army landed on Staten Island and then in southern Brooklyn, about twenty thousand British and Hessian troops faced about ten thousand Americans defending Brooklyn. The Americans had built fortifications on Brooklyn Heights, and they posted troops on the Heights of Guan, a forested ridge running across Brooklyn, which today passes through Prospect Park. There were four passes through the ridge. One of them, Flatbush Pass, also called Battle Pass, was right here, along what is now the East Drive near the zoo.

The Americans left the easternmost pass, the Jamaica Pass, almost undefended. The British, guided by local loyalists, marched through it overnight and attacked the Americans from behind. Meanwhile, Hessian troops attacked through the passes here. The Americans were caught between the two forces and routed. Many were killed or captured. The survivors fled west, toward the Gowanus Creek and the fortifications on Brooklyn Heights.

Look for the boulder with a plaque near the zoo, marking the site of Battle Pass, and the Dongan Oak, a giant tree that the Americans felled to block the road.

Nearby, look for the Lefferts Historic House, a Dutch colonial farmhouse with a gambrel roof and a long porch. The Lefferts family were Dutch settlers who farmed in Flatbush for generations. Their original house was burned by the Americans during the battle, to keep it from being used by the British. The family rebuilt it in 1783. In 1918, the house was moved into the park from its original site on Flatbush Avenue. It's now a museum about life on a farm in Brooklyn in the 1820s, including the history of the enslaved people who worked on the Lefferts farm, since slavery was common in Dutch Brooklyn.

Nearby is the Prospect Park Carousel, built in 1912 by the master carver Charles Carmel, with horses, a lion, a giraffe, and a deer.
=== STOP The Boathouse, the Lake & the Ravine @ 40.6606,-73.9656
Walk to the Boathouse, the white terracotta building with arched openings on the shore of the Lullwater, a narrow arm of Prospect Lake.

The Boathouse was completed in 1905, designed by the architects Helmle and Huberty in a Beaux-Arts style inspired by the Library of St. Mark's in Venice. It was built to serve boaters on the lake, and it's one of the most photographed spots in the park, reflected in the still water of the Lullwater. In the 1960s, the Parks Department planned to demolish it. A public campaign saved it, and in 1968 it became one of the early buildings designated as a landmark by the city. It's now the Audubon Center, the first urban Audubon center in the country, which offers nature exhibits, programs, and electric boat tours.

The Lullwater flows into Prospect Lake, about sixty acres, Brooklyn's only freshwater lake. In winter in the nineteenth century, the lake was crowded with skaters. Today, people fish, boat, and watch the birds on the lake, including swans, herons, and cormorants.

West of the Boathouse, across a bridge, is the Ravine, the wooded valley at the heart of the park. It's the only forest in Brooklyn, though it's as man-made as the rest of the park. Olmsted and Vaux designed a stream, the Ambergill, which flows through a rocky gorge, with waterfalls and pools, designed to evoke the Adirondack Mountains. When you're in the Ravine, you can't see the city at all, and the sound of water hides the noise. In the 1990s, the Prospect Park Alliance launched a long restoration of the Ravine's woodlands, which had been degraded by erosion and invasive plants.

Look also for the Cleft Ridge Span, a bridge near the lake, built in 1872. It's one of the earliest structures in the United States built of concrete, cast in blocks, with intricate decorative patterns molded into its arch. It was an experiment in a new building material that would later transform architecture.
=== STOP Park Slope Brownstones @ 40.6762,-73.9751
Walk west out of the park and into Park Slope, to Seventh Avenue and Sterling Place.

Park Slope rises up the hill from the Gowanus Canal to the edge of Prospect Park, which is why it's called Park Slope. It was developed mostly from the 1870s to the early 1900s, after the park opened, with rows of brownstones and brick houses for the growing middle class. Along Prospect Park West, wealthy families built mansions, and the area became known as Brooklyn's Gold Coast. Walk along the side streets between Seventh and Eighth Avenues, like Carroll Street, Montgomery Place, and Berkeley Place, to see some of the finest rows of nineteenth-century townhouses in America, in Romanesque, Queen Anne, and Renaissance Revival styles. Montgomery Place, a single block, has a remarkable collection of houses designed by the architect C. P. H. Gilbert.

At the corner of Eighth Avenue and Lincoln Place, look for the Montauk Club, a private club housed in a building modeled on the Ca' d'Oro, a Gothic palace on the Grand Canal in Venice, completed in 1891. Look closely at the facade: the friezes depict scenes from the life of the Montaukett, a Native American people of Long Island, after whom the club is named.

This intersection also has a tragic history. On December sixteenth, 1960, two passenger planes collided in the sky over Staten Island. One crashed there; the other, United Airlines Flight 826, crashed here, at Seventh Avenue and Sterling Place. All the people on both planes were killed, along with several people on the ground, including people in a church and in shops. In total, one hundred thirty-four people died. It was, at the time, the deadliest aviation disaster in history. An eleven-year-old boy on the United flight survived the crash, and he was found in a snowbank, but he died the next day. A memorial to the victims is in Green-Wood Cemetery.

A few blocks away, on Union Street, is the Park Slope Food Coop, founded in 1973, one of the oldest and largest member-owned food cooperatives in the country. Members must work a shift every few weeks in exchange for the right to shop there, at prices well below regular grocery stores.
=== STOP The Old Stone House & the Gowanus Canal @ 40.6731,-73.9846
End your walk downhill, at J. J. Byrne Park, on Fifth Avenue and Third Street, at the Old Stone House, a small two-story stone farmhouse.

The original house was built in 1699 by a Dutch farmer named Klaes Arents Vechte. On August twenty-seventh, 1776, during the Battle of Brooklyn, this house was at the center of one of the most heroic moments of the Revolution. As the American army fled toward the Gowanus Creek and the safety of Brooklyn Heights, a regiment of about four hundred Maryland soldiers, under Lord Stirling, turned to face the British, who had occupied the house. The Marylanders charged the British again and again, holding them off long enough for most of the American army to escape across the creek. Most of the Maryland soldiers were killed or captured. Watching from a nearby hill, George Washington reportedly exclaimed, Good God, what brave fellows I must this day lose. The soldiers are remembered as the Maryland Four Hundred.

The original house was buried in the 1890s, and the building you see is a reconstruction from the 1930s, using some of the original stones. There's also a twist: in the 1880s, the house served as the clubhouse for the baseball team that later became the Brooklyn Dodgers, which played at a ballpark nearby called Washington Park.

Walk a few blocks west to the Gowanus Canal. In the colonial era, Gowanus Creek was a tidal marsh, famous for its oysters. In the 1860s, it was turned into a canal, to serve the growing industrial waterfront. Factories, coal yards, gas plants, chemical works, tanneries, and cement plants lined its banks. It became one of the most polluted waterways in America, filled with industrial waste and raw sewage. Locals nicknamed it Lavender Lake, for its strange color. In 1911, the city built a flushing tunnel to pump in water from Buttermilk Channel, to flush the canal. It helped, but not enough.

In 2010, the canal was designated a federal Superfund site, and a massive cleanup is underway. In 2021, the city rezoned the neighborhood, allowing thousands of new apartments to be built along the canal. It's a remarkable transformation for a waterway that was once a symbol of industrial filth.

From the battlefield to the factories to the brownstones and the condos, this corner of Brooklyn has seen the whole history of the city.
