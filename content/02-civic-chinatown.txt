title: City Hall, Five Points & Chinatown
area: Manhattan
blurb: Newspaper Row, the Cathedral of Commerce, the African Burial Ground, Five Points, and the streets of Chinatown.
=== OVERVIEW
This walk covers a compact stretch of lower Manhattan where the city's government, its press, its poorest slums, and one of its most vibrant immigrant communities all grew up within a few blocks of each other.

In the colonial era, this area sat just north of the settled town. The open land known as the Commons, now City Hall Park, was the town's pasture, gallows ground, and gathering place. Just to the northeast was a deep, spring-fed lake called the Collect Pond, the main source of fresh water for the early city. In the 1700s it was a picnic spot. By the 1800s, tanneries, breweries, and slaughterhouses had turned it into an open sewer, and the city filled it in. The fill was poorly done. The ground settled, became damp and foul, and the people who could afford to leave did. What grew on top of the old pond was Five Points, the most notorious slum in nineteenth century America.

Meanwhile, the area around City Hall became the hub of civic power, and Park Row, facing the park, became Newspaper Row. In the late 1800s, the city's great papers, the Times, the Tribune, the Sun, Joseph Pulitzer's World, all had their headquarters here, competing to build the tallest tower. Their reporters walked to City Hall, the courts, and the police headquarters, and they walked into Five Points to find the stories that sold papers. The proximity of power and poverty in this neighborhood is the key to understanding it.

Just to the east, Chinatown took root in the late nineteenth century, on Mott, Pell, and Doyers Streets. The community grew under severe pressure: the federal Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred most Chinese immigration for six decades. For a long time, Chinatown was a small enclave of mostly men, organized around family associations, merchant guilds, and rival societies called tongs. After immigration law changed in 1965, the neighborhood expanded dramatically, absorbing much of what had been Little Italy and parts of the Lower East Side. Today it is one of the largest Chinese communities outside Asia.

On this walk you'll see City Hall, the Woolworth Building, the gateway to the Brooklyn Bridge, the African Burial Ground, the site of Five Points, and the twisting streets of old Chinatown, ending beneath the grand arch of the Manhattan Bridge.
=== STOP City Hall Park & Newspaper Row @ 40.7127,-74.0060
You're in City Hall Park, the old Commons. In the colonial era, this was the open field at the edge of town where people grazed cattle, drilled militia, protested, and hanged criminals. On July ninth, 1776, George Washington's troops assembled near here to hear the Declaration of Independence read aloud. The crowd then went down Broadway and tore down the king's statue at Bowling Green.

Look at City Hall itself, the elegant white building with the cupola. It was completed in 1812, designed by Joseph François Mangin, a French émigré, and John McComb Junior. It blends French Renaissance and American Federal styles, and it is still the working seat of city government, among the oldest city halls in the country still used for its original purpose. A popular story says that the front and sides were clad in fine marble, but the back was faced in cheaper brownstone, because the city fathers couldn't imagine anyone important ever living north of it. The story may be partly myth, but it captures how small New York still was in 1812.

In April 1865, Abraham Lincoln's body lay in state in the rotunda here, and tens of thousands of New Yorkers filed past.

Behind City Hall is the Tweed Courthouse. It is a monument to corruption. Construction began in 1861 under the control of William Magear Tweed, known as Boss Tweed, the leader of the Tammany Hall political machine. The project took about twenty years, and its cost ballooned to many times the original budget, as contractors billed the city for absurd amounts. One plasterer was paid a fortune for supposedly repairing the work he had just installed. The scandal, exposed by the New York Times and by Thomas Nast's cartoons in Harper's Weekly, eventually sent Tweed to prison, where he died.

Now look east, across the park, toward Park Row. That street was Newspaper Row. The New York Times, the Tribune, the Sun, and the World all stood along here. The competition between Joseph Pulitzer's World and William Randolph Hearst's Journal in the 1890s gave birth to the term yellow journalism. Pulitzer's World Building had a gilded dome that, for a while, made it one of the tallest buildings in the city. It was demolished in the 1950s to make way for Brooklyn Bridge ramps. A statue of Benjamin Franklin, holding a copy of his Pennsylvania Gazette, stands in the small plaza called Printing House Square as a reminder of the area's past.

One surviving tower from that era is 41 Park Row, the old New York Times building from the 1880s, now part of Pace University. And the tall, slender building with the two small cupolas, the Park Row Building at 15 Park Row, was the tallest office building in the world when it opened in 1899.
=== STOP The Woolworth Building @ 40.7124,-74.0082
Look up at the white terracotta tower on Broadway across from City Hall Park. This is the Woolworth Building, completed in 1913 and designed by Cass Gilbert, the same architect behind the Custom House at Bowling Green. At about seven hundred ninety two feet, it was the tallest building in the world until 1930.

Its owner, Frank Winfield Woolworth, built his fortune on a simple idea: the five-and-dime store. In 1879 he opened a store where everything cost five cents, and later ten cents. Prices were fixed and clearly marked, goods were displayed openly so customers could handle them, and volume made up for thin margins. By the time this building went up, F. W. Woolworth and Company had hundreds of stores. Woolworth reportedly paid for the tower in cash, around thirteen and a half million dollars. He liked to say it was built with nickels and dimes.

The Gothic detail was deliberate. Gilbert wrapped a modern steel skeleton in the language of medieval cathedrals: pointed arches, gargoyles, pinnacles, and tracery. At the opening ceremony, a clergyman called it the Cathedral of Commerce, and the nickname stuck. On the night of April twenty fourth, 1913, President Woodrow Wilson pressed a button in Washington, and the building's eighty thousand light bulbs switched on at once.

If you can get a look at the lobby, which is sometimes accessible by guided tour, it's one of the most lavish in the world, with a vaulted ceiling covered in glass mosaics. Hidden in the corners are small carved caricatures: one shows Frank Woolworth counting coins, another shows Cass Gilbert holding a model of the building, and another shows the building's banker. Gothic cathedrals often included small portraits of their builders, and Gilbert was playing on that tradition.

The Woolworth company itself is a lesson in corporate evolution. The five-and-dime stores faded in the late twentieth century, and the last American Woolworth stores closed in 1997. But one of the company's side ventures, a chain of athletic shoe stores, thrived. The corporation eventually renamed itself after it: Foot Locker. The company sold this building in 1998. Today, the upper floors have been turned into luxury apartments, including a penthouse in the pinnacle itself.
=== STOP The Municipal Building & Brooklyn Bridge Gateway @ 40.7129,-74.0040
In front of you, straddling Chambers Street, is the Manhattan Municipal Building, now named for David N. Dinkins, the city's first Black mayor. Completed in 1914 and designed by the firm McKim, Mead and White, it was one of the first skyscrapers built by the city for its own offices, created after the five boroughs were consolidated into Greater New York in 1898 and the city's bureaucracy exploded in size.

Notice that a street literally runs through the bottom of the building, under a grand vaulted arch, and that a subway station is built into its base. This was a new idea: a skyscraper designed as part of the city's transit infrastructure. At the very top stands a gilded copper statue called Civic Fame, holding a crown with five turrets, representing the five boroughs. It is one of the largest statues in the city.

The building was influential. Its tiered, wedding-cake silhouette is often said to have inspired the so-called Seven Sisters skyscrapers built in Moscow under Stalin, as well as other civic towers in the United States. This is also where many New Yorkers come to get married. The City Clerk's office, where civil ceremonies happen, has been in a nearby building on Worth Street since 2009, but for decades the weddings happened here.

To your right is the pedestrian entrance to the Brooklyn Bridge. When it opened in 1883, it was the longest suspension bridge in the world, and the first to use steel wire for its cables. It joined two separate cities, New York and Brooklyn, fifteen years before they merged. We'll cover the bridge's story in detail on the Brooklyn Bridge and DUMBO tour, but if you have time, walking even halfway across is one of the essential New York experiences. The best view of the Manhattan skyline, arguably, is from the bridge's first tower.

Look also for the Surrogate's Court building nearby, on Chambers Street, a Beaux-Arts building with sculptures representing the history of New York along its roofline. Inside, its lobby is modeled on the grand Paris Opera house. It holds the city's records of wills, adoptions, and estates.
=== STOP African Burial Ground @ 40.7146,-74.0045
You are standing at one of the most significant archaeological sites in the United States.

In 1991, the federal government began construction on a new office tower at 290 Broadway. Historic maps had shown that a burial ground for Africans had existed in this area in the colonial period, but most people assumed it had been destroyed long ago by two centuries of building. It hadn't. Construction workers began finding human remains, and archaeologists ultimately uncovered the remains of about four hundred nineteen people, buried here roughly between the 1690s and 1794. Scholars estimate the full burial ground, which covered several city blocks, may have held as many as fifteen thousand people.

To understand why this place exists, you need to know something that surprises many visitors: colonial New York was a slave city. The Dutch West India Company brought enslaved Africans to New Amsterdam as early as the 1620s, and under English rule slavery expanded. By the mid-1700s, enslaved people made up a significant share of the city's population, larger than in many Southern cities of the time. They built roads, fortifications, docks, and the original wall that gave Wall Street its name. Africans were barred from being buried in churchyards inside the city, so they buried their dead here, outside the settlement, beyond the Collect Pond.

Tensions ran high. In 1712, a group of enslaved people set fire to a building and attacked whites who came to put it out. In 1741, a series of fires led to hysteria over an alleged conspiracy, and dozens of people, mostly enslaved Africans, were executed, some burned at the stake. New York State passed gradual emancipation laws starting in 1799, and slavery wasn't fully abolished in New York until 1827.

The excavated remains were studied by researchers at Howard University, revealing much about the lives of the people buried here: evidence of hard labor, malnutrition, and early death, but also burial customs that connected them to West and Central Africa. Some coffins bore symbols, and one was decorated with a heart-shaped design that has been interpreted as an Akan symbol called Sankofa, meaning roughly to go back and retrieve what was lost.

The remains were reinterred here in 2003, and the site was designated a national monument in 2006. The outdoor memorial, designed by the architect Rodney Leon, includes a spiral ramp, a map of the Atlantic world, and an ancestral chamber. There's also a visitor center inside the federal building, free to the public.
=== STOP Foley Square & Five Points @ 40.7151,-74.0016
You are now at Foley Square, surrounded by courthouses. Look around: the hexagonal granite building with the massive Corinthian portico is the New York County Supreme Court, completed in 1927. It's been the backdrop for countless film and television scenes, especially courtroom dramas. Next to it, the tall tower with the gilded pyramid top is the Thurgood Marshall United States Courthouse, designed by Cass Gilbert and completed in 1936. These are monuments to order and law.

The irony is where they stand. Right around here, roughly to your northeast, was the Collect Pond, and once the pond was filled, a few blocks from here was Five Points, the most notorious slum in America. The name came from the five corners created by the intersection of three streets, Anthony, Orange, and Cross. Those streets have since been renamed or erased, but the intersection was roughly where Worth and Baxter Streets meet, near what is now Columbus Park, just a short walk east of here.

By the 1830s and 40s, Five Points was packed with the poorest immigrants in the city, mostly Irish refugees fleeing famine, along with free Black New Yorkers, Germans, and later Italians and Jews. Crowding was extreme. Tenements and rear buildings were stuffed into every lot. Disease, especially cholera, swept through regularly. The neighborhood also became a magnet for slumming tourists and moralizing reformers. Charles Dickens visited in 1842 and described it in his book American Notes, with horror and fascination.

But Five Points wasn't only misery. It was a place where cultures mixed in ways unthinkable elsewhere. In dance halls like Almack's, Irish jigs and African American dance traditions collided. The great Black dancer William Henry Lane, known as Master Juba, competed against Irish dancers here, and historians often trace the roots of tap dance to that cultural collision.

Five Points was also the territory of political gangs, groups with names like the Dead Rabbits, the Bowery Boys, and the Plug Uglies, whose rivalries were romanticized in Herbert Asbury's 1928 book The Gangs of New York.

A few blocks north, along Mulberry Street, was a stretch called Mulberry Bend, one of the worst blocks of all. The photographer and reporter Jacob Riis documented it in his 1890 book How the Other Half Lives, among the first works of photojournalism to use flash photography to reveal the interiors of tenements. Riis's images shocked middle class readers and helped push housing reform. The city eventually demolished Mulberry Bend, and in its place created a park, now Columbus Park, which opened in the 1890s. Today, Columbus Park is one of the most lively spaces in Chinatown, filled with people playing cards, mahjong, and Chinese chess, practicing tai chi, and making music.
=== STOP Mott, Pell & Doyers Streets @ 40.7144,-73.9982
You're now in the heart of old Chinatown, the triangle formed by Mott, Pell, and Doyers Streets.

Chinese immigrants began settling here in significant numbers in the 1870s and 1880s, many coming east after working on the railroads and in the mines of the American West, where violent anti-Chinese movements had made life dangerous. Then, in 1882, Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act, the first major federal law to bar immigration by a specific national group. It wasn't repealed until 1943. Because of exclusion and related laws, the community remained small and overwhelmingly male for decades. Most men worked in laundries, restaurants, and small shops, often sending money home to families in southern China, especially from the Guangdong province region around Taishan.

To navigate a hostile country, residents relied on family associations, district associations, and merchant organizations. The most prominent umbrella group was the Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association, which acted almost like a local government. There were also tongs, secret fraternal societies like the On Leong and Hip Sing, which could function as mutual aid societies but also controlled gambling and other rackets, and fought violent wars with each other in the early twentieth century.

Walk down Doyers Street, the short street with the sharp bend in it. That curve gave it the nickname the Bloody Angle. During the tong wars of the early 1900s, the curve made it a notorious ambush spot, because you couldn't see who was waiting around the corner. Today it's quiet, lined with restaurants and barbershops. One of them, Nom Wah Tea Parlor, has been serving dim sum and pastries on this street since 1920, making it one of the oldest restaurants in Chinatown.

On Mott Street, look for the Church of the Transfiguration, at the corner of Mott and Mosco Streets. The building dates to 1801 and was originally built as an Episcopal church. It later became a Catholic church that served, in succession, Irish immigrants, then Italians, then Chinese. In that sense, this one building tells the whole immigrant history of the neighborhood.

After the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 eliminated national-origin quotas, Chinatown grew rapidly, with newcomers from Hong Kong, Taiwan, Guangdong, and later Fujian province. The garment industry became the neighborhood's economic engine through the 1980s and 1990s, with hundreds of small factories employing tens of thousands of women. That industry has largely faded, but the neighborhood's restaurants, groceries, and markets remain.

At Chatham Square, where many streets converge, look for the Kimlau Memorial Arch, honoring Chinese Americans who died in World War Two, and the statue of Lin Zexu, the Qing dynasty official famous for trying to stop the British opium trade in the 1830s.
=== STOP Canal Street & the Manhattan Bridge Arch @ 40.7167,-73.9959
End your walk at the corner of Canal Street and the Bowery, at the entrance to the Manhattan Bridge.

Canal Street really was a canal, or at least a drainage ditch. Around 1805, the city dug a channel to drain the polluted Collect Pond westward to the Hudson River. It quickly became a stinking, stagnant mess, and by the 1820s it was covered over and turned into a broad street. Today, Canal Street is one of Manhattan's busiest and most chaotic commercial streets, long famous for its street vendors, jewelry shops, and, notoriously, counterfeit handbags and watches sold out of storefront back rooms.

The Bowery, the street running north from here, is one of the oldest roads in Manhattan, following the path of a Lenape trail. Its name comes from the Dutch word bouwerij, meaning farm. Peter Stuyvesant, the last Dutch director of New Amsterdam, had his farm along this road. We'll talk more about the Bowery on the Lower East Side and East Village tours.

Now look at the monumental arch and the curving colonnades in front of you. This is the Manhattan entrance to the Manhattan Bridge, which opened in 1909. The arch and colonnade were designed by the firm Carrère and Hastings, which also designed the New York Public Library. They were completed in 1915 as part of the City Beautiful movement, which held that grand public architecture could ennoble a city and its citizens. The arch was modeled on the Porte Saint-Denis in Paris, and the curving colonnades were inspired by Bernini's colonnade at St. Peter's Square in Rome. The sculptures on the arch, by Carl Augustus Heber, depict Native American and pioneer themes. The point was to give working-class commuters crossing to Brooklyn a gateway as grand as anything in Europe.

The Manhattan Bridge itself was a technical milestone. It was among the first suspension bridges designed using deflection theory, a mathematical method that allowed for a lighter, more flexible deck. That theory later influenced bridges like the Golden Gate. It also carries subway trains, which is why you may hear a rumble overhead.

If you have energy left, you can walk across the bridge's pedestrian path, which offers great views of the Brooklyn Bridge, and lands in DUMBO, where another tour in this app begins.
