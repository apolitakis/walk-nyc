title: East Village
area: Manhattan
blurb: Peter Cooper and Lincoln, CBGB and punk, Little Germany's tragedy, the Yiddish Rialto, and Loisaida's gardens.
=== OVERVIEW
For a long time, the East Village wasn't called the East Village at all. It was simply the northern part of the Lower East Side. The name was invented in the 1960s, partly by real estate agents hoping to attach some of Greenwich Village's bohemian glamour to cheaper tenement apartments to the east. The marketing worked, perhaps too well: within a few years, the neighborhood had become the center of the hippie counterculture, and later of punk rock.

But the history goes much deeper. In the seventeenth century, this was the farm, or bouwerij, of Peter Stuyvesant, the last Dutch director-general of New Amsterdam. His family held the land for generations, and a few traces of their estate survive in diagonal streets and an old church.

In the nineteenth century, the area became the heart of Kleindeutschland, Little Germany, one of the largest German-speaking communities in the world outside of Berlin and Vienna. It was a neighborhood of beer halls, bakeries, singing societies, gymnastics clubs called Turnvereine, and Lutheran and Catholic churches. It was also a hotbed of labor organizing and socialist politics.

As the Germans moved out in the early twentieth century, Eastern European Jews, Ukrainians, Poles, and Italians moved in. Second Avenue became the Yiddish Rialto, the center of a thriving Yiddish theater scene. After World War Two, Ukrainian refugees built a community around Seventh Street, and Puerto Rican families settled in the area east of Avenue A, known as Alphabet City, or in Spanglish, Loisaida.

Then came the artists and the counterculture. In the 1950s and 60s, Beat poets, jazz musicians, and abstract painters moved into cheap apartments here. In the 1960s, St. Mark's Place became the East Coast counterpart to San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury. In the 1970s, the CBGB club on the Bowery launched punk rock. In the 1980s, the neighborhood was the center of a vibrant art and music scene, but it also suffered from drugs, abandoned buildings, arson, and the AIDS crisis. In the 1990s and 2000s, it gentrified rapidly.

As you walk, notice the layers: German names carved on old buildings, Ukrainian churches, Yiddish theater history, Puerto Rican murals and community gardens, punk landmarks, and new luxury buildings, all mixed together.
=== STOP Cooper Union & Astor Place @ 40.7292,-73.9907
You're at Astor Place, the busy intersection where Lafayette Street, Fourth Avenue, the Bowery, and Eighth Street converge.

Start with the large brownstone building on the triangular block to the south. This is the Foundation Building of the Cooper Union, completed in 1859. It was founded by Peter Cooper, one of the most remarkable figures in American industrial history. Cooper had very little formal schooling, but he became a successful inventor and industrialist. He built the Tom Thumb, one of the first American-built steam locomotives, in 1830. He ran glue factories and ironworks, made rolled iron beams used in early fireproof buildings, and was a major backer of the first transatlantic telegraph cable. He even patented a gelatin dessert, decades before Jell-O.

Cooper wanted to give working people the education he never had. He founded Cooper Union to provide free education in science, engineering, and art, open to men and women, regardless of race or religion, which was radical for the time. For most of its history, tuition was free for all students.

Fun detail: Cooper was so convinced that the passenger elevator would soon be practical that he had a round shaft built into the Foundation Building, before a working elevator design existed. He assumed elevators would be cylindrical. Today, an elevator runs in that shaft.

The building's Great Hall, in the basement, is one of the most historically important rooms in America. On February twenty-seventh, 1860, a little-known Illinois lawyer named Abraham Lincoln gave a speech there arguing against the expansion of slavery into the western territories. The speech was carefully reasoned and widely reprinted, and it helped make Lincoln a serious contender for the Republican nomination for president. A photograph taken by Mathew Brady the same day became one of his most famous portraits. Lincoln later reportedly said that Brady and the Cooper Union speech made him president. The Great Hall has hosted presidents, suffragists, and civil rights leaders ever since.

Now look at the big black cube balanced on one corner in the middle of the plaza. That's Alamo, a sculpture by Tony Rosenthal, installed in 1967. If you push it, it spins.

The plaza's name honors John Jacob Astor, a German immigrant who made a fortune in the fur trade and then invested it in Manhattan real estate, becoming the richest man in America. Near here stood the Astor Opera House, site of the Astor Place Riot of 1849. The riot began as a rivalry between two actors, the American Edwin Forrest and the Englishman William Charles Macready, but it became a violent clash between working-class nativists and the upper-class patrons of the opera house. Militia troops fired into the crowd, killing over twenty people.

Across Lafayette Street, the large Italianate brownstone building is the Public Theater. It was originally the Astor Library, founded with a bequest from John Jacob Astor and one of the first free public libraries in the city. Its collection later became part of the New York Public Library. In the 1960s, the producer Joseph Papp saved the building from demolition and turned it into the Public Theater. Papp also founded Shakespeare in the Park. The Public premiered the musical Hair in 1967, A Chorus Line in 1975, and Hamilton in 2015, all of which went on to Broadway.
=== STOP CBGB & the Merchant's House @ 40.7253,-73.9920
You're on the Bowery, at 315 Bowery, near Bleecker Street. In 1973, a club owner named Hilly Kristal opened a bar here called CBGB, which stood for Country, Bluegrass, and Blues, the kind of music he planned to book. That's not what happened.

In the 1970s, the Bowery was still skid row, lined with flophouses, and CBGB was a narrow, dingy room with a long bar and a tiny stage. Kristal had a policy that bands had to play their own original music. A group of young musicians began playing there, and between about 1974 and 1977, CBGB became the birthplace of American punk rock and new wave. The Ramones played their early shows here, short, fast, loud songs that stripped rock and roll to its basics. So did Patti Smith, Television, Blondie, and the Talking Heads. These bands sounded nothing alike, but they shared a stripped-down, do-it-yourself aesthetic that changed popular music.

CBGB's bathrooms were legendarily filthy, and its awning, with its white letters on a dark background, became an iconic image, endlessly reproduced on T-shirts. The club closed in 2006 after a rent dispute, and the space later became a high-end clothing store, which preserved some of the graffiti-covered walls.

Now walk a couple of blocks west to East Fourth Street, between the Bowery and Lafayette Street, and look for a red brick house with a Greek Revival doorway at number 29. This is the Merchant's House Museum, and it's a time capsule from an entirely different world.

Built in 1832, it was bought in 1835 by Seabury Tredwell, a wealthy hardware merchant. At that time, this street was one of the most fashionable addresses in the city. The Tredwell family lived here for almost a century. The youngest daughter, Gertrude Tredwell, was born in the house and lived there until she died in 1933. As the neighborhood changed around her, she kept the house and furnishings almost exactly as they had been. A relative turned it into a museum in 1936.

It's one of very few nineteenth-century family homes in New York preserved with original furniture, clothing, and belongings intact. It's also said to be among the most haunted houses in Manhattan: staff and visitors have reported sightings of Gertrude Tredwell. The museum offers candlelit ghost tours around Halloween. It's a reminder that before the Bowery was skid row, and before it was punk, it was the edge of an elegant residential neighborhood.
=== STOP McSorley's & Little Ukraine @ 40.7286,-73.9896
You're on East Seventh Street, between Second and Third Avenues. Look for McSorley's Old Ale House, at number 15, with its sawdust floor and dark wood interior. McSorley's claims to date from 1854, which would make it one of the oldest continuously operating bars in New York, though some historians think it opened a bit later. Its motto is: Be good or be gone.

McSorley's serves only two kinds of beer, light and dark, and you order them two mugs at a time. The walls are covered with a dense layer of old photographs, newspaper clippings, and memorabilia, much of it untouched for over a century. Above the bar hang wishbones, said to have been left by young men going off to fight in World War One, intended to be collected when they returned. The ones still hanging belonged to those who didn't come back.

For most of its history, McSorley's banned women. It finally admitted them in 1970, after two lawyers from the National Organization for Women sued and won, and New York City passed a law banning sex discrimination in public places. Even then, it didn't have a women's restroom until 1986.

Now look east along Seventh Street, toward Second Avenue. This stretch is the heart of Little Ukraine. Ukrainians settled here starting in the late nineteenth century, and a larger wave arrived after World War Two, many of them people displaced by the war and fleeing Soviet rule. Look for St. George Ukrainian Catholic Church, with its large dome, on Seventh Street. Every year, its parish holds a Ukrainian festival.

At the corner of Second Avenue and Ninth Street, look for Veselka, a Ukrainian restaurant open since 1954, famous for its pierogi, borscht, and blintzes, and open nearly around the clock. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Veselka became a gathering place for the community and raised money for relief efforts. The Ukrainian Museum, on East Sixth Street, holds a fine collection of folk art, including painted Easter eggs called pysanky.

Also on Seventh Street, look for the old building of the Ukrainian National Home, and the occasional blue and yellow flag in windows. The community has been shrinking for decades, but it remains visible here.
=== STOP St. Mark's Place @ 40.7288,-73.9876
You're on St. Mark's Place, the stretch of Eighth Street between Third Avenue and Avenue A. It's probably the most famous street in the East Village.

It started out respectable. In the 1830s, the street was lined with fine Federal-style houses. Look for number 4 St. Mark's Place, the Hamilton-Holly House, built in 1831. Colonel Alexander Hamilton Junior, the son of the Alexander Hamilton buried at Trinity Church, lived there with his sister Eliza for a time.

By the late nineteenth century, the street was part of Little Germany, lined with German clubs and halls. At 19 to 25 St. Mark's Place stood the Arlington Hall, and later a Polish social hall called the Polski Dom Narodowy. In 1966, Andy Warhol rented the space for his multimedia show, the Exploding Plastic Inevitable, featuring the Velvet Underground and Nico, with light shows, film projections, and dancing. The building later became a nightclub called the Electric Circus, one of the first psychedelic discotheques.

In the late 1960s, St. Mark's Place was the East Coast capital of the hippie counterculture, packed with head shops, poster stores, and young runaways. In the 1970s and 80s it became the main drag of punk. The clothing store Trash and Vaudeville, which outfitted punk and rock musicians for decades, was a fixture of the street. Its owners have said that the Ramones, Debbie Harry, and many others shopped there.

Near Avenue A, look for the pair of tenement buildings at 96 and 98 St. Mark's Place. Their facade appears on the cover of Led Zeppelin's 1975 album Physical Graffiti, with the windows cut out on the album cover. The Rolling Stones also filmed their 1981 music video for Waiting on a Friend in front of these buildings.

In recent decades, St. Mark's Place became known for something else: Japanese food. Starting in the 1980s, a cluster of Japanese restaurants, izakayas, and shops opened on and around the street, giving the area the nickname Little Tokyo.

The famous Gem Spa newsstand, at the corner of St. Mark's Place and Second Avenue, sold what many considered the best egg cream in the city for decades. An egg cream, by the way, contains neither egg nor cream: it's chocolate syrup, milk, and seltzer. Gem Spa closed in 2020, another casualty of the neighborhood's changing economics.
=== STOP St. Mark's Church in-the-Bowery @ 40.7301,-73.9867
At the corner of Second Avenue and East 10th Street, set back from the street in a small churchyard, stands St. Mark's Church in-the-Bowery. It is one of the oldest churches in New York.

This land was part of Peter Stuyvesant's farm. Stuyvesant, the peg-legged director-general of New Amsterdam who surrendered the colony to the English in 1664, built a family chapel here in the 1660s. He was buried in a vault beneath that chapel in 1672. The current church was built on the same site in 1799, making it among the oldest church buildings in Manhattan, second only to St. Paul's Chapel. The steeple was added in the 1820s, and the cast iron portico in the 1850s. Stuyvesant's remains are still in the vault beneath the church, and there's a bust of him in the churchyard. Some say his ghost walks the grounds, tapping his wooden leg.

Notice that the church doesn't line up with the street grid. It's oriented to Stuyvesant Street, a diagonal street just to the west, which follows an old road through the Stuyvesant farm. Stuyvesant Street is one of the only streets in Manhattan that runs true east-west, as opposed to the grid, which is tilted about twenty nine degrees. It's lined with historic houses, including the Stuyvesant-Fish House, built in 1804, and the charming Renwick Triangle, a group of townhouses designed in the 1860s.

In the twentieth century, St. Mark's became one of the most artistically adventurous churches in the city. In the 1920s, its rector hosted dancers, poets, and the architect Frank Lloyd Wright, who designed a tower for the property that was never built. Since 1966, the church has been home to the Poetry Project, where poets like Allen Ginsberg, Patti Smith, and many others have read their work. It also hosts experimental dance and theater.

In 1978, the church was badly damaged by fire. It was restored with the help of neighborhood volunteers, many of them young people trained in construction skills as part of the restoration. A reminder that in this neighborhood, even a two-hundred-year-old church is part of the creative scene.
=== STOP The Yiddish Rialto @ 40.7316,-73.9867
Walk north along Second Avenue to East 12th Street. In the early twentieth century, the stretch of Second Avenue from Houston Street to about 14th Street was the Yiddish Rialto, the center of Yiddish theater in America and, arguably, in the world.

At its height, in the 1910s and 1920s, there were more than a dozen Yiddish theaters in the area, presenting everything from melodramas and musical comedies to serious adaptations of Shakespeare and Ibsen in Yiddish. Audiences of immigrant workers packed the theaters, cheered and booed, and treated stars like royalty. Some great performers began here. The actor Paul Muni started in Yiddish theater before becoming a Hollywood star. Molly Picon became a beloved comedic star. Boris Thomashefsky and Jacob Adler were among the great actor-managers of the era. Jacob Adler's daughter, Stella Adler, later became one of the most influential acting teachers in America, teaching Marlon Brando and many others.

Look at the theater at the corner of Second Avenue and 12th Street. It's now a movie theater, the Village East by Angelika, but it was built in 1926 as the Yiddish Art Theatre, commissioned by the businessman Louis Jaffe for the actor-director Maurice Schwartz. Its interior features an extravagant Moorish-style ceiling with a giant Star of David. If you go in to see a movie, look up.

A few blocks south, at the corner of Second Avenue and 10th Street, look down at the sidewalk for small stars with names on them. This is the Yiddish Walk of Fame, honoring stars of the Yiddish stage. For decades it was in front of the Second Avenue Deli, a famous kosher deli that operated here from 1954 until 2006, before moving uptown.

Yiddish theater declined as immigration was cut off after 1924, as the children of immigrants assimilated and moved away, and as movies and radio took over. The Holocaust destroyed the European Yiddish-speaking world that sustained it. But its influence on American entertainment is enormous: many of the comedians, actors, songwriters, and producers who shaped Broadway, Hollywood, and television came out of this world.
=== STOP Tompkins Square Park & the General Slocum @ 40.7265,-73.9818
You're in Tompkins Square Park, between Avenues A and B and 7th and 10th Streets. It has been the center stage of the neighborhood's dramas for over a century and a half.

In the nineteenth century, the park was the heart of Little Germany. It was also a gathering place for protests. In January 1874, in the depths of an economic depression, thousands of unemployed workers gathered here to demand public works jobs. Police charged the crowd on horseback, beating demonstrators indiscriminately. The Tompkins Square Riot became a landmark event in American labor history. One young man who was there, a cigar maker named Samuel Gompers, later founded the American Federation of Labor. He said the experience taught him the importance of practical, disciplined organizing.

Now look for a small marble fountain near the north side of the park, showing a boy and a girl looking out to sea. This is the General Slocum memorial, and it marks one of the worst disasters in New York history.

On June fifteenth, 1904, the members of St. Mark's Evangelical Lutheran Church, a German congregation on East Sixth Street, chartered a paddle-wheel steamboat called the General Slocum for their annual Sunday school picnic. More than thirteen hundred people boarded, most of them women and children, since the men were at work. As the boat steamed up the East River, a fire broke out. The crew was poorly trained, the life preservers were rotten and useless, and the fire hoses were rotten too. The captain kept steaming forward instead of beaching the boat immediately. More than a thousand people died, burned or drowned, within sight of shore.

It was the deadliest single-day disaster in New York City's history until September eleventh, 2001. The tragedy devastated Little Germany. So many families lost wives and children that many survivors couldn't bear to stay. The German community, which had already begun migrating uptown, largely moved away to Yorkville on the Upper East Side.

The park has other stories. Under one of the large elms, look for a plaque. In 1966, the Indian spiritual teacher A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada held a public chanting session under this tree, and this is often considered the founding moment of the Hare Krishna movement in America. The jazz saxophonist Charlie Parker lived in a townhouse facing the park on Avenue B in the early 1950s, and a jazz festival is held here in his honor every summer.

In 1988, the park was the scene of another riot, when police tried to enforce a curfew to clear out homeless encampments, and clashed violently with protesters. The park was closed for a renovation in 1991 and reopened in 1992. It's now a lively, much-loved neighborhood park, with a famous dog run, one of the first in the city.
=== STOP Loisaida & the Nuyorican Poets Cafe @ 40.7222,-73.9819
End your walk in Alphabet City, near Avenue C and East Third Street. Look up at the street signs: on Avenue C, you'll see a second name, Loisaida Avenue. Loisaida is a Spanglish pronunciation of Lower East Side, popularized by Puerto Rican residents and artists in the 1970s.

Puerto Ricans began arriving in New York in large numbers after World War Two, many in the so-called Great Migration of the 1950s, when cheap airfares made the journey possible. They came for factory jobs and settled in East Harlem, the South Bronx, Brooklyn, and here. By the 1970s, much of Alphabet City was Puerto Rican.

Those were brutal years. The city was nearly bankrupt, landlords abandoned buildings or set them on fire for insurance money, and the city took ownership of thousands of abandoned properties. Drugs and crime were rampant. But residents fought back. Community activists organized to rehabilitate buildings through sweat equity, doing the work themselves in exchange for ownership. And they turned rubble-strewn lots into community gardens.

The community garden movement has deep roots here. In 1973, an artist named Liz Christy and a group called the Green Guerillas began throwing seed bombs, balloons filled with seeds and fertilizer, over fences into vacant lots. They planted a garden at the corner of the Bowery and Houston Street, now the Liz Christy Community Garden, considered the first community garden in New York City. Dozens of others followed in Alphabet City. Look for gardens like La Plaza Cultural, at Avenue C and East 9th Street. In the late 1990s, Mayor Rudy Giuliani tried to auction off many garden lots for development, provoking huge protests. Many gardens were saved.

At 236 East Third Street, between Avenues B and C, is the Nuyorican Poets Cafe. Founded in 1973 by the poet and professor Miguel Algarín, along with the playwright Miguel Piñero and others, it began as an informal gathering in Algarín's living room. The term Nuyorican refers to New Yorkers of Puerto Rican descent, and the café became a home for their poetry, music, and theater. In the 1990s, it became famous for its poetry slams, competitive performance poetry that helped launch the spoken word movement nationally. Piñero's play Short Eyes, written about his experiences in prison, was a major success and was nominated for Tony Awards.

In the 1990s, the musical Rent was set in this neighborhood, in the world of artists, squatters, and people living with HIV. Today, Alphabet City has gentrified dramatically, but the murals, gardens, and casitas, small wooden houses built in community gardens in the style of rural Puerto Rico, preserve the memory of Loisaida.
