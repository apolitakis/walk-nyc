=== TOUR
id: chelsea
title: Meatpacking, the High Line & Chelsea
area: Manhattan
blurb: Slaughterhouses to fashion, the Oreo's birthplace, Google's freight-terminal offices, the Titanic's pier, and Hudson Yards.
=== OVERVIEW
The far west side of lower Manhattan, from the Meatpacking District up through Chelsea to Hudson Yards, was for most of its history the city's back of house. This was where the goods came in.

Along the Hudson River, a continuous wall of piers handled ocean liners and freighters. Behind them ran the freight railroad tracks of the New York Central, which carried meat, milk, produce, and manufactured goods into the city. At first, the trains ran at street level down Tenth and Eleventh Avenues, so dangerous to pedestrians that Tenth Avenue was nicknamed Death Avenue, and the railroad employed men on horseback called West Side Cowboys to ride ahead of the trains waving red flags. In the 1930s the tracks were lifted onto an elevated viaduct that ran directly into the warehouses and factories. Today that viaduct is the High Line.

The warehouses and factories along the line included some of the great names in American industry. The National Biscuit Company, Nabisco, baked crackers and cookies here, including the first Oreo. Massive freight terminals were built so trucks and trains could deliver directly to upper floors. In the Meatpacking District, hundreds of slaughterhouses and packing plants processed meat for the city.

Inland, Chelsea itself has a gentler history. The name comes from an estate owned in the 1700s by Captain Thomas Clarke, who named it after the Royal Hospital Chelsea in London. His grandson, Clement Clarke Moore, developed the estate into a neighborhood of elegant rowhouses in the 1830s and 40s. In the late nineteenth century, West 23rd Street was the center of the American theater, and later an early center of the film industry.

After World War Two, shipping moved to container ports in New Jersey, trucks replaced trains, and industry left Manhattan. The piers rotted. The High Line was abandoned. The Meatpacking District became a mix of the remaining meat businesses and late night clubs. Then, starting in the 1990s, art galleries began moving to West Chelsea from SoHo, followed by restaurants, boutiques, and luxury condos. The High Line reopened as a park in 2009 and became one of the most influential urban design projects of the century, copied in cities around the world. And at the north end, Hudson Yards rose on a platform built over an active rail yard.

This is also, as it happens, one of the main centers of the tech industry in New York. Google has a cluster of buildings here, and much of the neighborhood's transformation has been driven by companies that wanted big, open, industrial floors for their offices. As you walk, you'll see how buildings designed for freight trains and meat hooks became homes for software engineers.
=== STOP The Meatpacking District @ 40.7397,-74.0068
You're on the cobblestone streets of the Meatpacking District, near Gansevoort Street and Washington Street.

Look up at the metal canopies sticking out over the sidewalks from many of the buildings. These were built to shelter meat as it was unloaded and hung on hooks. If you look closely, you might still spot the steel rails and hooks that carried carcasses into the buildings.

The district's name comes from its role as a center of meat processing. By 1900, there were reportedly around two hundred fifty slaughterhouses and packing plants in the area, serving the hotels, restaurants, and butcher shops of the city. Animals arrived by train and boat, and meat went out by truck and cart.

The area's oldest name is Gansevoort, after Fort Gansevoort, a small fort that stood near the river in the early nineteenth century. The fort was named after Peter Gansevoort, a Revolutionary War hero. His grandson was the novelist Herman Melville, who, after his literary career faded and Moby-Dick failed to find an audience, took a job as a customs inspector on the New York docks. For nearly twenty years, the man who wrote one of the greatest American novels inspected cargo on the waterfront, including at a pier on Gansevoort Street, the street named for his own grandfather.

Gansevoort Market, an earlier market building, was a major wholesale center. In the mid-twentieth century, the meat trade began to decline, as refrigerated trucks and national meatpacking companies made the old local system obsolete. By the 1980s, the neighborhood was gritty and partly abandoned at night, known for underground gay clubs, sex clubs, and late night street activity.

Then the neighborhood flipped. In 1999, the French bistro Pastis opened here, and it became a celebrity hangout. Shows like Sex and the City featured the neighborhood. Fashion designers and boutiques arrived, including Diane von Furstenberg and Stella McCartney. Hotels and restaurants followed. A handful of meat businesses still operate here, in a cooperative building, but they're a small presence compared to the luxury stores.

As you walk the streets, notice the contrast between the cobblestones and old brick warehouses and the sleek glass buildings now rising among them.
=== STOP The Whitney & the Start of the High Line @ 40.7396,-74.0089
You're at the southern entrance to the High Line, at Gansevoort Street, next to the Whitney Museum of American Art.

The museum, the large, asymmetrical building with steel cladding and outdoor terraces stepping down toward the river, was designed by Renzo Piano and opened here in 2015. The Whitney's story begins with Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney, a sculptor and heiress who collected the work of living American artists. In 1929, she offered her collection to the Metropolitan Museum of Art. The Met declined. So in 1931, she founded her own museum, in Greenwich Village. The Whitney later moved to Midtown and then to a famous building on Madison Avenue, before coming here. Its Biennial exhibition is one of the most important surveys of contemporary American art.

Now look at the elevated structure above you, the High Line.

As you heard in the overview, the railroad tracks were lifted off the street in the 1930s as part of the West Side Improvement Project, which removed over a hundred street-level crossings. The new elevated line, completed in 1934, ran from St. John's Terminal in Hudson Square up to the rail yards at 34th Street. It ran through the middle of blocks rather than over the avenues, and it passed directly into factories and warehouses, so trains could unload right inside buildings.

As trucking took over, rail traffic dwindled. The last train, reportedly carrying a load of frozen turkeys, ran in 1980. The southern section was demolished. The rest sat abandoned for nearly twenty years, and wild grasses, flowers, and small trees took root on the old tracks.

Property owners wanted it torn down. In 1999, two neighborhood residents, Joshua David and Robert Hammond, met at a community meeting and founded Friends of the High Line to save it. Initially, the idea seemed absurd. But photographs by Joel Sternfeld of the wild meadow growing on the tracks helped capture the public imagination. The city, under Mayor Michael Bloomberg, embraced the project. The first section opened as a park in 2009.

The design, by the landscape architecture firm James Corner Field Operations with the architects Diller Scofidio and Renfro, kept the old rails in places and used planting by the Dutch designer Piet Oudolf, who chose grasses and perennials inspired by the self-seeded landscape that grew there during the abandoned years.

The High Line became an instant hit, and a catalyst for development. Billions of dollars in new buildings went up alongside it. It also became a symbol of a broader debate about how public improvements can drive gentrification. Even its founders have acknowledged that the park benefited wealthy newcomers more than longtime residents of nearby public housing.
=== STOP Little Island & Pier 54: The Titanic's Destination @ 40.7420,-74.0103
Walk to the river, along the Hudson River Park, to the park built on what look like giant concrete tulips rising out of the water. This is Little Island, which opened in 2021. It was designed by the British designer Thomas Heatherwick and funded largely by the media mogul Barry Diller and his wife, the fashion designer Diane von Furstenberg. Its undulating surface is held up by about one hundred thirty two concrete pots on piles, and it contains lawns, gardens, and an amphitheater.

But look just south of it, at the remains of an old pier and a steel archway. That arch is the last remnant of Pier 54, and it carries one of the great stories of the steamship era.

In the early twentieth century, this stretch of the Hudson, from about 12th to 23rd Streets, was home to the Chelsea Piers, a set of grand ocean liner terminals designed by the architects Warren and Wetmore, who also designed Grand Central Terminal. They opened in 1910 and were used by the great transatlantic lines, including Cunard and White Star.

In April 1912, the White Star Line's new ship, the Titanic, was headed for these piers on its maiden voyage. It never arrived. After it struck an iceberg and sank, the Cunard ship Carpathia rescued about seven hundred survivors and brought them here to Pier 54 on the night of April eighteenth. Thousands of people crowded the waterfront in the rain, waiting for news of their loved ones.

Three years later, in May 1915, another famous ship left from Pier 54: the Cunard liner Lusitania, bound for Liverpool. Off the coast of Ireland, it was torpedoed by a German submarine and sank, killing nearly twelve hundred people, including over one hundred Americans. The sinking helped turn American public opinion against Germany and toward entry into World War One.

Look for the word Cunard still faintly visible on the steel arch. The great piers declined after ocean liners moved to larger terminals farther north in the 1930s, and air travel eventually ended the transatlantic liner era. A few blocks north, the Chelsea Piers complex has been converted into a giant sports and entertainment center.

Also nearby is Pier 57, a former marine terminal that has been redeveloped and now includes offices for Google, a food hall, and a rooftop park. It's another example of how the old maritime infrastructure of the west side has been transformed.
=== STOP Chelsea Market: The Birthplace of the Oreo @ 40.7424,-74.0060
You're at Chelsea Market, the block-long complex of brick buildings between Ninth and Tenth Avenues and 15th and 16th Streets. Walk inside and look at the exposed brick, iron beams, and old pipes. This was a factory, and a historic one.

In 1898, several large baking companies merged to form the National Biscuit Company, later known as Nabisco. Its headquarters and main bakery were here, in a complex of about eighteen buildings that eventually spread over several blocks. The company pioneered mass-market packaged foods. Its first major national brand, launched in 1898, was the Uneeda Biscuit, a soda cracker sold in an innovative package lined with waxed paper to keep it fresh, at a time when crackers were usually sold loose from barrels. Nabisco's advertising campaign for Uneeda was one of the first large-scale national ad campaigns in American history.

And in 1912, the bakers here created a new cookie: two chocolate wafers with a cream filling, called the Oreo. Nobody knows for sure where the name came from. The Oreo went on to become the best-selling cookie in the world. Animal Crackers and Fig Newtons were also produced by Nabisco in this era. A plaque on the Ninth Avenue side commemorates the Oreo's birth on this block, and the street outside has at times been nicknamed Oreo Way.

Nabisco moved its manufacturing to a modern plant in New Jersey in 1958, and the buildings were used by other businesses. In the 1990s, a developer, Irwin Cohen, converted the ground floor into a food hall and market, which opened in 1997 as Chelsea Market. He kept many industrial details, including a waterfall made from a pipe. The upper floors became offices for media companies and tech firms.

In 2018, Google bought Chelsea Market, for a reported price of about two point four billion dollars, one of the most expensive real estate deals for a single building in New York at the time. It's now part of Google's Chelsea campus.

Note that the High Line passes right through the building's western side. When the rail viaduct was built, Nabisco had loading docks connected directly to the tracks, so flour and sugar could come in and finished products could go out without ever touching the street.
=== STOP 111 Eighth Avenue: Google's Freight Terminal @ 40.7414,-74.0031
Across Ninth Avenue, filling the entire block from Eighth to Ninth Avenues and 15th to 16th Streets, is a massive Art Deco building: 111 Eighth Avenue. If you work at Google, you may know this building very well.

It was completed in 1932 as the Port Authority Commerce Building, also called the Union Inland Terminal Number One. The Port Authority of New York, which built bridges and tunnels, wanted a freight distribution hub where trucks coming through the new Holland Tunnel could drop off goods. The building was designed so trucks could drive right inside and ride oversized elevators up to the upper floors, delivering goods directly to warehouses on each floor. Its floors are enormous and built to carry very heavy loads.

At about three million square feet, it was one of the largest buildings in the city by floor area. In fact, at the time it was built, it was among the largest buildings in the world by floor space. It covers an entire city block.

Later, the building became a hub for a different kind of freight: data. In the internet age, its central location, huge floors, strong structure, and electrical capacity made it a major hub for telecommunications and data centers, like 60 Hudson Street downtown.

In 2010, Google bought the building for about one point nine billion dollars, at the time one of the largest single-building sales in the country. Google had been leasing space here since 2006, and the building became the core of its New York operations. It's now joined by other Google buildings in the neighborhood: Chelsea Market, Pier 57, and St. John's Terminal in Hudson Square.

There's something poetic about this. The building was designed to make the movement of physical goods more efficient, and it now houses engineers building the software that moves information around the world. And it's not unique. Up and down the west side, buildings designed for freight have found new life as offices for tech companies, because the same things that made them good for freight, big floors, high ceilings, strong structures, and lots of power, make them good for modern offices too.
=== STOP The Hotel Chelsea @ 40.7444,-73.9968
On West 23rd Street, between Seventh and Eighth Avenues, look for the red brick building with ornate iron balconies and a vertical sign reading Hotel Chelsea.

It was built in 1884 as one of the city's first cooperative apartment buildings, and for a time it was among the tallest buildings in the city. In the 1880s, West 23rd Street was the heart of New York's theater district, with theaters and music halls lining the street. Within a couple of decades, the cooperative had failed, and the building became a hotel.

Over the twentieth century, the Chelsea became legendary as a residence for artists, writers, and musicians, many of them on long-term stays. Its manager for many years, Stanley Bard, was known for letting struggling artists pay their rent late, or even with artwork, which covered the walls of the lobby.

The list of residents is remarkable. Mark Twain and O. Henry are said to have stayed here. The poet Dylan Thomas was living here when he fell ill in 1953. Arthur C. Clarke worked on 2001: A Space Odyssey while staying here. Andy Warhol filmed parts of his 1966 film Chelsea Girls here. Leonard Cohen wrote a song about an encounter at the hotel with Janis Joplin. Patti Smith and Robert Mapplethorpe lived here in the late 1960s, as she describes in her memoir Just Kids. Bob Dylan, Jimi Hendrix, Madonna, and many others passed through.

The hotel's history also has darker chapters. In October 1978, Nancy Spungen, the girlfriend of the Sex Pistols bassist Sid Vicious, was found stabbed to death in their room. Vicious was charged with her murder but died of a heroin overdose before trial.

The hotel closed to new guests in 2011 for a long and contentious renovation, during which some long-term residents remained. It reopened in 2022 as a luxury hotel.

Also on this street, look for the Chelsea's neighbor, the old YMCA building, and the site of the Grand Opera House, which once stood on Eighth Avenue and was owned by the notorious financier Jim Fisk, who ran the Erie Railroad from offices there. In 1872, Fisk was shot dead by a rival in love and business at a hotel on lower Broadway. Chelsea has always been full of drama.
=== STOP Clement Clarke Moore's Chelsea @ 40.7458,-74.0035
You're now in the Chelsea Historic District, near Ninth Avenue and West 21st Street. Look at the full block of green lawn and Gothic buildings, the campus of the General Theological Seminary, the oldest seminary of the Episcopal Church.

The land was donated to the seminary in 1827 by Clement Clarke Moore, who owned the Chelsea estate. Moore was a scholar of Hebrew and Greek who taught at the seminary. He's best known, though, for something else: he's generally credited with writing, in 1822, the poem A Visit from St. Nicholas, which begins, Twas the night before Christmas. The poem helped create the modern American image of Santa Claus, with his sleigh, eight reindeer, and trip down the chimney. Some scholars have argued that another writer, Henry Livingston Junior, actually wrote it, but Moore's authorship is the traditional view.

Moore had good reason to develop his land. When the city's street grid was imposed in 1811, Ninth Avenue was cut through the middle of his estate. He was furious, but he made the best of it, dividing the land into lots and selling them with strict covenants about what could be built: residential houses only, with setbacks from the street, and no stables or factories. That's why this area has such elegant, consistent rows of townhouses.

Walk along West 20th Street, between Ninth and Tenth Avenues, to see Cushman Row, a row of Greek Revival houses built in the 1840s by a merchant named Don Alonzo Cushman, a friend of Moore's. Look for the small, square attic windows with iron wreaths around them, and the iron pineapples, a symbol of hospitality, on the newel posts of some stoops.

On Tenth Avenue, look also for the Empire Diner, an Art Deco diner car, and to the north, the massive London Terrace complex, built in 1930 and once one of the largest apartment buildings in the world, with its own swimming pool and doormen dressed as London bobbies.

It's a remarkable contrast: just a block or two from the freight terminals, the slaughterhouses, and the piers, Moore's carefully planned neighborhood of churches and townhouses has survived nearly two centuries.
=== STOP West Chelsea Galleries & the Starrett-Lehigh Building @ 40.7516,-74.0059
You're in West Chelsea, near Eleventh Avenue and West 26th Street. The blocks around you, roughly between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues from 18th to 28th Streets, contain one of the densest concentrations of art galleries in the world, hundreds of them, many in former garages and warehouses.

Galleries began moving here from SoHo in the late 1980s and 1990s, as SoHo's rents rose. The Dia Art Foundation opened an exhibition space on West 22nd Street in 1987, and big dealers followed. Garages, with their high ceilings, large open spaces, and freight doors, were perfect for showing large contemporary works. Today, galleries like Gagosian, David Zwirner, and Pace operate huge spaces here, and Thursday evenings, when many shows open, the sidewalks fill with art lovers.

Now look at the massive building that fills the block between 26th and 27th Streets, along Eleventh Avenue, with continuous horizontal bands of windows wrapping around its rounded corners. This is the Starrett-Lehigh Building, completed in 1931, and it's one of the great industrial buildings of the twentieth century.

It was built as a joint venture between the Starrett construction family and the Lehigh Valley Railroad. The railroad had a freight yard here, and the building was designed so freight trains could enter the ground floor on tracks. Even more remarkable, trucks could drive into enormous elevators and be lifted directly to any of the upper floors, then drive right up to the tenants' doors. It was essentially a vertical freight yard.

Its architecture is equally striking. Instead of a traditional facade with vertical columns, the building has cantilevered concrete floors with ribbons of steel windows running continuously around it. It was celebrated by the Museum of Modern Art in an early exhibition on modern architecture as an example of the new International Style.

Like 111 Eighth Avenue, it later found new life with creative companies. Martha Stewart's media company had offices here, as have photographers, fashion companies, and tech startups. The interior's huge windows offer some of the best Hudson River views in the city.
=== STOP Hudson Yards & the Vessel @ 40.7538,-74.0021
End your walk at Hudson Yards, the cluster of glass towers rising at the northern end of the High Line, around West 33rd Street.

The towers you see are built on a platform over an active rail yard. Beneath your feet, the Long Island Rail Road stores commuter trains between rush hours. For decades, the yard was an enormous hole on the far west side of Manhattan. Plans to cover it went back to the 1950s. In the 2000s, the city tried to build a football stadium here for the New York Jets, as part of a bid for the 2012 Olympics. That plan collapsed. Instead, the city rezoned the area, and the developers Related Companies and Oxford Properties built Hudson Yards, which opened its first phase in 2019.

To build it, engineers drilled hundreds of caissons down to bedrock between the rail tracks and built a giant platform over the yard while trains continued to run. It is often described as the largest private real estate development in American history. The city also extended the 7 subway line to a new station here, which opened in 2015.

The towers house offices for companies including financial firms, media companies, and tech companies, as well as a shopping mall, apartments, and a hotel. Look up at 30 Hudson Yards, the tallest tower, which has an outdoor observation deck called Edge, jutting out from the building in a triangular point at about eleven hundred feet. It's one of the highest outdoor observation decks in the Western Hemisphere.

In the central plaza, you'll see the Vessel, a honeycomb-like structure of interlocking staircases, designed by Thomas Heatherwick, the designer of Little Island. It has about one hundred fifty four flights of stairs and was meant to be climbed. Access has been restricted at times for safety reasons, with added barriers on the upper levels.

Next to it, the building with the giant movable outer shell, on rails, is The Shed, a cultural center that can expand its size by rolling its shell outward over the adjacent plaza.

Hudson Yards has been controversial. Critics have called it a playground for the rich, and debated the tax incentives the city provided to support it. Admirers point to the engineering achievement and the economic activity it brought to a former rail yard. Either way, it represents the latest chapter of the west side's transformation, from freight trains and piers to the most expensive real estate in America, all in about a century.
