=== OVERVIEW
Before the Brooklyn Bridge, Brooklyn was a separate city, and for much of the nineteenth century, it was the third largest city in the United States, after New York and Philadelphia. The only way to get to Manhattan was by boat. The ferry from the foot of Fulton Street was the main link, and the area around the ferry landing became the commercial heart of Brooklyn.

The waterfront north and south of the ferry developed into one of the busiest port districts in the world. Massive warehouses, called stores, lined the shore, holding coffee, sugar, tobacco, grain, and other goods unloaded from ships. Factories sprang up in the blocks behind them, making paper boxes, machinery, soap, and other products. The neighborhood that we now call DUMBO, for Down Under the Manhattan Bridge Overpass, was one of the great industrial districts of the country. Some of its businesses became household names. Others invented things you use every day without knowing where they came from.

Then came the bridges. The Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883, followed by the Williamsburg Bridge in 1903 and the Manhattan Bridge in 1909. In 1898, Brooklyn voted, narrowly, to merge with New York, becoming one of its five boroughs. Many Brooklynites still call it the Great Mistake of 1898.

After World War Two, the waterfront declined. Container shipping moved the port to New Jersey, the warehouses emptied, and factories moved out. By the 1970s, the area under the Manhattan Bridge was a desolate industrial zone, with a few artists living illegally in the empty lofts. In 1978, residents coined the name DUMBO, reportedly on purpose to make it sound unappealing and keep developers away. It didn't work. A developer named David Walentas began buying buildings in the early 1980s, and over the following decades, he and others transformed DUMBO into one of the most expensive neighborhoods in New York, filled with tech companies, designers, and luxury apartments. Meanwhile, the piers along the water were transformed into Brooklyn Bridge Park.

This tour begins on the Brooklyn Bridge and ends in the park. If you're starting in Manhattan, walk across the bridge, and the first stop will play as you approach the first tower.
=== STOP The Brooklyn Bridge @ 40.7058,-73.9963
You're on the Brooklyn Bridge. Look up at the massive granite towers with their Gothic arches, and the web of steel cables radiating from them.

The bridge was designed by John Augustus Roebling, a German immigrant engineer who had pioneered the manufacture of wire rope and built several suspension bridges. In 1869, while surveying the site, Roebling's foot was crushed by a ferry against the pilings at the Fulton Ferry landing. His toes were amputated, and he died of tetanus a few weeks later. He was sixty-three.

His son, Washington Roebling, a thirty-two-year-old Civil War veteran and engineer, took over. To build the towers' foundations, workers labored inside caissons, huge upside-down wooden boxes sunk to the river bottom, filled with compressed air to keep water out. As the caissons sank deeper, workers began suffering from a mysterious illness: crippling joint pain, paralysis, and sometimes death. They called it caisson disease. Today we know it as decompression sickness, or the bends, caused by nitrogen bubbles forming in the blood when people come out of high pressure too quickly. Several workers died. Washington Roebling himself was struck by it in 1872 and left partially paralyzed and in pain for the rest of his life.

Unable to visit the site, Roebling directed construction from his bedroom window in Brooklyn Heights, watching through a telescope. His wife, Emily Warren Roebling, became his link to the project. She learned higher mathematics, the strength of materials, and cable construction, and relayed his instructions to the engineers. Over time, she effectively became a manager of the project. On opening day, May twenty-fourth, 1883, she was given the honor of the first ride across the bridge, carrying a rooster as a symbol of victory. A plaque on the bridge honors her, along with her husband and father-in-law.

The bridge was an engineering marvel. Its main span, about sixteen hundred feet, was the longest in the world. It was the first suspension bridge to use steel wire for its cables. There was a scandal during construction: the contractor supplying the wire, J. Lloyd Haigh, was caught substituting defective wire. Some of it had already been woven into the cables. Roebling compensated by adding extra wires, and the bridge was built to be several times stronger than necessary, which is part of why it's still standing.

A week after it opened, on May thirtieth, a rumor that the bridge was collapsing caused a stampede on the crowded walkway, and twelve people were crushed to death. To prove the bridge was safe, in 1884, the showman P. T. Barnum led a parade of twenty-one elephants across it.
=== STOP Fulton Ferry Landing @ 40.7034,-73.9945
You're at Fulton Ferry Landing, at the foot of Old Fulton Street, on the water just north of the Brooklyn Bridge. This was the gateway to Brooklyn for centuries.

Ferries crossed here as early as the 1600s, rowed or sailed across the treacherous East River. In 1814, Robert Fulton, the steamboat pioneer buried at Trinity Church, launched steam ferry service here. The street leading up the hill was renamed Fulton Street in his honor, and it became Brooklyn's main street.

This landing was also the site of one of the most important moments of the American Revolution. In August 1776, the British defeated George Washington's army at the Battle of Brooklyn, also called the Battle of Long Island, the largest battle of the war. Washington's army was trapped in Brooklyn Heights, with the British army in front and the East River behind. On the night of August twenty-ninth, Washington ordered a secret retreat. Using every boat they could find, soldiers from a Massachusetts regiment of fishermen and sailors from Marblehead, led by Colonel John Glover, ferried about nine thousand troops, with their supplies and artillery, across the river to Manhattan, in total silence, through the night. As dawn broke, a thick fog rolled in, hiding the last boats from the British. By the time the fog lifted, the entire army was gone. If the British had captured Washington's army that night, the Revolution might well have ended.

Walt Whitman, the great poet, was a Brooklyn man, and he rode this ferry constantly. In his poem Crossing Brooklyn Ferry, published in 1856, he imagined future generations making the same crossing, looking at the same river and sky, and he addressed them directly: Just as you feel when you look on the river and sky, so I felt. Whitman also worked as editor of the Brooklyn Daily Eagle, the newspaper whose former warehouse, the Eagle Warehouse, still stands just up Old Fulton Street, now converted to apartments.

Look around the landing. The barge moored nearby is Bargemusic, a floating concert hall that has hosted chamber music concerts since 1977. Nearby is the River Café, a celebrated restaurant opened in 1977, when this area was still desolate. And up the street are two of the most famous pizzerias in the city, which have long attracted lines of tourists.
=== STOP Empire Stores & Jane's Carousel @ 40.7036,-73.9918
Walk north along the water into Brooklyn Bridge Park, to the long row of massive brick warehouses facing the river. These are the Empire Stores, built in two phases between 1869 and 1885.

In the nineteenth century, a warehouse was called a store, because it stored goods. Ships from around the world unloaded their cargoes here: coffee from Brazil, sugar from the Caribbean, jute, hides, and other goods. The thick brick walls and small windows kept goods safe and the temperature stable. Workers used iron shutters to protect the cargo, and you can still see some of them on the windows.

The waterfront here was dominated by coffee and sugar. One of the most important companies was Arbuckle Brothers, a coffee company founded in Pittsburgh. Arbuckle had a brilliant innovation: it sold coffee already roasted, sealed in one-pound paper packages, at a time when most people bought green coffee beans and roasted them at home. The coffee was coated with a glaze of sugar and egg to keep it fresh. Their brand, Ariosa, became so popular in the American West in the late nineteenth century that it was called the coffee that won the West. Arbuckle moved its operations to Brooklyn, along this waterfront, and also went into sugar refining, challenging the powerful sugar trust in a famous price war.

The Empire Stores sat empty for decades after the shipping industry declined. They were redeveloped in the 2010s into offices, shops, and restaurants, with a rooftop terrace.

Next door, the roofless brick ruin with arched openings is the Tobacco Warehouse, built in the 1870s and used to store tobacco during inspection. The roof was removed in the 1990s, and part of it is now home to St. Ann's Warehouse, a theater.

Now look at the glass box by the water. Inside is Jane's Carousel, a carousel built in 1922 by the Philadelphia Toboggan Company for an amusement park in Youngstown, Ohio. In 1984, Jane Walentas, an artist and the wife of the developer David Walentas, bought it at auction and spent more than two decades restoring it by hand, stripping away layers of paint to reveal the original colors of its forty-eight horses. In 2011, it reopened here, inside a transparent pavilion designed by the French architect Jean Nouvel. The carousel survived Hurricane Sandy in 2012, when the pavilion flooded but the carousel was undamaged, and photos of it lit up in the storm went viral.
=== STOP Robert Gair & the Invention of the Cardboard Box @ 40.7033,-73.9897
Walk up to Washington Street, at Water Street, and turn to face the Manhattan Bridge. This is one of the most photographed views in New York: the narrow street framed by brick warehouses, with the Manhattan Bridge's tower filling the gap at the end, and the Empire State Building visible through the bridge's arch.

The buildings around you were once part of Gairville, the factory empire of Robert Gair, a Scottish immigrant who invented a product you've probably used today: the folding cardboard box.

Gair ran a business printing and making paper bags. In 1879, one of his workers made a mistake: a metal printing rule, used to crease paper, shifted, and it cut through a stack of paper bags instead of creasing them. Gair realized that he could use the same principle to cut and crease cardboard in one operation, producing flat, pre-cut boxes that could be folded into shape. Before this, boxes had to be cut and assembled by hand, slowly and expensively. Gair's machines could produce thousands quickly and cheaply.

The innovation transformed packaging. Gair's company became one of the largest producers of boxes in the world. One of his most important customers was the National Biscuit Company, Nabisco, which used Gair's boxes for its Uneeda Biscuits, the first crackers sold in sealed packages, which you may have heard about on the Chelsea tour. The cardboard box was essential to the rise of national brands, because it allowed companies to package, label, and ship products directly to consumers.

Gair bought and built more and more buildings in this area. They were among the first large reinforced concrete factory buildings in the city. Look for the building at 1 Main Street with a clock tower on top. That was one of Gair's buildings. Its clock tower penthouse, with enormous clock faces as windows, was converted into one of the most expensive apartments in Brooklyn.

The Gair company moved out in the 1920s, and the buildings were used by other manufacturers. Today, many of them house tech companies, design studios, and media firms. DUMBO is part of what the city has promoted as the Brooklyn Tech Triangle, along with the Navy Yard and Downtown Brooklyn. It's fitting that a neighborhood built on a packaging innovation is now full of startups trying to invent the next one.
=== STOP Squibb & the Watchtower @ 40.7004,-73.9930
Walk south, under the Brooklyn Bridge, to the corner of Columbia Heights and Old Fulton Street, at the foot of Brooklyn Heights. Look up at the large buildings climbing the hillside above you.

For most of the twentieth century, these buildings were home to two very different organizations. The first was E. R. Squibb and Sons, the pharmaceutical company. Edward Robinson Squibb was a Navy doctor who was horrified by the poor quality of the medicines given to sailors. In 1858, he set up a laboratory here in Brooklyn to manufacture pure, standardized drugs, especially ether, an anesthetic, and chloroform. During the Civil War, his company supplied medicines to the Union army. Squibb was a crusader for drug quality and an early advocate of federal regulation of food and drugs. His company became one of the leading pharmaceutical companies in the country, and it's an ancestor of today's Bristol Myers Squibb.

The second organization was the Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society, the headquarters of the Jehovah's Witnesses. The Witnesses moved their headquarters to Brooklyn in 1909, and over the decades, they bought many buildings in Brooklyn Heights and DUMBO, including the old Squibb buildings, for their offices, residences, and printing plants. They printed hundreds of millions of copies of their magazines, The Watchtower and Awake!, here, distributing them around the world. For decades, a giant red sign reading Watchtower, with the time and temperature, sat on top of one of the buildings, and it became a familiar sight for anyone crossing the Brooklyn Bridge.

In the 2010s, the Witnesses moved their headquarters to Warwick, in upstate New York, and sold their Brooklyn properties to developers for large sums. The Watchtower sign was taken down in 2017. The buildings have been converted into offices and apartments.

Up the hill, along Columbia Heights, is the beginning of Brooklyn Heights, America's first designated historic district, and the Brooklyn Heights Promenade, which you can explore on the Brooklyn Heights tour. It was from a house on Columbia Heights, near here, that Washington Roebling watched the Brooklyn Bridge being built through his telescope.
=== STOP Brooklyn Bridge Park & the Piers @ 40.7019,-73.9966
End your walk on Pier 1 in Brooklyn Bridge Park, just south of the Brooklyn Bridge, on the water.

Look out at the view: the towers of lower Manhattan rising across the river, the Statue of Liberty in the harbor to the south, and the Brooklyn Bridge arching overhead. It's one of the best views in New York.

This waterfront was once among the busiest port areas in the world. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the New York Dock Company and other companies operated a continuous line of piers, warehouses, and rail yards along the Brooklyn shore, from here south to Red Hook. Brooklyn's docks handled a huge share of the cargo coming into the Port of New York. Thousands of longshoremen, many of them Irish and Italian immigrants, loaded and unloaded ships by hand. The work was brutal, and the docks were controlled by corrupt unions and organized crime, as dramatized in the 1954 film On the Waterfront.

Then came the shipping container. In 1956, a trucking entrepreneur named Malcolm McLean shipped containers from Port Newark, in New Jersey, on a converted tanker, launching the container revolution. Containers could be lifted by crane directly from ship to truck or train, eliminating most of the labor of loading and unloading. But they needed huge open spaces for stacking and handling, which Brooklyn's narrow piers didn't have. The shipping industry moved to the vast container terminals in New Jersey. By the 1980s, Brooklyn's piers were nearly abandoned.

In the 2000s, after years of community advocacy, the piers were transformed into Brooklyn Bridge Park, designed by the landscape architecture firm Michael Van Valkenburgh Associates. The park stretches about a mile and a half along the waterfront, from here to Atlantic Avenue. Each pier has a different use: lawns, playgrounds, sports fields, a salt marsh, and even a beach for kayaks. The park was designed to be financially self-sustaining: luxury apartment buildings and a hotel were built within its boundaries, and their fees pay for the park's maintenance, a model that has been both praised and criticized.

The park also uses salvaged materials. Look for benches made of wood from the old pier sheds, and parts of the old industrial waterfront incorporated into the design. Stand at the end of the pier and imagine this view a century ago, filled with ships, ferries, and barges.
